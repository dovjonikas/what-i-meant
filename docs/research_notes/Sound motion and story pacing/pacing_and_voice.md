# Story structure, pacing and second-person voice for "Text me when you get home"

Scope: evidence and craft on emotional arcs, pacing, second-person present narration, twists and clarity, then a beat-referenced critique of the draft script at `site/stories/text-me/js/script.js` (line numbers below refer to that file as read on 2026-10-30).

Labels used in every bullet: **[Research]** = peer-reviewed or data study. **[Practitioner]** = a named maker's own statement or a design source. **[Forum]** = informal community heuristic. **[Inference]** = this researcher's reasoning from the cited material. **[Craft opinion]** = judgement with no study behind it. **[Script]** = a fact read or counted from script.js.

## 1. Emotional arcs, peak-end, comic relief, the rule of three, setup and payoff spacing

### Takeaway
Six arc shapes dominate fiction, and the most downloaded ones contain more than one turn (fall-rise-fall, rise-fall, two "man in a hole" dips in sequence); this script's life line is already a two-dip shape. Memory of an experience is driven by its peak and its end, not its length, so the last minute and the single strongest moment matter far more than total runtime. Humor inside a death story raised enjoyment without lowering appreciation in the one direct experiment found. No study gives an ideal setup-to-payoff spacing; the evidence on time shifts suggests a setup planted before a time jump needs a re-cue before its payoff.

### Cited Findings
- [Research] Reagan, Mitchell, Kiley, Danforth and Dodds classified the emotional arcs of a filtered Project Gutenberg fiction set (1,737 books in the arXiv preprint; the published EPJ Data Science version is indexed as 1,327) and found six core arcs, confirmed with SVD, hierarchical clustering and self-organizing maps: Rags to riches (rise), Tragedy or Riches to rags (fall), Man in a hole (fall-rise), Icarus (rise-fall), Cinderella (rise-fall-rise), Oedipus (fall-rise-fall). [arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.07772v1.pdf); [EPJ Data Science](https://epjds.epj.org/articles/epjdata/abs/2016/01/13688_2016_Article_93/13688_2016_Article_93.html); [DOAJ record, 1,327 count](https://doaj.org/article/ed9cacc0f7284941a5907ab0fe5cbb0a)
- [Research] Sentiment was scored with the labMT word list over a sliding window. By downloads, "the first four modes, which contain the greatest total number of books, are not the most popular"; the most successful arcs were "Icarus", "Oedipus", "two sequential 'Man in a hole' arcs" and "Cinderella with a tragic ending". Hierarchical clustering put Man in a hole stories at 23 percent of the corpus; the SOM grouping gave Rags to riches 19 percent, Icarus 12 percent, Tragedy 6 percent. [arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.07772v1.pdf)
- [Research] Fredrickson and Kahneman (1993, JPSP 65(1), 45 to 55) showed with pleasant and aversive film clips of varying length that global retrospective ratings largely ignore duration ("duration neglect") and track the most intense moment and the final moment. [Paper PDF](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/fredr-kahneman-jpsp1993.pdf); [Duration neglect overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duration_neglect)
- [Research] Duration neglect is not fixed: it depends partly on whether duration information is made available and meaningful to the evaluator. [Duration neglect overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duration_neglect)
- [Research] Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier (1993, Psychological Science), "When more pain is preferred to less: adding a better end": adding a milder ending to an aversive episode made people prefer the longer episode. [Paper PDF](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Cognitive%20Psychology/When%20more%20pain%20is%20preferred%20to%20less.pdf)
- [Research] Diener, Wirtz and Oishi (2001, Psychological Science 12(2), 124 to 128), "the James Dean effect": a wonderful life that ends abruptly is rated more desirable than the same life with extra mildly pleasant years; endings weigh heavily and the length of each phase is largely ignored. [Record](https://scilit.com/publications/a9d0541822a5d3517ea84a7c627ec097)
- [Research] Rozin and Stellar (2009, Judgment and Decision Making 4(4), 273 to 279): events after a character's death shift readers' judgments of how good and happy that whole life was, in the direction of the posthumous event. [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/judgdm/v4y2009i4p273-279_2.html)
- [Research] Fitzgerald, Francemone and Grizzard (2022, Frontiers in Communication), N = 241 adults who had lost a loved one, a eulogy story in a humor-focused or poignant-focused version: narrative engagement equal (5.20 vs 5.17), appreciation equal and high (5.80 humor vs 5.95 poignant, not significant), enjoyment much higher with humor (4.35 vs 2.82), death acceptance higher with humor (4.16 vs 3.72), connectedness equal (5.30 vs 5.29). The authors conclude that death is inherently poignant and "the addition of humorous elements in bereavement narratives may be especially effective". They also note stories about loss "tend to be appreciated even when specifically intended to be humorous". [Paper PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2022.973239/pdf)
- [Research] The same paper reviews the Distancing-Embracing model (Menninghaus et al., 2017): fictional framing keeps negative emotion at a distance, and the interplay of positive and negative emotion is one of the "embracing" factors that lets sad content be enjoyed. [Paper PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2022.973239/pdf)
- [Research] Zwaan (1996, JEP: LMC 22, 1196 to 1207): sentences after a narrative time shift take longer to read, and time shifts deactivate previously active information; a 2000 follow-up qualified this (deactivation only when earlier events are not assumed to still be ongoing). Speer and Zacks (2005) found "an hour later" is read as an event boundary while "a moment later" is not. [Secondary description, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03193388); [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2667942)
- [Practitioner] Vera Tobin (Elements of Surprise, Harvard UP 2018) locates the pleasure of a twist mainly in its setup rather than the shock; readers enjoy returning to the clues they missed. [Mental Floss summary](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/544879/why-our-brains-love-plot-twists)
- [Practitioner] Florence runs about 30 minutes in 20 brief chapters grouped into six acts, played linearly. [Florence, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_(video_game))

### Inferences
- [Inference] The script's life line is fall-rise-fall-rise with a lift at the end: childhood warmth, the Noon losses (Grandpa, the dog, the friend), the Dawn rise (partner, kid, broken by Grandma), the Afternoon trough (Dad, Mom), the Late and porch rise. That is close to Reagan's "two sequential Man in a hole" shape, one of the most downloaded, with a separate suspense line (the porch clock) laid over it. The shape is sound; the work is in the trough placement (see critique: Dad and Mom die about 15 beats apart).
- [Inference] Duration neglect and the James Dean effect point the same way: runtime matters little to how the piece is remembered, the peak and the last minute matter a lot, and mildly pleasant material placed after the peak can lower the overall judgment. For this script that means: protect the final wait and the final "home" tap; keep the coda after the last tap to two or three beats; cutting sag in the middle costs little.
- [Inference] Rozin and Stellar's posthumous-event result is close to what the twist does: a reveal after Mom's death (she was awake that night) re-scores her whole life. Evidence supports placing the Mom reveal after her death, as the draft does.
- [Inference] The Fitzgerald result supports keeping comic beats next to deaths (the draft does this after Grandma with the pregnancy texts, lines 326 to 334) and suggests the one stretch with no relief at all (Dad at line 440 to Mom at line 477) could take one light beat without losing weight.
- [Inference] Zwaan's deactivation finding implies that every life-lines jump is a point where earlier material drops out of working memory. A setup planted before several jumps needs a re-cue (a mid-point reminder) before its payoff, or the payoff reads as new information. The draft's strongest chains already follow plant, remind, pay off (apple curl 103, 129, 154 to 157; "Come visit more" 108, 308, 462, 503).
- [Inference] Rough conversion for this script: about 224 beats on one path over a 20 to 25 minute play gives about 9 to 11 beats per minute, so a span of 100 beats is roughly 10 minutes. Spans in the draft: cold open (beat 3) to its re-cue at the city night (about beat 103) to the reveal (about beat 218); "You won't remember any of this" (about beat 15) to "Your kid won't remember any of this" (about beat 150); the teen choice (about beat 71) to its only payoff (about beat 218, roughly 15 minutes, with no re-cue).
- [Craft opinion] Rule of three: plant, vary, turn. The draft's best motifs already do it ("I've got you" twice at 119, turned into "I've got it" at 422; "ok." with the full stop at 359, 361, then Mom's "ok." at 466 and 468). Two patterns stop at two: "absolutely not" (96, 97) and "Neither of you jumps" (396, 499, repeated word for word).

### Gaps
- No study found that measures an ideal setup-to-payoff distance in minutes for short fiction or games.
- No study found on the spacing (how often, how soon after a loss) of comic relief; Fitzgerald et al. compare whole-story versions, not placement.
- A secondary summary reported r = .78 between peak-and-end average and global ratings of the pleasant film clips in Fredrickson and Kahneman (1993); not verified in the original, so not used above.
- No empirical work found on the "rule of three" in narrative; it stays a craft heuristic here.
- Reagan et al. measure downloads of public-domain books, not player experience of 30-minute games; transfer to this format is an inference.

## 2. Pacing in interactive narrative: interaction rhythm, reading tolerance on phones, dropout, chapter length, time skips

### Takeaway
There is no peer-reviewed number for "beats per minute" or for how long phone players will read between interactions; the only figures are community heuristics (a choice every 100 to 400 words). Measured against those, this script is light on words (about 66 words per interaction) but heavy on taps (about 165 caption taps), and its first meaningful choice comes roughly a third of the way in. Completion data for games show most players do not finish, shorter games finish more often, and the correlation with length is weak.

### Cited Findings
- [Forum] Choice of Games forum heuristics: "a choice after every 100-400 words"; "200ish words, and avoiding more than 400"; "300 words max in between choices". A moderator-style reply says there is no rule set in stone. No official Choice of Games guideline was found. [Forum thread 1](https://forum.choiceofgames.com/t/how-long-is-too-long-to-go-without-a-choice/58610); [Forum thread 2](https://forum.choiceofgames.com/t/how-often-should-a-choice-be-presented-and-how-often-should-i-release-a-demo/26444)
- [Forum] An unofficial IF craft corpus lists 300 to 800 words per passage for Choice of Games style and 300 to 600 words between choices for standard pacing. [IF craft corpus](https://pvliesdonk.github.io/if-craft-corpus/corpus-docs/scope-and-planning/scope_and_length)
- [Research] Steam achievement study: mean completion about 14 percent across games with a completion achievement (partial sample of 825 entries). [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-66715-7_65)
- [Research] An analysis of 100 games reported by Galaxus/Digitec (the search summary attributes it to Larian; attribution not verified): average completion 38.8 percent, weak negative correlation between game length and completion (r = -0.24); Mouthwashing (about 2.5 hours) 79 percent. [Galaxus article](https://www.galaxus.at/en/page/hardly-anyone-finishes-games-so-why-are-they-so-long-37002)
- [Research] Short linear games on Steam: Stray completion achievement about 40.8 percent, Dead Space remake final chapter about 40.6 percent. [Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/?p=824046)
- [Practitioner] Florence: about 30 minutes, 20 chapters in six acts, so roughly 1.5 minutes per chapter; reviewers' headlines called it "a little brief" and "brief and breathtaking". [Florence, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_(video_game))
- [Practitioner] In Florence, puzzle difficulty rises when Florence struggles and eases when things go well (an analysis, not Wong's words). [fzine](https://www.fzine.com/culture/ken-wong-mountains-interview-florence-creative-director)

### Inferences
- [Inference] Runtime estimate: 165 caption taps at 3.5 to 5 seconds each (read plus tap) is about 10 to 14 minutes; 71 chat items at 1.5 to 2.5 seconds is about 2 to 3 minutes; fixed waits and holds about 1.7 minutes; life lines about 1.5 minutes plus pauses; 7 cards and 40 scene changes about 1 to 1.5 minutes. Total about 16 to 21 minutes for a reader who does not linger. The stated 25 to 30 minutes is plausible only with slow readers or long lingering; worth timing with real players before cutting anything for length.
- [Inference] Against the 100 to 400 word heuristic, words are not the problem (about 66 caption and chat words per interaction overall, about 210 words before the first hold). The pacing cost is tap count and the long wait for the first meaningful choice: about a third of the game passes before the player learns that choices exist and come back.
- [Inference] Porch cadence is even (every 48 to 59 beats) and the minute counter converges on the stated 40-minute drive exactly at the last return (21:52). This is the script's best pacing device; keep the spacing when cutting.
- [Inference] Chapter load is uneven against life length: Noon spends 46 beats on 8 years and holds three losses; Afternoon spends 49 beats on 32 years and holds two deaths in its last 15 beats. Compressing midlife fits the theme (time speeds up), but it crowds the two biggest deaths together.
- [Inference] Dropout risk is likely highest in the opening (no interaction for about 29 taps, no choice for about 71 beats). The script's `save: true` beats could double as checkpoints: logging the furthest save reached per session would show where players actually stop, which no public dataset can tell.
- [Craft opinion] Typing indicators and status changes (Delivered, Read, typing) are the strongest suspense tools this format has (Seeley's point). The draft uses them well at 512 to 520 and 440 to 445; it should use them once more as a clue (see critique, line 227).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed data found on beats per minute in visual novels or narrative games.
- No data found on reading tolerance per screen or per interaction on phones specifically.
- No public dropout or completion data found for free, short web story games; the Steam figures cover paid PC games of much greater length.

## 3. Second-person present narration: immersion, identification, risks, and how comparable games handle voice

### Takeaway
Lab evidence favours "you" for putting readers inside the actor's viewpoint, especially in present tense, but the evidence is thin and mostly from single sentences or short passages; a literary-story study found first person beat third person for immersion and did not test second person. Practitioners split: Kurvitz finds second person gimmicky in prose but natural in games, Emily Short found second-person present could carry a strongly characterised player character. The main risk is complicity: a strong "you" makes imposed actions feel like the player's own, which helps guilt beats and hurts when the player disagrees.

### Cited Findings
- [Research] Brunyé, Ditman, Mahoney, Augustyn and Taylor (2009, Psychological Science 20(1), 27 to 32): readers read action sentences in first, second or third person and matched pictures shown from an internal or external viewpoint; "you" sentences led readers to adopt the actor's (internal) perspective, and second-person statements produced a stronger sense of "being there". In a second experiment, added narrative context made "I" sentences produce a third-person (observer) perspective. [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/02/090218135124.htm); [APS](https://psychologicalscience.org/?p=32390)
- [Research] A 2016 Diegesis review of this line of work (seen via a search summary; the page now redirects): single first- or second-person sentences mostly prompted an internal perspective, second person more consistently; longer second-person passages kept the internal perspective while longer first-person passages tended toward external; consistent embodiment appeared only in second-person present-tense narrative. [Diegesis article](https://www.diegesis.uni-wuppertal.de/index.php/diegesis/article/download/214/305)
- [Research] Brunyé and colleagues (2011, Journal of Cognitive Psychology, "Better you than I") compared "you" and "I" protagonists in narratives; result not retrieved (see Gaps). [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/02/090218135124.htm)
- [Research] Hartung, Burke, Hagoort and Willems (2016, PLOS ONE 11(5), e0154732): literary stories in first vs third person; first person produced higher immersion, specifically transportation and mental imagery; arousal (skin conductance) tentatively higher for third person; appreciation not affected. No second-person condition. [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4883771)
- [Research] Hartung, Hagoort and Willems (2017, fMRI): no evidence for a neural dissociation between first- and third-person stories. [PDF](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2419703_2/component/file_2419702/Hartung_Hagoort_Wilems_2017.pdf)
- [Practitioner] Robert Kurvitz (Disco Elysium) argues tabletop RPGs, and likely CRPGs, are second-person narratives; second person feels gimmicky in literature but natural when a game master speaks to players; in Disco Elysium the skills take the narrating role and address the player. [Edge Q&A via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/australia/edge/20191107/282961041930482)
- [Practitioner] Emily Short, on Eric Eve's Shelter from the Storm: second-person present narration can create a strongly characterised player character; playing a first-person version felt more distancing to her. [emshort.blog](https://emshort.blog/2009/06/02/eric-eves-shelter-from-the-storm/)
- [Practitioner] Emily Short (2023): an emotional connection between player character and an NPC is less robust if the player character is not well defined, though players do not always want a predefined character. [Choice of Games interview](https://www.choiceofgames.com/2023/07/author-interview-emily-short-elite-status-platinum-concierge/)
- [Practitioner] Short's resource list on second person points to Nick Montfort's view that the player character "is not played at all, but is a constraint and possibility defined by the author", and to works (Photopia, Rameses) that make a point of the player's inability to change the plot, plus complicity with an immoral protagonist (1981, Bliss, The Last Hour). [emshort.blog second person resources](https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/books-and-other-resources/second-person/)
- [Practitioner] Kyle Seeley: the nostalgia of the chat client "transports the player into their own memories", and players project their own memories and experiences onto the characters. [We Got This Covered](https://wegotthiscovered.com/gaming/interview-kyle-seeley-emily-is-away/); [Inverse](https://inverse.com/article/9721-an-appreciation-of-emily-is-away-a-game-based-in-aim-chat-and-millennial-nostalgia)
- [Practitioner] Florence has little written dialogue; music carries the voices (cello for Krish, piano for Florence); the team chose a linear structure partly because players with more life experience would make the "correct" choices and miss the point. [Florence, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_(video_game))
- [Practitioner] Ken Wong: leaving things unsaid lets the audience fill in the gaps; the Monument Valley protagonist has no face and the camera never gets close. [GameDaily](https://gamedaily.biz/?p=2307); Wong also describes the Florence soundtrack as standing in for the characters' voices. [MCV](https://mcvuk.com/?p=23751)
- [Practitioner] What Remains of Edith Finch: Edith narrates from her journal, and the voice-over appears as text placed in the game world; the end reveals the reader is her son Christopher, for whom the journal was written. [Edith Finch, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_Remains_of_Edith_Finch). Ian Dallas: each family story was built first and the connective structure found later. [GamingTrend](https://gamingtrend.com/interviews/interview-ian-dallas-talks-about-ambiguity-cut-features-and-magic-in-what-remains-of-edith-finch); [God is a Geek](https://www.godisageek.com/2017/04/interview-with-ian-dallas-creative-director-on-what-remains-of-edith-finch/)
- [Practitioner] To the Moon: two doctors (a frame) travel backwards through a dying man's memories via "mementos"; the late reveals (a forgotten childhood meeting, beta blockers, River's paper rabbits) re-read a recurring object. [To the Moon, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_the_Moon_(video_game)). An interviewer notes the game "wasn't afraid to provide some lighter moments throughout". [Analog Addiction](https://analogaddiction.org/2014/09/05/to-the-moon-director-talks-about-a-bird-story-part-1)
- [Practitioner] 80 Days is narrated through Passepartout, the player-controlled valet; about 750,000 words, of which one journey shows a fraction. [80 Days, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/80_Days_(2014_video_game)); [inkle announcement](https://www.inklestudios.com/2014/04/01/eighty-days)

### Inferences
- [Inference] The draft's choice (second person, present tense, short declarative captions) is the configuration with the most lab support for an inside-the-actor viewpoint. The cost of that strength: every imposed act lands as the player's act.
- [Inference] The draft imposes small neglects on "you" without asking: not texting Dad from the lake (148), letting the friend go (206), "Nobody needs to know you got home" (229), not opening the jar (325). These are universal and forgivable, which suits complicity design (Photopia-style inevitability). The two morally loaded moments are offered as choices (teen reply 170, Send/Delete 382). That split is right; keep morally loaded acts as choices and keep imposed acts small and common.
- [Inference] The draft voice sits closest to Emily is Away (the phone is the stage, waiting is the tension) and Florence (short beats, a life, near-wordless deaths), with an Edith Finch / To the Moon reveal that re-identifies a recurring object or reader. Edith Finch's lesson for the ending: the reveal is carried by the frame device (who holds the journal), not by narration explaining it.
- [Craft opinion] One voice risk in the draft is the thesis line: a caption that states the lesson ("Some people are there for one chapter. It still counts.", "The door was open the whole time.", "Most lines cross yours once and keep going."). Wong's "leave things unsaid" and Kurvitz's warning about gimmick both argue for trusting the beat. The draft's best captions are concrete ("You're fine. The bush isn't.", "She never used to put the full stop.").
- [Inference] Emily Short's point on definition applies to the "people you keep close" picks (271 to 278): they are labels, never characters, so their payoffs (450, 480, 493 to 498) attach to a word, not a person.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests second-person narration in interactive fiction or chat-style games against first or third person.
- The 2011 Brunyé "Better you than I" result was not retrieved; its direction is unverified here.
- No study found on reader resistance or reactance when "you" does something the reader would not; the complicity point rests on practitioner writing.
- The grammatical person of 80 Days' narration was not confirmed by a source in this pass; only that Passepartout narrates.
- No primary Kan Gao statement on the reverse structure or tone balance of To the Moon was found.

## 4. Twists and reveals: fair clues, recontextualisation, spoilers and suspense

### Takeaway
Research says surprise is valued and remembered, suspense survives knowing the outcome, and spoilers do not reliably ruin enjoyment (contested). Tobin's account: a well-made surprise is a sudden reinterpretation plus the feeling the evidence was there all along, so the craft is in planting clues the reader sees but reads the wrong way. The draft has three plants for its twist; the ending then explains the twist in words three times, which takes the "of course" away from the player.

### Cited Findings
- [Research] Hoeken and van Vliet (2000, Poetics 27, 277 to 286), building on Brewer and Lichtenstein's structural affect theory (1982): suspense comes from postponing the outcome, curiosity from presenting the outcome before the events that led to it, surprise from an unexpected event. Suspense was evoked even when readers knew the ending; a surprising event was highly appreciated and improved memory for events. [Abstract](https://www.aminer.cn/pub/53e9b823b7602d97043dc075/suspense-curiosity-and-surprise-how-discourse-structure-influences-the-affective-and-cognitive)
- [Research] Gerrig (1989, Journal of Memory and Language 28, 633 to 648), "anomalous suspense": an obstacle placed before a well-known outcome (George Washington's election) made readers slower to verify that outcome; the effect lasted across a short delay and disappeared once the suspense was resolved. [Stony Brook record](https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/suspense-in-the-absence-of-uncertainty/); [Frontiers 2020 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7644968)
- [Research] Leavitt and Christenfeld (2011, Psychological Science): 819 participants each read three short stories (including ironic-twist stories and mysteries); versions with a spoiler paragraph presented before the story were enjoyed more; the benefit appeared when the spoiler came in advance, outside the piece. [Psychology Today summary by Keith Oatley](https://psychologytoday.com/gb/blog/the-psychology-of-fiction/201110/spoilers-dont-spoil-stories); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/853515)
- [Research] Contradicted: Johnson and Rosenbaum (2015) and Levine, Betzner and Autry found spoilers reduce enjoyment; the conflict is partly attributed to different spoiler types. [Later paper, CORE](https://core.ac.uk/download/72840983.pdf)
- [Practitioner] Tobin: the "curse of knowledge" (assuming others know what you know) is something stories exploit; a well-made surprise gives "a sudden reinterpretation" together with the feeling that "the evidence for this interpretation was there all along"; the best plots feel like "a well-oiled trap". [PopMatters review](https://popmatters.com/elements-of-surprise-vera-tobin-2566987422.html); [Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/544879/why-our-brains-love-plot-twists)
- [Practitioner] Edith Finch and To the Moon both resolve on an identity or memory reveal that re-reads a recurring device (the journal's reader; the paper rabbits). [Edith Finch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_Remains_of_Edith_Finch); [To the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_the_Moon_(video_game))

## 5. Clarity: confusion, transportation, cognitive load, who/when/where signposting in a time-jumping story

### Takeaway
Transportation is a convergence of attention on the story world, and it predicts the story's emotional and persuasive impact. Time shifts measurably raise processing load and drop earlier information from memory, and cuts are perceived as event boundaries when the action breaks. In practice: after each jump, the first line should re-establish who and when, and a name should replace a pronoun whose referent was last seen before the jump.

### Cited Findings
- [Research] Green and Brock (2000, JPSP 79, 701 to 721): four experiments; a transportation scale of 11 general items plus 4 story-specific imagery items; higher transportation went with more story-consistent beliefs and more favourable evaluations of protagonists. Transportation is described as a convergent process in which "all mental systems and capacities become focused on events occurring in the narrative". [Paper PDF](https://gwern.net/docs/www/pdfs.semanticscholar.org/39691cee32e52fbfc33f1a0316aa0d008cb699a4.pdf); [OnFiction summary](https://www.onfiction.ca/2014/06/transportation-into-narrative-worlds.html)
- [Research] Zwaan's event-indexing model: readers track time, space, causality, intentionality and protagonist; a discontinuity on a dimension raises reading time; time shifts deactivate prior information. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03193388); [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2667942)
- [Research] Magliano and Zacks (2011, Cognitive Science): viewers segmenting a commercial film perceived event boundaries at cuts with situational discontinuities; a break in action appears necessary for an event boundary in film; in a well-edited film, scene-break cuts are perceived as event boundaries. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3208769); [2009 abstract](https://condor.depaul.edu/dallbrit/extra/psy588/topics/abstract-2009-magliano.html)
- [Research] Hartung et al. (2016): transportation and mental imagery are separable components of immersion that pronoun choice moved. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4883771)
- [Practitioner] Edith Finch places its narration as text in the scene, so who is speaking and where is carried by the space. [Edith Finch, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_Remains_of_Edith_Finch)
- [Script] Pronouns after jumps: line 129 "You let him anyway" (Grandpa; last named at 103 to 104, and the latest male referent is Dad at 121); 151 "he's at the gate" (the dog) right after two lines about Dad (148 to 149); 154 "He laughs" (Grandpa) right after two lines about the dog (151 to 152); 163 "One day he isn't at the gate" (the dog) after lines about Grandpa, Grandma and the friend. The dog's name "Biscuit" appears once (97).
- [Script] Good first lines after jumps: "Saturday." (133), "Eighteen. One bag, one bus." (211), "One Sunday you get up early" (263), "Sundays you drive out to Mom." (460). The flashback at 530 carries the label `when: 'years ago'`.
- [Script] Life-line moves with no caption: Dad moves away at age 6.1 (111); the friend leaves at 14.3 (160) but the caption comes at 17 (196), after the whole dog sequence.

### Inferences
- [Inference] The phone UI already signposts who and when well (chat header, day and time stamps), which is why the chat beats are the clearest in the draft. The captions are where clarity slips, almost always at a pronoun after a jump. Naming the referent in the first line after a jump is the cheapest fix and is directly supported by the deactivation finding.
- [Inference] Un-captioned life-line moves are the film equivalent of a cut without an action break: some players will read them as events (Dad moving away at six can read as a separation). Either caption a move or make it too small to read as an event.
- [Inference] Lowering confusion matters most at the end, where transportation should be highest. The draft's one real clarity risk there is "you sound like grandpa" (523), which points at the wrong man for most players (see critique).

### Gaps
- No study found that directly measures confusion lowering transportation in games or short fiction; the link here is inferred from the convergent-attention definition and the time-shift processing costs.
- No study found on legibility of flashback labels ("years ago") vs visual-only cues in games.
