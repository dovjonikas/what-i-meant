# Tears, poignancy and endings: the psychology behind a story that hits

Scope: what produces tears and poignancy, how time running out and meaning shape them, and how to build to a peak and end. Kama muta, music chills, regret, humour beside sadness, pet loss and visual novel craft were covered in earlier notes and are not repeated here.

## 1. Crying and tears: which moments produce them

### Takeaway
Adult tears come less from sadness itself than from **helplessness, loss and separation**, and, on the positive side, from **closeness plus a moral act** (love, self-sacrifice, reunion, kindness). Tears are a social signal: they mark that something matters and pull others in. The strongest lab sadness stimulus ever tested is a child weeping over his dead father.

### Cited Findings
- The review names powerlessness as "probably the most common emotional trigger" of adult tears, usually blended with sadness, anger, fear or disappointment. [Gračanin et al., PMC6402489](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6402489/)
- Prototypical triggers: bereavement, breakups, moves and separations; loneliness and rejection are among the strongest attachment triggers (citing Vingerhoets 2013). [Gračanin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6402489/)
- Vingerhoets (2013) sorts tear antecedents into five types: physical pain, attachment-related pain, empathic pain, societal pain, and sentimental or moral feelings. [Gračanin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6402489/)
- Moral or sentimental tears are triggered by films and stories about self-sacrifice, altruism and lasting love (citing Tan and Frijda 1999). Vingerhoets calls these tears "exclamation marks": they flag that a situation matters to the person and to society. [Gračanin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6402489/)
- Positive tears come at weddings, reunions and music, and older adults cry more and more at positive, meaning-giving experiences (citing Cova and Deonna 2014; Vingerhoets 2013). [Gračanin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6402489/)
- People *predict* that losses and sad films make them cry, but their most recent real crying episodes more often involved conflict, criticism, small failures or rejection. [Gračanin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6402489/)
- Functions: tears signal helplessness and ask for help (attachment behaviour, Bowlby), promote bonding and inhibit aggression (Hasson 2009). Visible tears raise observers' attributed kindness, empathy, connectedness and willingness to help (Balsters et al. 2013; Hendriks and Vingerhoets 2006). [Gračanin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6402489/)
- Tears versus sadness: crying faces drew more felt sadness, more support and less avoidance from observers than sad faces without tears. [Gračanin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6402489/)
- Mood after crying at a sad film: mood drops right after the film; 90 minutes later criers were better than both their post-film mood and their baseline (Gračanin et al. 2015). Over 70% of people believe crying helps; only about 50% report mood improvement after their latest cry. [Gračanin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6402489/)
- Experts in the field agree the key antecedents are helplessness, hopelessness and having no adequate response to a situation, rather than sadness as such. **[snippet, from a Vingerhoets co-authored summary seen in search]** [search source list](https://vingerhoets.squarespace.com/research)
- Positive versus negative tears: tears during positive events went with feeling moved, goosebumps and chest warmth, and appraisals of **closeness and moral acts** explained the difference (Seibt et al. 2017, *Emotion*). **[snippet]** [abstract record](https://biomed.reviewer.ly/app/publication/051d490e-e7e9-4d7d-9941-6c703e2c9aa6)
- Tan and Frijda describe sentimental emotion as being moved more than the cause seems to warrant, tied to softness and helplessness; it is a reaction to others' fate, and as a "witness emotion" it does not push the viewer to act, because the viewer cannot act on the screen. **[snippet; the page returned 404 when fetched]** [HFS summary](https://hfs.hr/hfs/ljetopis_clanak_detail_e.asp?sif=1217)
- Gross and Levenson (1995) tested 78 sad scenes on about 500 people; the ending of *The Champ* (1979), a boy weeping over his father's body, produced the most sadness, ahead of Bambi's mother's death. It became the most used sadness clip in research. **[press only, original paper not fetched]** [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/the-saddest-movie-in-the-world-33826787/)

### Inferences
- The tear moment is not "the saddest fact" but the moment where **nothing can be done** (helplessness) or where **love is shown in an act** (moral and attachment tears). The best beats do both at once: loss plus someone's care inside the loss.
- *The Champ* clip is a child witnessing a parent's death and being unable to change it: parent-child bond, loss, total helplessness, all in one image. The father-child axis is the most reliable sadness channel known in the lab.
- Players should not be steered by what they *say* makes them cry (big losses); small, ordinary wounds (rejection, being left on read, a friend drifting) are what real crying episodes are made of.
- Because tears lift mood later rather than at once, a story does not need to repair the player's mood before it ends.

### Gaps
- Vingerhoets' book itself was not read; its antecedent taxonomy is known only through the 2018 review.
- No study found that measured tears frame by frame in a narrative to show *which* moment in a scene triggers them (Wassiliwizky et al. 2017, "Tears falling on goosebumps", exists but its stimuli and results were not retrieved).

## 2. Poignancy and mixed emotions at endings

### Takeaway
Making people aware that something good is **ending** adds sadness **without removing happiness**; the mix is poignancy. It works at any age: young adults told "this is the last time" respond like old people, whose sense of limited time already gives them more mixed feelings.

### Cited Findings
- Ersner-Hershfield, Mikels, Sullivan and Carstensen (2008) define poignancy as mixed happiness and sadness arising when a meaningful thing ends; they cite Duncker: a lack weighs more when it is "a no-longer-having, a loss". [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2807633)
- Study 1: 60 younger (mean age 19.6) and 60 older adults (77.5) imagined a meaningful place three times. On the third trial the experimental group imagined it as their **last** visit. Only that group showed a significant rise in sadness and in mixed emotions; their happiness fell but stayed above the scale midpoint. No age differences. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2807633)
- Study 2: 110 Stanford seniors on graduation day. Those told that today was their last day as students reported the same happiness as controls but significantly more sadness and more mixed emotion. Controls in this real ending already showed more mixed emotion than lab controls. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2807633)
- The authors read this through socioemotional selectivity theory: limited time turns people toward emotionally meaningful goals (more positive feeling) but also brings "last times" (added sadness). The age shift in mixed emotion is driven by time perspective, not age. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2807633)
- Poignancy is about anticipating an ending; nostalgia is about the past. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2807633)
- An OSF replication of the graduation study found a discrepancy; its authors point to a different university and to surveying before rather than after the ceremony. The original group replicated Study 1 in 2009. **[snippet]** [OSF](https://osf.io/sq8k9/wiki/home)
- A 2021 *Emotion* paper (Larsen, Hershfield and colleagues, with Carstensen) proposes that meaningful endings make people **reminisce about good times**, which partly explains the mixed feelings. **[snippet]** [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8817627)
- Larsen, McGraw and Cacioppo (2001): in ordinary moments people report happy *or* sad, but many felt both right after watching *Life Is Beautiful*, moving out of their dorms, or graduating. **[snippet, abstract]** [PDF](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/larsen.mcgraw.cacioppo.2001.pdf)

### Inferences
- The "last time" frame is the engine of poignancy, and its effect is in the **sadness channel only**: it does not cost happiness. So a happy scene can be made poignant by one cue that it will not come again, without making it a sad scene.
- In Study 1 the same imagined place came three times and only the third was framed as last. That is repetition with variation as an experimental design: the ending hits because the earlier instances were there.
- Ending + reminiscence is exactly the structure of a parent waiting on a porch and remembering a life. The frame itself is a poignancy machine.
- Putting a young player in an old person's place gives them the old person's time perspective for the length of the story, which is what Study 1 suggests is enough.

### Gaps
- No study found on whether the "last time" cue works better when known *during* the moment versus discovered afterward (the 2008 studies only test awareness during or just before).

## 3. Time scarcity and appreciation

### Takeaway
Framing time with something as scarce makes people enjoy it more and reach toward people more; labelling an item "the last" makes it seem better. People also assume they have stopped changing, which a life story can quietly disprove.

### Cited Findings
- Kurtz (2008, *Psychological Science*): college seniors six weeks before graduation who framed the remaining time as short enjoyed it more and took more advantage of it; Kurtz later said the "now or never" frame raised social engagement and happiness. **[snippet; manipulation wording not seen]** [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/01/090112110104.htm); [ISSEP essay](https://www.issep.org/features/times-scarcity-promotes-emotional-savoring)
- Layous, Kurtz, Chancellor and Lyubomirsky (2018): people asked to live a month as if it were their last in their city (n = 69) gained well-being over controls (n = 70), and the gain was explained by more **connectedness**, competence and autonomy. **[snippet]** [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2gh2s77q/qt2gh2s77q.pdf). Press coverage gives a different sample and duration (111 students, six weeks). [Pacific Standard](https://psmag.com/?p=16628)
- O'Brien and Ellsworth (2012): 52 students tasted five chocolates; those told the fifth was "the last one" liked it more, and the effect holds for trivial events too. **[snippet]** [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/what-kind-of-chocolate-is-best-the-last-you-taste-says-a-new-study.html)
- End of history illusion (Quoidbach, Gilbert and Wilson 2013, *Science*, over 19,000 people): at every age people report big change over the past decade but expect little over the next. **[snippet]** [Harvard](https://psychology.fas.harvard.edu/publications/end-history-illusion). Critique: the result rests on remembered past selves, not measured change. [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/end-history-illusion-illusion)

### Inferences
- Scarcity framing works through **connection**: in Layous et al. the benefit ran through feeling closer to people. That is the mechanism a story about texting home wants.
- The experiments frame the person's *own* window ("your last month here"). A generic statistic told by a narrator is a different thing; it can read as a lecture (see section 7 on reactance).
- The end of history illusion is why "you became the one who waits" lands: players do not expect to become their parents, and the story shows it happening.

### Gaps
- No experiment found that tests time-scarcity framing inside fiction (all studies frame the participant's real life).
- Kurtz's exact manipulation wording was not seen.

## 4. Elevation and meaningful entertainment: what makes people act on a story

### Takeaway
Meaningful (eudaimonic) media is the kind that is moving and thought-provoking, with mixed feelings; it is driven by depictions of **virtue, love, kindness and connectedness**. The emotion that carries into behaviour is **elevation** (seeing goodness), not happiness, amusement or sadness. Evidence shows people *feel* closer to family afterwards; no study found shows they actually call.

### Cited Findings
- Oliver and Bartsch (2011): appreciation of entertainment is strongest for works that show human virtue and make viewers think about life's purpose, and it involves mixed feelings such as tenderness, awe and inspiration. **[snippet, preprint]** [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/20483029.pdf)
- Schnall, Roper and Fessler (2010): people shown a clip of musicians thanking their mentors volunteered more for an unpaid study and spent about twice as long helping on a tedious task; **elevation, not amusement or happiness, predicted how much they helped**. **[snippet]** [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/02/100203121600.htm)
- Oliver et al. (2012), 483 students: meaningful films produced more elevation and motivation to be better and help others; pleasurable films motivated enjoyment and popularity seeking. [Greater Good](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_positive_media_can_make_us_better_people)
- Janicke(-Bowles) and Oliver: meaningful films showed more love, kindness and connectedness; elevation from them increased felt connection to close friends and **family**, compassionate love and kindness toward strangers. The article reports felt connection only, **not whether anyone contacted loved ones**. [Greater Good](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_positive_media_can_make_us_better_people). Note: the article dates this work 2015; the journal paper is Janicke and Oliver 2017, *Psychology of Popular Media Culture* 6(3). **[snippet]** [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/345020572.pdf)
- Elevation was triggered by moral acts: gratitude, generosity, loyalty. [Greater Good](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_positive_media_can_make_us_better_people)
- Tan and Frijda: sentimental (sad, witness) emotion does not push toward action. **[snippet]** [HFS summary](https://hfs.hr/hfs/ljetopis_clanak_detail_e.asp?sif=1217)

### Inferences
- Sadness moves people; goodness moves people **to do something**. If the hope is that a player texts their mother afterwards, the peak has to be an act of care (someone holding the dog, someone texting "Home." before you shut the door), not only a death.
- Kuiken, Miall and Sikora (2004) describe "expressive enactment", where readers blur the line between themselves and the narrator and the feeling keeps developing; it was more frequent in people still grieving an old loss. **[snippet]** [PDF](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/MiallPub/Kuiken-Miall-Sikora-2004.pdf). The second person and the player's own choices invite exactly this.

### Gaps
- No study found that measured actual contact with family (calls, texts) after a meaningful story. This is the key untested link for the author's goal.

## 5. Helplessness, waiting and the unsaid

### Takeaway
Fathers, especially with sons, tend to show love through supportive acts rather than words, so a rare spoken line carries huge weight. In grief, **unspoken affirmations and missed chances** are a distinct, distressing kind of unfinished business, and ordinary objects can become the link to the dead.

### Cited Findings
- Floyd and Morman (1999; 55 father and young adult son pairs): affection travels more through **social support** than through direct verbal or nonverbal expressions. A 2003 follow-up (170 men) supported the prediction that fathers show affection through supportive activities more than statements. **[snippet]** [Floyd PDF](https://kory-floyd-dev.squarespace.com/s/affectionate-communication-between-fathers-and-young-adult-sons.pdf); [Floyd essay](https://blavity.com/the-understated-affection-of-fathers)
- Klingspon, Holland, Neimeyer and Lichtenthal (2015, 224 bereaved adults): distress about unresolved issues with the deceased was a robust correlate of grief outcomes; the *type* of unfinished business did not matter. **[snippet]** [MSKCC record](https://synapse.mskcc.org/synapse/works/76720)
- The Unfinished Business in Bereavement Scale (Holland et al. 2020) has two factors: **Unfulfilled Wishes** (unspoken affirmations, missed opportunities) and Unresolved Conflict. Unfinished business plus meaning-making explained 50 to 60% of variance in prolonged grief symptoms. **[snippet]** [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7473452)
- Volkan's "linking objects": ordinary objects the bereaved keep that hold a felt connection to the dead (in his clinical frame, sometimes a sign of stuck mourning). **[publisher blurb only]** [Freud Museum shop](https://shop.freud.org.uk/products/linking-objects-and-linking-phenomena-a-study-of-the-forms-symptoms-metapsychology-and-therapy-of-complicated-mourning-vamik-d-volkan)
- Helplessness is the most common tear trigger (section 1). [Gračanin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6402489/)

### Inferences
- A father who only ever says "ok." is psychologically realistic, and the one "proud of you." is powerful because it breaks a long, visible pattern of love-as-acts. Every extra verbal affection before it would weaken it.
- The unsaid hurts most when the player sees the exact place it could have been said (an unanswered message, a pick never made). The research says the *kind* of missed thing does not matter; the felt missed chance does.
- Waiting for a text is the purest everyday helplessness: the player can do nothing. That is the tear condition from section 1, sustained.
- Objects (a jar, a signed text) work as linking objects; their weight comes from being kept after the person is gone and from being ordinary.

### Gaps
- No empirical work found on "inarticulate parents" in fiction specifically, or on how audiences read terse texts from parents.
- Guilt appeals: research on whether making the audience feel blamed backfires was not searched; flagged as a risk for the "you left it on read" callbacks.

## 6. Building to the peak

### Takeaway
Leftover arousal from one scene amplifies the next emotion, so tension right before a release makes the release bigger. Naming a feeling dampens it. Foreknowledge of a story's structure can help, but spoiling the beats makes stories less moving. Direct evidence that a callback beats a first instance was not found.

### Cited Findings
- Excitation transfer (Zillmann): residual arousal from an earlier emotion intensifies the next emotion if the second comes before the first has decayed; especially relevant to compact media where emotional scenes follow each other closely. **[snippet, encyclopedia level]** [Encyclopedia.com](https://encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/arousal-processes-and-media-effects)
- Lieberman et al. (2007, fMRI): labelling the emotion of an image (choosing "angry" or "scared") reduced amygdala response compared with other encoding; a neutral label (a name) did not. Lieberman: putting feelings into words is like hitting the brakes. **[snippet]** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576282/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/06/070622090727.htm)
- Torre and Lieberman (2018) review: affect labelling acts as **implicit** emotion regulation, lowering self-reported affect and autonomic responses, and may not feel like regulation as it happens. Evidence is mainly on negative stimuli. **[snippet]** [Emotion Review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1754073917742706)
- Leavitt and Christenfeld (2011, 819 readers, 12 stories): spoilers did not reduce, and seemed to increase, enjoyment; foreknowledge may help readers organise the story and attend to its meaning. **[snippet]** [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/08/110810093735.htm); [Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/gb/blog/the-psychology-of-fiction/201110/spoilers-dont-spoil-stories)
- Contradicted by Johnson and Rosenbaum (2015, 412 students): spoiled stories were rated lower on suspense and fun and were **less moving and thought-provoking**, with less immersion. **[snippet]** [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/12/151210101816.htm); a later thesis notes two follow-ups found spoilers decrease enjoyment. [Arkansas](https://scholarworks.uark.edu/psycuht/10)
- Ersner-Hershfield Study 1 structure: two ordinary repetitions, then the third framed as last; only the third raised sadness and mixed feeling. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2807633)

### Inferences
- Put tension (waiting, a typing indicator that stops) right before a release (a reply), not a calm gap; the leftover arousal spills into the relief.
- "Show, don't tell" has a mechanism behind it: labelling an emotion engages the brakes. The studies are about perceivers labelling, not a narrator labelling for the reader, so this is an inference, but the direction is consistent: narration should state facts and acts, never the feeling.
- Reconciling the spoiler studies: telling the shape of the story (a parent waits for a text) is foreknowledge that organises; telling a beat before it lands (a death signalled before the line) costs the moving part.
- Callbacks: the experimental pattern (repeat, then mark as last) supports building a phrase or gesture through ordinary repetitions before the one that counts. That a callback hits harder than a first instance is craft wisdom, not a tested finding.

### Gaps
- No experimental evidence found for "the second time is stronger" in narrative, or for emotional contrast effects beyond excitation transfer.
- Foregrounding research (Miall and Kuiken, striking style slows reading and raises feeling) was not searched.

## 7. Endings

### Takeaway
Memory of an experience is built from its **peak and its end**; length barely counts. Endings dominate even judgments of a whole life. A slightly better final stretch improves how the whole is remembered. Directives ("you must...") provoke reactance, and stories persuade precisely because they hide persuasive intent.

### Cited Findings
- Fredrickson and Kahneman (1993) used **film clips**: global ratings of pleasant and aversive clips were barely related to their length; people judged them by snapshots, mainly the most intense moment and the final one (peak and end). Duration sensitivity returns when people have a comparison standard or a familiar experience. **[snippet, abstract and summaries]** [PDF](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/fredr-kahneman-jpsp1993.pdf)
- Kahneman et al. (1993), "When more pain is preferred to less: adding a better end": people chose to repeat a longer cold-water trial that ended slightly less painfully. **[snippet]** [PDF](https://ius.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:5ae9adc9-61ec-4174-b37c-4b752f36c23b/Kahnemann%20et%20al.%20-%20When%20More%20Pain%20is%20Preferred%20to%20Less%20(1993).pdf)
- Diener, Wirtz and Oishi (2001), the James Dean effect: a wonderful life ending abruptly was rated better than the same life with extra mildly pleasant years; intense years placed at the end weighed more than the same years in the middle. **[snippet]** [Scilit](https://scilit.com/publications/a9d0541822a5d3517ea84a7c627ec097). Rozin and Stellar (2009) extend this: even events after death shift how good a life is judged. [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/37DFF6F33E312B3ACA7A445E7BE0D9A7/core-reader)
- O'Brien and Ellsworth (2012): the item labelled "last" is liked more. **[snippet]** [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/what-kind-of-chocolate-is-best-the-last-you-taste-says-a-new-study.html)
- Miller et al. (2007): controlling language in health messages produced a number of negative outcomes; a "restoration postscript" restoring the reader's choice produced more positive ones; concrete language got more attention. **[snippet]** [record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_85675885)
- Moyer-Gusé and Nabi (2010, 367 students): a dramatic narrative reduced reactance by building parasocial bonds with characters and **lowering perceived persuasive intent**. **[snippet]** [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/357574034.pdf)
- After a sad film, criers' mood dips and then rises above baseline within 90 minutes (Gračanin et al. 2015). [Gračanin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6402489/)

### Inferences
- A life story is judged like a life: by its last minutes. The final text and the final line will decide how the whole is remembered, more than any middle scene.
- A quiet beat after the peak acts as the "better end" added to tension: it lowers arousal gently instead of cutting at the peak. No study found tests a quiet final beat directly.
- A bittersweet end (relief after accumulated losses) keeps the mixed state that defines appreciation (section 4); an ending that tries to cheer the player up is not needed, since mood recovers by itself after tears.
- A closing "call your parents" makes the persuasive intent visible, which is exactly what narrative escapes; with controlling wording it invites reactance. If any bridge to real life exists, it should be optional, non-directive and leave the choice with the player.

### Gaps
- No study found compares happy, sad and bittersweet endings head to head on later behaviour.
- No study found tests a real-world prompt at the end of a fiction against no prompt. Stok and Renner (2017, healthy eating, autonomy-supportive versus controlling language) was seen only in a search summary without a confirmed URL.

## What this means for Text me when you get home

Based on reading the current draft script (`site/stories/text-me/js/script.js`). Any wording below is an illustration of the idea, not proposed text; the author writes the lines. Each item has a test.

1. **Keep the frame helpless and make it felt once.** (Helplessness is the top tear trigger, section 1.) On one porch return, let the player tap the phone and get nothing: the status stays Delivered. A failed action makes powerlessness felt instead of told. *Test:* players rate tension at the final "Read" with and without the dead tap.

2. **Add a porch return inside Afternoon, right after Mom's death.** (Helplessness plus loss; excitation transfer, section 6.) The longest stretch without the frame (21:44 until the end) holds both parents' deaths. One "Nothing yet." beat after "She signed it." stacks the old loss on the present wait. *Test:* free recall at the end ("which moment stayed with you?"); see if Mom's death moves up.

3. **Give the player one "last time" they know is last while it happens.** (Ersner-Hershfield: awareness of an ending adds sadness without taking happiness; O'Brien: "last" is liked more, sections 2 and 3.) Today every last time is discovered afterward (Grandma, Dad's chat, Mom's weather text), which produces regret, not poignancy. The natural place is the old dog: one walk to the gate the player knows is the last, played as a happy scene. *Test:* after the scene ask "how happy" and "how sad" separately; poignancy shows as both above zero.

4. **Narration never names the feeling.** (Affect labelling dampens emotion, section 6.) Audit lines such as "It was the best morning in a long time", "You were glad of that", "You were glad you went". Emotion words stay only inside characters' mouths, where they are acts ("proud of you.", "He wasn't scared."). *Test:* search the script for glad, sad, happy, scared, miss, lonely, proud and justify each hit.

5. **Rework or cut the line about most days with your parents being behind you.** (Scarcity works when it frames the person's own window; generic statements raise perceived persuasive intent, sections 3 and 7.) If kept, make it a count of something the player has already seen in the story, not a statistic. *Test:* ask playtesters "did the game ever feel like it was telling you something?"; if they name this line, change it.

6. **Protect Dad's silence: no verbal affection from him before "proud of you."** (Floyd and Morman: fathers' love as acts; the rare word breaks a long pattern, section 5.) The bike, "text me when you get there", the bag on and off the bus and holding the dog are already four acts; keep them and add no words.

7. **Make Dad's last chat a pattern, not one line.** (Repetition then ending, section 2; unfulfilled wishes, section 5.) Show several dated "home" and "ok." pairs scrolling up, so the player sees years of themselves texting home (the teen who never did, changed) and his reply every time. If the earlier "proud of you." sits in that history unanswered, the unsaid is visible without a word of narration. *Test:* ask players what "ok." meant; aim for "love" without prompting.

8. **Pair every death with an act of care.** (Moral and attachment tears; elevation drives action, sections 1 and 4.) Dog: Dad holding him. Grandma: the jar. Mom: the signed text. Dad: the "ok.". Grandpa has none; either give him one small act early, or keep him the death the player "didn't get yet" on purpose. *Test:* which deaths players can recall a day later.

9. **Close the jar thread with an action, or leave it open on purpose.** (Linking objects, section 5; endings dominate memory, section 7.) "You didn't open it for a long time" never closes; the late callback uses Grandma's words, not the jar. One option is the player handing a jar to their own kid at the last dinner. *Test:* A/B the two versions; compare end ratings and whether players mention the jar unprompted.

10. **Keep all callbacks as role swaps.** ("You don't remember any of this. They do." to "Your kid won't remember any of this. You will."; "I've got you" to "I've got it"; "Come visit more" from Grandma to you; "Text me when you get home" from parents to you to the kid.) The swap is what carries time and the end of history illusion (section 3). Rule: every callback is set up at least twice before the one that counts (section 2's design). *Test:* count how many callbacks playtesters notice unprompted.

11. **Never signal a death before its line lands.** (Spoiled beats are less moving, section 6.) The porch frame may reveal the shape of the story; the life lines must not end a person's line visually before the words arrive. *Test:* step through every `end:` event and check the order of animation and text.

12. **Keep the guilt callbacks forgiving.** ("She waited up anyway" is right.) The teen choice and the weekend choice are unfulfilled wishes (section 5) and should hurt, but the narration must not accuse; blame was not researched and is a risk. *Test:* ask "did the game judge you?"; the answer should be no.

13. **Keep the tension before "home." and do not shorten it.** (Excitation transfer and "adding a better end", sections 6 and 7.) Delivered, the long pause, Read, typing that stops and starts again, then "home.". Try one variant a little longer. *Test:* two timings, ask which ending felt stronger.

14. **End quiet, after the peak, with no coda and no instruction.** (Peak-end; James Dean effect; reactance, section 7.) "You turn off the kitchen light. ... You sleep fine." is the better end added after the peak. Add no "call your parents" line. If a bridge to real life is wanted, make it an optional, non-directive affordance after the end card that leaves the choice with the player. *Test:* A/B with and without it; ask a day later whether players texted or called someone after playing (the outcome no study has measured).

15. **Make the final reply an act of goodness, not only relief.** (Elevation, not happiness, predicts action, section 4.) The kid texting "home." follows the rule the player broke as a teen; that is the moral act, and the line just before it ("She waited up anyway") already sets it up. Keep that order. *Test:* ask players in their own words what the ending was about; "being cared for" or "becoming my parents" means it worked.
