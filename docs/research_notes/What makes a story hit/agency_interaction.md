# Agency and interaction: how choice, the player's own actions, waiting and messaging change emotion in a story

Evidence markers: [FULL] read in full or the relevant pages read; [ABS] abstract, publisher page, press release or a trade-press report of the work; [SNIP] seen only in a search-engine summary or snippet, not verified; [GK] general knowledge, not checked in this session. Earlier notes (`docs/research_notes/Story games emotion and web tools/engagement_science.md`) already hold the headline Fendt et al. result, Cardona-Rivera, Thue, Ryan/Rigby/Przybylski and the Arrambide dissertation; they are not repeated here except where new detail was found. That file listed "regret, guilt and responsibility after choices" as a gap; section 1 fills it.

## 1. Agency and emotion: does making a choice raise felt responsibility, guilt, regret and attachment? What happens once players notice the illusion of choice?

### Takeaway
Choices raise appreciation and felt responsibility when they are moral, social and have consequences, and players treat characters as if they were real people. Guilt follows choices the player sees as their own and unjustified, and it does not reduce enjoyment. Two limits matter: people feel less agency over bad outcomes (they disown them), and forced complicity tends to produce resentment more than guilt. Acknowledging a choice right away recovers most felt agency. The one thing it does not recover is the sense that "the story would have been different", which comes from callbacks or real consequences.

### Cited Findings
- Iten, Steinemann and Opwis (CHI 2018), "Choosing to Help Monsters": a qualitative study of what makes game choices meaningful, then an experiment. Choices rated high on moral, social and consequential characteristics raised appreciation (the reflective "this meant something" response) - [ABS] [researchr](https://researchr.org/publication/ItenSO18). The authors suggest that players often not seeing choices as meaningful may explain why narrative games affect people inconsistently - same source.
- Steinemann et al. (Journal of Media Psychology, 2017): an interactive and a non-interactive version of the same narrative did not differ significantly in later prosocial behaviour. Appreciation of the narrative and engagement with it did predict prosocial behaviour - [SNIP] [Aalto research portal](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/interactive-narratives-affecting-social-change-a-closer-look-at-t/)
- Fendt et al. (ICIDS 2012), new detail from the full paper: a branching story (n = 52 on the comparison path), a linear story with immediate acknowledgement of each choice (n = 54) and a linear story with minimal feedback ("You grab your weapon and head out", n = 44). The branching story beat minimal feedback on every story-level agency item. Against acknowledged-but-linear, it differed significantly on only one item, "the story would have been different if I had selected different choices" (p = 0.030), with a marginal difference on "control over aspects I wanted control over" (p = 0.084). In the branching version, early choices were referenced again later in the story, and this was the only feature the linear-with-feedback version lacked - [FULL] [Fendt et al. PDF](https://www.cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/fendt2012achieving.pdf). Caveat: the acknowledged-linear group was 47 men and 7 women, while the branching group had more women - same source, Table 1.
- Weaver and Lewis (2012, Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking), n = 75 playing the first act of Fallout 3: most players made moral decisions and treated non-player characters as if the exchanges were real. Antisocial choices increased guilt but did not lower enjoyment. Moral foundations scores predicted choices - [ABS] [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23017118/)
- Hartmann, Toz and Brandon (2010, Media Psychology 13), "Just a game? Unjustified virtual violence produces guilt in empathetic players". The title states the finding: guilt depends on the act being unjustified and on the player's empathy - [SNIP] (known through later papers that cite it, e.g. [Frontiers in Psychology 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.666518))
- Grizzard et al. (2014): guilt from playing an immoral role correlated with greater sensitivity to the specific moral foundations the player had violated. The authors suggest that guilt caused by virtual acts may lead to prosocial behaviour - [ABS] [University at Buffalo release](https://www.buffalo.edu/news/releases/2014/06/037.html). A 2016 follow-up found that guilt fades with repeated play (desensitization), though the mechanism is unclear - [ABS] [UB Now](https://www.buffalo.edu/ubnow/stories/2016/04/grizzard-guilt-video-games.html)
- Yoshie and Haggard (2013, Current Biology): when a key press led to a negative emotional sound rather than a positive or neutral one, people perceived a longer delay between action and outcome (weaker intentional binding), meaning a lower sense of agency over bad outcomes. Coverage linked this to taking credit for good results and resisting blame for bad ones - [ABS] [UCL Discovery](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1425000); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/10/131003121248.htm). A 2017 follow-up says the effect depends on predicting the outcome's valence before acting - [ABS] [Scientific Reports via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/54809997cd694d269356f19b47986f2b)
- Bopp, Mekler and Opwis (CHI 2016), 121 players' accounts of emotionally moving game moments: most players enjoyed and appreciated negative emotions such as sadness. Triggers included in-game loss, attachment to characters, lack of agency, and personal memories, often with self-reflection - [ABS] [Aalto research portal](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/negative-emotion-positive-experience-emotionally-moving-moments-i/)
- Bopp et al. (CHI PLAY 2019), qualitative survey of 213 players: seven distinct forms of attachment to game characters, from admiring their competence to deep concern for their well-being - [ABS] [Aalto repository](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/6cef35fe-1653-4627-912d-79a8947070fe). Bopp's later summary: a vulnerable character arouses worry, a strong one enthusiasm - [SNIP] [Aalto news](https://aalto.fi/en/news/video-games-evoke-emotions-that-even-music-and-cinema-fail-to-reach)
- Telltale's The Walking Dead, studio statistics (not a study): in episode 2, 87% waited rather than shoot, 85% amputated David's leg and 80% spared Andy St. John. Telltale's marketing director said "the majority of people will try to do the 'right' thing" even when no right option exists - [ABS] [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/telltale-games-the-walking-dead-statistics-trailer/)
- Spec Ops: The Line: at GDC 2013 the writer Walt Williams argued that a game is defined by the actions the player takes, so those actions carry its meaning - [ABS] [Kotaku report](https://kotaku.com/spec-ops-writer-on-violent-games-were-better-than-th-460992384). In an interview, he said the player should be pushed to ask whether to keep going - [SNIP] [quoted on The Dish](https://dish.andrewsullivan.com/2013/05/21/gaming-morality/). Critics note that the white phosphorus scene gives no real alternative, then blames the player for pressing the button. This is commentary, not data - [SNIP] [The Artifice](https://the-artifice.com/white-phosphorus-in-spec-ops/)
- Jon Ingold (inkle): choices make players buy into the game and "take some responsibility for it". He works in scenes with one entry and one exit and dislikes feedback that shows the variables underneath - [ABS] [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/should-games-have-meaningful-choices-)
- That Dragon, Cancer: Amy Green describes collapsing the choices onto the player so that they discover nothing they do changes the outcome for Joel - [SNIP] [TED talk transcript mirror](https://rosetta.to/u/ted/a-video-game-to-cope-with-grief-amy-green)
- Illusion of choice: a thesis testing whether players dislike the illusion of choice found that on average they do not, while still wanting meaningful choice - [ABS] [Theseus](https://www.theseus.fi/handle/10024/167282). Research on magicians' forcing techniques shows people get an illusory sense of agency and are poor at judging whether their action changed the outcome - [ABS] [Goldsmiths, Pailhès, Kumari and Kuhn](https://research.gold.ac.uk/29234)

### Inferences
- Guilt and responsibility in games come from three ingredients that line up with the lab work: the act feels chosen (Ingold, Iten), it hurts someone the player cares about (Bopp 2019, Weaver), and it was not forced or justified (Hartmann). Remove any one and the feeling weakens.
- Bad outcomes reduce felt authorship (Yoshie and Haggard). A story that punishes a choice with a disaster invites the player to disown it ("the game made that happen") rather than own it. Small, plausible consequences that the player could have foreseen are more likely to be owned than dramatic ones.
- Forced complicity (Spec Ops) works for a story about the player being complicit, but a story about ordinary family life gains more from mirroring than from blame: show the player the same situation from the other side and let them judge themselves.
- Players mostly choose the kind option (Telltale, Weaver). Any choice where one option is "the right thing" will be decided that way by most players, so its emotional value lies in the cost of the kind option rather than in the branch itself.
- The illusion of choice is tolerated (Theseus, Fendt) until a replay or a comparison with a friend exposes it. In a short web story that people may replay or share, the exposure risk is higher than in a 10-hour game. The cheapest protection is to make each option produce a different next line and a later callback.

### Gaps
- Almost all guilt research uses violent or antisocial acts. I found no study of guilt or regret after small relational choices in games, such as ignoring a parent's message or skipping a visit.
- No empirical study measures the "feel cheated on replay" effect; it is argued in criticism only.
- A search summary claimed Telltale data showed players pulling punches because Clementine was watching. I could not find the original source, so it is left out.
- The full text of Iten et al. 2018 was not reachable (403), so effect sizes are unknown.

## 2. Embodied interaction and enactment: does doing an action (holding, pressing, typing) rather than watching it raise emotion, empathy or memory?

### Takeaway
Doing beats watching for memory, by a large margin. The benefit comes mainly from planning the action and less from the movement itself, and it shows most clearly when performed actions are mixed with watched ones. The developers whose input is the emotional peak tie one simple verb to the meaning of the scene (holding on, letting go, hugging, being two brothers at once) and change the meaning of the verb at the climax. Haptics add arousal in small studies; the evidence on empathy is thin.

### Cited Findings
- Roberts, MacLeod and Fernandes (2022, Psychological Bulletin) meta-analysis of the enactment effect: 145 behavioural studies, mean effect g = 1.23 for memory of performed versus non-performed actions. The benefit is attributed mainly to a mental contribution (planning the action) and secondarily to the physical action itself - [ABS] [Waterloo lab PDF](https://uwaterloo.ca/memory-attention-cognition-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/enact22.pdf)
- Steffens, von Stülpnagel and Schult (2015): in free recall, the enactment effect is typically found when performed and observed actions are mixed in one list, and rarely when every action is performed or every action is observed. Recognition shows it more consistently - [ABS] [Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01907/full)
- Florence (Ken Wong): the feeling "has to come from the interactions, not from the images". He wanted players to act events out rather than tap dialogue to advance, and more than half the levels the team tried did not work - [ABS] [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/designing-i-florence-i-to-convey-the-ineffable-feeling-of-being-in-love). (The puzzle-pieces-per-bubble detail is already in `First story feel and look/life_story_structure.md`.)
- Before Your Eyes: Graham Parkes says the player is "trying to hold on to these moments" but must eventually blink. Bela Messex says the physical discomfort is deliberate and gives the player an incentive to leave the scene. Most scenes are very short, to mirror fleeting memory - [ABS] [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gone-before-you-blink-the-webcam-driven-gameplay-of-before-your-eyes)
- Brothers: A Tale of Two Sons: Josef Fares says he knew the control scheme and the ending from day one, insisted on single player, and said co-op would destroy the vision - [SNIP] [Giant Bomb interview](https://giantbomb.com/articles/a-tale-of-one-game/1100-4736/) (403 on fetch); [Shacknews](https://shacknews.com/article/82627/brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons-creator-talks-inspiration-and). A review argues co-op loses the kinship felt through dual command - [SNIP] [Press Start](https://press-start.com.au/?p=152678). At the ending, the surviving younger brother must do something he could not do before (swim), and the player uses the dead older brother's side of the controller to do it [GK, not verified here].
- Spiritfarer: creative director Nicolas Guérin said it was basically impossible to make a game about care without physical affection. The hug came through iteration and became central to development, reinforcing mechanical ways of caring for others - [ABS] [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/inside-the-thoughtful-design-of-thunder-lotus-i-spiritfarer-i-); [GameSkinny](https://www.gameskinny.com/culture/thunder-lotus-on-spiritfarer-death-hugs-and-legacies/)
- That Dragon, Cancer: reviews note that scenes alternate between observer and participant perspectives - [SNIP] [A.V. Club](https://www.avclub.com/that-dragon-cancer-asks-players-to-share-the-burden-of-1798186343)
- Haptics: tactile signals following speech amplitude made speech feel more arousing and dominant than speech alone, framed as a way to carry emotion over mobile phones - [SNIP] [Advances in HCI 2012 via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/e95310d33d224910bb0d0b43b8704774). A two-page poster (n = 9) found that vibration triggered by an actor's reactions raised empathy and excitement - [ABS] [Eurographics digital library](https://diglib.eg.org/items/17b90c8c-a1fc-46bd-a415-9a28dab4b8e8/full). Smartphone vibration frequency and intensity predicted preference or aversion in 59 people - [ABS] [CMS conferences](https://openaccess.cms-conferences.org/publications/book/978-1-964867-32-8/article/978-1-964867-32-8_6)
- Web vibration (navigator.vibrate) works in Chrome on Android but not in Safari on iOS [GK, not verified here].

### Inferences
- The enactment effect concerns memory, not emotion directly. The link to emotion runs through attention and through the player owning the moment, which is an inference.
- Since planning is the main ingredient, the decision of when to start, stop or release counts for more than how long a finger stays down. A hold earns its place when releasing it means something, as in Before Your Eyes.
- The mixed-list result says embodied moments stand out against watched ones. A story that makes every beat physical loses the contrast, so a few physical moments placed among tap-to-read stretches should hit harder than many.
- Every developer example uses one verb with a fixed meaning that is then bent at the peak (both sticks in Brothers, blinking in Before Your Eyes, hugging in Spiritfarer). The verb works as a metaphor the player performs.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly compares emotional intensity for holding versus tapping versus watching the same story beat.
- No peer-reviewed analysis of Brothers' ending or of Journey's touch and chirp mechanics was found in this pass.
- Haptic and empathy evidence comes from small samples (9 to 59 people).

## 3. Effort, the IKEA effect and effort justification: does effort or holding time make an outcome feel more meaningful, and where does it tip into annoyance on a phone?

### Takeaway
Effort raises the value of an outcome only when the effort is completed and visible: the IKEA effect disappears when a build is not finished or is destroyed, and shown work raises the value of a wait. The effort heuristic replicates only partly. Annoyance begins when the effort is unexplained, cannot be finished, or excludes people physically. Accessibility guidance says long holds should have an alternative.

### Cited Findings
- Norton, Mochon and Ariely (2012, Journal of Consumer Psychology), "The IKEA effect": in four studies (IKEA boxes, origami, Lego), people valued what they had built more highly. The effect faded when participants built and then destroyed their creations, or failed to complete them - [ABS] [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/12136084)
- Kruger et al. (2004), "The effort heuristic": a poem, paintings and armour were rated higher when people were told more effort went into them, for example a poem said to have taken 18 hours versus 4 - [SNIP] [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effort_heuristic). Two preregistered replications in 2023 (N = 1405) were mixed: one supported Experiment 2 but not Experiment 1, and a revised Experiment 1 affected liking but not monetary value - [SNIP] same source.
- Buell and Norton (2011, Management Science), "The labor illusion": when a website showed the work it was doing (for example, listing each airline as it searched), people valued the service more and could prefer a longer wait to an instant result. Perceived effort produced reciprocity, which mediated the effect - [ABS] [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:57:y:2011:i:9:p:1564-1579). A secondary source says ratings stayed higher even with forced waits of up to a minute - [SNIP] [Atticus Li blog](https://atticusli.com/blog/posts/labor-illusion-operational-transparency-ryan-buell-behavioral-economics/)
- Xbox Accessibility Guideline 107 (Input): a primary input method may be offered but must not be required, and alternatives to holds and combos are recommended. Microsoft's example is holding a trigger for three seconds to open a door, which excludes players who cannot sustain the hold - [ABS] [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/107)
- Before Your Eyes deliberately uses discomfort (keeping eyes open) as the cost that ends a scene - [ABS] [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gone-before-you-blink-the-webcam-driven-gameplay-of-before-your-eyes)
- Weaver and Lewis: guilt did not reduce enjoyment; Bopp et al. 2016: players valued sadness. Negative feeling is not the same as annoyance - [ABS] [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23017118/); [Aalto](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/negative-emotion-positive-experience-emotionally-moving-moments-i/)

### Inferences
- A hold works when the player can see it adding up (distance shrinking, rain thickening, the house getting closer) and when it ends in a finished thing (arriving). That matches both the IKEA completion condition and the labor illusion's "visible work".
- Annoyance on a phone comes from holds that are long, give no sign of progress, punish a lift of the thumb, or exist only to slow the player down. A released finger should never cost progress.
- Effort justification predicts that players value what they struggled for. In a 20 to 30 minute story the safer lever is the emotional effort of a hard choice, not physical endurance.

### Gaps
- No study was found on the hold length at which phone users switch from engagement to irritation. Thresholds have to come from playtests.
- The classic effort-justification study (Aronson and Mills 1959) was not checked in this session.

## 4. Waiting, suspense and uncertainty, especially in messaging (Delivered versus Read, typing indicators, response-time anxiety)

### Takeaway
The feeling of not knowing intensifies whatever emotion is present, good or bad. Suspense needs someone liked, a threat and incomplete certainty. In messaging, a "Read" with no reply produces negative feeling sooner than an unread message, and delays lower liking even when dots are shown. Typing indicators work as "uncertain" signs of presence that build suspense. Arousal rises through the typing phase into the reply, especially after a long wait.

### Cited Findings
- Bar-Anan, Wilson and Gilbert (2009, Emotion 9(1)): in four studies, participants watched film clips while repeating phrases that suggested certainty or uncertainty. The information was held constant; the feeling of uncertainty alone intensified reactions, making unpleasant events more unpleasant and pleasant ones more pleasant - [ABS] [Harvard PDF](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/danielgilbert/files/gilbert_feelinguncertaintyintensifies.pdf)
- Zillmann (1996), quoted in a 2016 study: suspense is an "acute, fearful apprehension about deplorable events that threaten liked protagonists", with high but incomplete certainty that they will happen - [ABS] [Frontiers in Psychology 2016](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01913/full). That study, on news stories, found the opposite of the fiction prediction: a likely good ending went with more suspense and appreciation - same source.
- Maister (1985), "The Psychology of Waiting Lines": uncertain waits feel longer than known, finite ones, unexplained waits feel longer than explained ones, and anxiety makes waits feel longer - [ABS] [davidmaister.com](https://davidmaister.com/articles/5/52/index.html); [Psych Central summary](https://psychcentral.com/blog/8-reasons-why-waiting-in-line-drives-us-crazy)
- Kato et al. (2018, 2020), 213 female college students in Japan: negative emotions arose significantly sooner when waiting for a reply to a message marked as read than to an unread one, and sooner again in people highly dependent on messaging - [ABS] [IJTHI 2020](https://irma-international.org/article/reply-speed-as-nonverbal-cue-in-text-messaging-with-a-read-receipt-display-function/239530/); [IGI 2018](https://www.igi-global.com/article/nobody-read-or-reply-your-messages/198333)
- In a survey of people in long-distance relationships (n = 113), a partner reading a message without replying was linked to anxiety and stress - [SNIP] [Aalto thesis record](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/175570ce-49d9-4d24-bfd9-46927f6f46ce). Hoyle et al. (CHI 2017) found a range of negative emotions around Facebook Messenger's "seen" indicator - [ABS] [Oberlin PDF](https://www.cs.oberlin.edu/~rhoyle/papers/hoyle-chi17.pdf), as summarised in [Iftikhar and Huang](https://momentine.github.io/files/paper.pdf)
- Hwang, Fussell and Khojasteh (2019, PACM HCI): during a reply delay, participants rated partners as less involved, felt more frustrated and liked them less, whether or not a typing indicator was shown - [ABS] [catalog record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........184df5ea3f3b54c14f9242f69e90701a)
- Camerini et al. (2022, Frontiers in Psychology), 65 young adults in a WhatsApp-style lab chat, with replies after 60 s or 420 s: skin conductance (arousal) rose from the typing phase into the reaction phase, mainly in the delayed condition. Heart rate did not change. Self-reports did not differ by delay, but the reply's warmth strongly changed how pleasant the exchange felt - [FULL] [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.840845/full)
- Herrie and Sørensen (2023) describe typing indicators as "uncertain indices" that create attentional presence through unknowability and suspense - [ABS] [PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/HERSWM). Iftikhar and Huang review work showing that typing indicators act as a social cue that raises social presence, and that read receipts and typing indicators kept users attentive and caused significant stress (Shin et al.) - [FULL, pages 1 to 2] [Iftikhar and Huang PDF](https://momentine.github.io/files/paper.pdf)
- Naomi Baron, quoted in an essay, called typing indicators "interpersonally soothing", while the same piece notes that people still leave seen messages for hours or days - [SNIP] [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2016/5/20/16044998/typing-awareness-indicators-and-a-certain-kind-of-chat-app-hell-4df2cd948001)
- Labor illusion (section 3): visible effort during a wait raises the value of the result - [ABS] [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:57:y:2011:i:9:p:1564-1579)

### Inferences
- In messaging, each state signals something different: "Delivered" means it got there, "Read" means they saw it and chose not to answer yet (the worst stretch, per Kato), and dots mean they are answering, with an uncertain content. A wait built on these three states has a ready-made rising curve, and the longest silence belongs after "Read".
- Bar-Anan predicts that uncertainty during the wait will make the relief at "home." larger, as long as the relief actually arrives.
- Hwang's result is a warning: a long delay can turn worry into irritation with the person who is late. The wait has to feel like worry about the kid, not frustration with the kid or the interface. A visible state change (Read, dots) is what keeps it reading as the kid rather than the game.
- Dots that run for a while and then deliver a single word make the reply feel effortful (labor illusion) and leave the player wondering what was typed and deleted. That fits a project called What I Meant, though this is interpretation.

### Gaps
- No study of "Delivered" versus "Read" as separate states was found; Kato compares read with unread.
- The Hwang 2019 sample size and delay lengths were not verified (abstract only).
- No study was found on waiting as an interactive-fiction mechanic, apart from developer examples in earlier notes.

## 5. Parents, teens and check-in messages: why parents ask, how teens feel, how adults reinterpret it, and role reversal with ageing parents

### Takeaway
Parents give teens phones above all to stay reachable, and both sides say it makes them feel safer. Teens also experience the phone as a tether. What parents actually know comes mostly from what the teen chooses to tell, not from tracking. Contact the teen starts goes with closeness, while contact felt as overmonitoring goes with conflict. Worry for ageing parents ("filial anxiety") is theorised as the same attachment system turned around. I found no study of adults later reinterpreting their parents' check-ins.

### Cited Findings
- Pew Research (2010, "Teens and Mobile Phones"): 98% of parents of teens with phones said a major reason was being able to be in touch wherever the teen is. 94% of parents and 93% of teens agreed they felt safer because the teen could always call for help. 94% of teen phone users said phones gave them more freedom because they could reach their parents. 48% of parents used the phone to monitor the child's location, 64% looked at the phone's contents, and 62% had taken it away as punishment. Some teens resent the phone as an electronic tether (no figure given) - [FULL] [Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2010/04/20/teens-and-mobile-phones/). The data predate smartphone-era messaging.
- Pew (2016, parents of 13 to 17 year olds): 48% had looked through their teen's call records or messages and 43% knew the phone password - [SNIP] [InfoDocket summary](https://www.infodocket.com/2016/01/07/parents-teens-and-digital-monitoring-new-survey-findings-from-pew-research/)
- Stattin and Kerr (2000, Child Development), 703 Swedish 14-year-olds: parents' knowledge of their children's whereabouts came mainly from the children volunteering information, not from parents tracking or asking. Child disclosure was the factor most tied to lower delinquency - [ABS] [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/1467-8624.00210). A 2009 replication with a more diverse US sample found knowledge more strongly related to disclosure than to parental questioning - [ABS] [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2682426)
- Weisskirch (2009, 2011), 196 parent-adolescent pairs: calls teens made seeking support went with more communication and closeness. Teen-initiated calls in response to monitoring raised parents' ratings of support, closeness and monitoring. Parent-initiated calls that teens saw as overmonitoring soured relationships, and calls to track schoolwork or made when the parent was upset went with more conflict - [ABS] [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/06/110629171102.htm); [OJP abstract](https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/parenting-cell-phone-parental-monitoring-adolescents-and-family). Correlational self-report.
- Ling and Haddon: the mobile phone works as a kind of "umbilical cord" between parent and child that also allows a cautious independence - [SNIP] [CORE PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/35436404.pdf)
- Location tracking in families supports and threatens parental values at the same time (trust, self-direction, privacy) - [ABS] [Bath, CSCW 2012](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/a-case-study-of-non-adoption-the-values-of-location-tracking-in-t/). In a Belgian study of 147 young people aged 13 to 16, teens negotiated tracking through humour, revoking access and getting around it - [SNIP] [Hasselt PDF](https://documentserver.uhasselt.be/bitstream/1942/49244/1/zmag010.pdf). Teens in Ghosh et al. (CHI 2018) found parental control apps restrictive and invasive - [SNIP] [arXiv paper citing it](https://arxiv.org/html/2204.07749v2)
- Filial anxiety: an adult child's concern about an ageing parent's decline and their own ability to provide care, rooted in Bowlby's attachment theory as anxiety over failing to protect the parent - [ABS] [SAGE Encyclopedia entry](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/the-sage-encyclopedia-of-lifespan-human-development/chpt/filial-anxiety). In 530 Chinese adults aged 26 to 40, filial anxiety was higher than in Western samples, especially about parents' welfare - [ABS] [PMC conference abstract](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10737369/)
- From the parents' side of role reversal, parents control the information they share with their adult children and carry anxiety about ageing and death - [ABS] [Oberlin Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.oberlin.edu/faculty_schol/4180)
- First-time parents (n = 23) described the transition to parenthood as changing their relationships with their own parents and in-laws (10 kinds of turning point) - [SNIP] [Journal of Family Communication DOI](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/15267431.2010.489218) (403 on fetch)

### Inferences
- "Text me when you get home" is the low-cost form of monitoring that the research favours: it asks for a disclosure instead of tracking. The reply ("home.") is the teen's own act. That makes the kid's text, not the parent's waiting, the act of love in the final scene.
- Teens feel the request as both care and tether (Pew, Ling, the location studies). A story that shows the teen's eye-roll first and the parent's wait later lets the player feel both sides, which matches the mirrored structure the story already has.
- The research supports the idea that the worry passes down the generations and turns back on the parents (filial anxiety, attachment theory), but no source tests the "now I understand why mum asked" reinterpretation directly.

### Gaps
- No study was found on how adults later reinterpret their parents' check-in requests, or on whether becoming a parent changes how they read them.
- No current (2020s) Pew figures on parent-teen check-in texting were found; the 2010 data are the closest.
- No research was found on "leave on read" between teens and parents specifically.

## 6. Interaction pacing on phones: meaningful taps versus busywork, how many choices a 20 to 30 minute story can carry, tap-through, and making key lines stick

### Takeaway
There is no published rule for choice density. Designers recommend a series of small two-option choices, with heavy weight saved for a few moments and every choice acknowledged. Telemetry shows players skip less near the climax, so the key lines are partly protected by the story itself. Lines stick through contrast (a change of verb or rhythm) more than through constant friction.

### Cited Findings
- BioWare's Mass Effect 2 telemetry: 15% of conversations were skipped across all players, more in non-critical hub conversations and much less near the climax - [SNIP] [GameBanshee interview](https://www.gamebanshee.com/news/99618-mass-effect-2-statistics-interview.html); [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2010-09-07-half-of-mass-effect-2-players-imported-a-save-and-other-stats.html)
- Hideo Kojima said player data showed many players skipped Death Stranding's cutscenes (no figure given) - [SNIP] [FRVR](https://frvr.com/blog/news/hideo-kojima-says-a-lot-of-people-skipped-death-stranding-cutscenes-but-they-are-part-of-the-game/)
- Failbetter Games: five two-option choices are better than ten options picked five times. Not every choice should be a big deal. A complex situation should be laid out in one passage and the decision made in the next. Choices should be recorded and the world should respond to them now and then, without a combinatorial explosion. Do not tell players what their character feels - [FULL] [Failbetter](https://www.failbettergames.com/narrative-snippets-agency-and-choices)
- Ingold: scenes with one entry and exit, with the emotional interplay inside a scene carrying the player through - [ABS] [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/should-games-have-meaningful-choices-)
- Fendt et al. used six two-option decision points in a short text story, two of them truly branching, and still produced agency similar to full branching where choices were acknowledged - [FULL] [Fendt et al. PDF](https://www.cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/fendt2012achieving.pdf)
- Bopp et al. 2016: lack of agency itself was one trigger of emotionally moving moments - [ABS] [Aalto](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/negative-emotion-positive-experience-emotionally-moving-moments-i/)
- Before Your Eyes keeps most scenes very short - [ABS] [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gone-before-you-blink-the-webcam-driven-gameplay-of-before-your-eyes)
- Mixed-list enactment (Steffens et al. 2015): performed actions stand out against observed ones - [ABS] [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01907/full)
- Iten et al.: the choices that matter are moral, social and consequential - [ABS] [researchr](https://researchr.org/publication/ItenSO18)

### Inferences
- For a 20 to 30 minute phone story, four to six real choices plus a few physical moments is in line with the Fendt design and Failbetter's advice. Every other input is pacing and should not pose as a choice.
- Tap-to-read is fine as the base rhythm, since players skip less where the story matters (ME2). Forced reading time is best kept for a few lines and signalled by a change in the interface (a different verb, input pausing), not used everywhere.
- A moment with no agency can be a peak (Bopp, That Dragon Cancer). The final wait does not need a choice to land.

### Gaps
- No published research gives a choice count per minute for short mobile narrative, or data on tap-through rates in web interactive fiction.
- No study was found on forced minimum reading time and recall in games; the recommendation rests on the contrast and enactment findings.

## 7. Ethics: avoiding manipulation and harm for players who lost parents, children or pets; content notes; gentle design for grieving players

### Takeaway
Trigger warnings do not reduce distress after the content, do not reduce avoidance, and do raise anticipatory anxiety. Bereaved players can find meaning in loss-themed games, but sudden or violent deaths push them away, and they need control of pace and permission to stop. The ethical line in the design literature is between letting the player feel the weight of their own choices and forcing an act and then blaming them for it.

### Cited Findings
- Bellet, Jones and McNally (2018, Journal of Behavior Therapy and Experimental Psychiatry): online participants given trigger warnings before literary passages rated themselves and others as more emotionally vulnerable after trauma. Warnings raised anxiety in response to the passages only in people who believed words can cause harm. The sample was non-traumatized and the effects small - [SNIP] [McNally lab PDF](https://www.mcnallylab.com/wp-content/uploads/Bellet-Jones-McNally-2018-Journal-of-Behavior-Therapy-and-Experimental-Psychiatry.pdf); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/2018/08/study-finds-evidence-that-trigger-warnings-can-be-psychologically-harmful-52007)
- Bridgland, Jones and Bellet (2023/2024, Clinical Psychological Science) meta-analysis of 12 studies: warnings raised anticipatory anxiety (5 studies), did not change distress, fear or anxiety after viewing (9 studies), and did not reduce engagement with the content (sometimes increasing it) - [ABS] [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/2023-october-content-warnings-distress.html); [Flinders record](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-the-efficacy-of-trigger-warnings-content-warni/)
- A 2020 study with trauma survivors (Jones, Bellet and McNally, "Helping or Harming?") is often cited for the same pattern. A search summary describes a 2020 replication with a trivially small increase in anxiety in which the vulnerability effects did not replicate. I could not confirm which 2020 paper that summary refers to - [SNIP] [Gwern-hosted PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/2020-jones.pdf)
- Bereaved adults playing Spiritfarer and Bear's Restaurant (12 adults, diaries and interviews, Frontiers in Psychology 2023): seven themes, including recalling memories, avoiding engagement with the pain (sudden or violent deaths caused shock and distancing), recognising positive emotions and gratitude toward living loved ones, and planning a better future. People with clinical grief were excluded, and the authors warn that such games may trigger rumination and are not treatments - [FULL] [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10426378/)
- Eum et al. (CHI 2021), 6 bereaved Spiritfarer players: their grief followed the dual process model, oscillating between loss and restoration, and some reappraised their loss. How they engaged depended on their prior loss and on whether they focused on themselves or on the game - [ABS] [KAIST repository](https://koasas.kaist.ac.kr/handle/10203/308290)
- That Dragon, Cancer: Ryan Green said sharing their story gave other people permission to share theirs - [SNIP] [CNN Money](https://money.cnn.com/2016/06/16/technology/that-dragon-cancer-ryan-green/index.html)
- Players resent being forced into an act and then blamed for it (Spec Ops commentary) - [SNIP] [The Artifice](https://the-artifice.com/white-phosphorus-in-spec-ops/). People disown agency over bad outcomes (Yoshie and Haggard) - [ABS] [UCL Discovery](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1425000)
- Guilt from virtual acts fades with repetition (Grizzard 2016) - [ABS] [UB Now](https://www.buffalo.edu/ubnow/stories/2016/04/grizzard-guilt-video-games.html)
- Holds must have an alternative for players who cannot sustain them (XAG 107) - [ABS] [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/107)

### Inferences
- An upfront warning screen would cost every player some dread and spoil the ending, while protecting no one measurably. A specific, opt-in note gives control to those who want it without adding anticipatory anxiety for everyone else. The opt-in form is inference: none of the trials tested opt-in notes.
- For grieving players, the risks in this story are the ageing or absent parent, grandma, and a final wait that could read as "the child did not make it". The bereaved-player study says sudden and violent loss is what drives people away. The story has none, so the main risk is the final wait. It must always end in "home.", and a player who needs to know that should be able to find out.
- Manipulation, in practice, means guilt the player could not avoid. Mirroring (showing the other side of what the player did) gives the player material to judge themselves, and the story never has to judge them.

### Gaps
- No experiment was found on opt-in or menu-placed content notes versus upfront warnings.
- No study of players who lost a child or a parent playing parent-child stories was found; the bereaved-player studies are small (6 and 12 people) and excluded clinical grief.
- No research-based definition of "manipulative" emotional design in games was found.

## What this means for "Text me when you get home"

Each change names the finding it rests on and how to test it. Timings are starting guesses to tune in playtests, not research values.

1. **Build the final wait as three rising states, with the longest silence after "Read".** Delivered (short), then Read with nothing moving (longest), then typing dots, then the dots stop once and start again, then "home." Finding: negative feeling arrives sooner after "Read" than after an unread message (Kato); the feeling of uncertainty intensifies emotion (Bar-Anan); dots are uncertain signs that build suspense (Herrie and Sørensen); arousal climbs through typing into the reply after a delay (Camerini). Test: 5 or more first-time players narrate aloud during the wait. Success means they name the kid or a worry ("why isn't she answering") at the "Read" stage. Starting values: Delivered 1.5 s, Read 6 s, dots 3 s, pause 2 s, dots 2 s.
2. **Keep the wait bounded, always end it with "home.", and keep the state visibly changing.** Finding: delay turns into frustration and lower liking of the partner even with dots (Hwang); unexplained, uncertain waits feel longest (Maister); bereaved parents are the players most at risk (bereaved-player studies). Test: if any player taps repeatedly, asks "is it stuck?" or puts the phone down, the wait has passed from worry into interface doubt. Shorten the "Read" stage first.
3. **During the wait, tapping does nothing.** Optionally, holding a finger on the screen keeps the phone lit, the way a parent keeps checking. Finding: lack of agency is itself a trigger of moving moments (Bopp 2016; That Dragon Cancer's collapsed choices); the earlier hold meant "keep going home", and reusing it with a changed meaning is the Brothers or Before Your Eyes pattern. Test: count how many players try the old hold without being told. If over half do and report it as meaningful, keep it; if they read it as a bug, drop it.
4. **A/B test a single temptation during the wait: a "?" or "call" option.** Version A has no option. In version B the option can be used once; whatever the player does, the kid's "home." still arrives. Finding: what parents know comes from the child's disclosure, not checking (Stattin and Kerr); contact felt as overmonitoring sours relationships (Weisskirch); resisting an available action is itself a performed act (Before Your Eyes). Test: compare how strongly "home." lands (1 to 7 rating plus one sentence) between versions, and log how many B players hold back.
5. **Make mom's "leave on read" a mirror, not a punishment.** If the player leaves mom on read, show "Read" under her message in the teen's thread, then let that same word, "Read", be the one the player stares at on their own message at the end. The kid answers in both branches. Finding: people disown bad outcomes (Yoshie and Haggard) and resent forced blame (Spec Ops commentary), while a later reference is what separates real branching from mere acknowledgement (Fendt, the "would have been different" item). Test: after the ending, ask players who left mom on read "Did the ending remind you of anything you did earlier?" At least half should name mom unprompted. If fewer do, make the visual echo more exact (same bubble, same "Read" label).
6. **Let the player type the two loaded texts ("hey" to the best friend, "ok" to mom) letter by letter, with backspace. Keep auto-typing for every other reply.** Finding: enactment strengthens memory, mainly through planning the action, and shows most when performed actions are mixed with watched ones (Roberts et al.; Steffens et al.); Florence wanted events acted out, not dialogue tapped through (Wong). Test: the next day, ask players which texts they sent. The two hand-typed ones should be recalled more often than auto-typed lines from similar positions.
7. **For "hey", allow typing, deleting and closing without sending, and acknowledge the choice not to send.** If the player sends, show "Delivered" and hold the friend's answer back for a while. Finding: uncertainty intensifies emotion (Bar-Anan); choices need an immediate acknowledgement and a later reference (Fendt); a social, consequential choice raises appreciation (Iten). Test: log how many players delete at least once before deciding. Ask both senders and non-senders whether the moment felt like theirs (1 to 7).
8. **Tie each hold to visible progress, a meaningful release and a finished arrival.** On the bike, the hold carries the player away and dad grows smaller; releasing makes the bike coast and the view glance back, with no lost progress. On the walk, the hold walks and releasing leaves the player standing in the rain. Both holds end in arriving somewhere. Finding: the IKEA effect needs completion (Norton et al.); visible work raises value (labor illusion); planning the action, including when to stop, carries most of the enactment benefit (Roberts et al.). Test: log releases per hold. More than two releases per hold, or any complaint about thumb strain, means the hold is too long.
9. **Offer an alternative to every hold (tap to toggle, or "keep walking" after a short hold).** Finding: XAG 107 says holds should have alternatives, because sustained holds exclude some players. Test: complete the story using only taps.
10. **Every one of the three "keep close" people must come back by name at least once, and grandma or lake must be called back later in both branches.** Finding: acknowledgement alone did not give the "story would have been different" feeling, and later references did (Fendt); record choices and respond now and then (Failbetter). Test: audit the script so that every option set has (a) a different next line and (b), for the major choices, a later callback. After play, ask "Did your picks change anything?" Most players should say yes.
11. **Do not punish the lake choice with a disaster.** Let its cost be a small, foreseeable miss, such as a call that goes unanswered or a detail grandma mentions that the player was not there for. Finding: bad outcomes lower felt agency (Yoshie and Haggard); most players pick the kind option anyway (Telltale data, Weaver and Lewis), so the lake branch is where the guilt lives and it must feel owned, not imposed. Test: ask lake choosers whether they felt "blamed by the story" or "sorry myself". Aim for "sorry myself".
12. **Cap the story at four to six real choices and remove any option set that only looks like a choice.** If two reply options lead to the same next line with no acknowledgement, cut one and make it a single auto-typed reply. Finding: minimal-feedback choices gave the lowest agency (Fendt, Story 3); five two-option choices beat bigger menus, and not every choice should be big (Failbetter); in a short story people replay and compare, which exposes the illusion. Test: replay twice, choosing differently, and list any moment where the second run feels identical.
13. **Protect the three to five key lines with a change of input, not general slowness.** Before a key line, briefly pause input or switch from tap to hold or type, and let fast tappers move freely everywhere else. Finding: players skip less near the climax (Mass Effect 2 telemetry); the contrast between modes makes performed moments stand out (Steffens et al.). Test: the next day, ask players to quote any line. Key lines should dominate what they recall.
14. **Use one vibration, on Android only, when "home." arrives, and none anywhere else.** Finding: touch adds arousal to emotional signals (Salminen et al., small samples); a single use keeps the contrast. iOS Safari does not support web vibration [GK], so the moment must work without it. Test: A/B on Android players, rating how real the arrival felt.
15. **No warning screen up front. Put an opt-in "before you play" line on the menu card that names the themes plainly (a parent getting older, an old friendship, waiting for your child), with a second tap that says the story ends with the kid home safe.** Finding: warnings raise anticipatory anxiety without reducing distress or avoidance (Bridgland et al. meta-analysis; Bellet et al.); bereaved players need control and are driven away by sudden loss (bereaved-player study). Test: show the menu to two or three players who have lost a parent or a pet. Ask whether the note helped, spoiled anything or worried them.
16. **The player can always stop and resume, and nothing is ever timed against them.** Finding: low-pressure pacing and permission to disengage supported bereaved players (Frontiers 2023, Eum et al.). Test: close the tab mid-scene, including during a hold and during the final wait, reopen, and check that the story resumes at the same beat.
17. **Optional, weaker evidence: one quiet beat where the adult player checks on their now-older mom, or sees her old "text me when you get home" in the thread.** Finding: filial anxiety is the same attachment worry turned around (SAGE entry; Chinese adult-children study); parents manage what they tell their adult children (Oberlin abstract). Test: if playtesters describe the story as "a circle" without prompting, it works; if the beat adds length without comment, cut it.
