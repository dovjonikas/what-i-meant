# What pulls a reader in and makes a story feel like their own life: transportation, identification, self-reference, nostalgia, autobiographical memory

Scope note: the research ran on WebSearch and WebFetch only, within a budget of about 18 calls. Most papers were reached through abstracts, repository pages, press releases or secondary summaries, not full texts. Each finding below says how it was seen. Two full texts could not be read: the van Laer et al. (2014) PDF came back as binary, and the Wildschut et al. (2006) PDF came back empty. Their exact effect sizes and trigger percentages are therefore listed under Gaps and not filled in from memory.

## 1. Narrative transportation: what raises it, what breaks it, and how interactivity and person affect it

### Takeaway
Across the largest meta-analysis, transportation goes up with identifiable characters, a plot that is easy to picture, events that feel possible and plausible, and a receiver who is paying full attention and already knows the territory. It goes down when something makes readers aware of themselves as readers. Direct evidence on interactivity is still mostly theory. Pronoun evidence favours first and second person (an actor's view) over third person (an observer's view) for immersion and imagery.

### Cited Findings
- The Extended Transportation-Imagery Model meta-analysis (van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti, Wetzels 2014, Journal of Consumer Research 40(5): 797-817) pools 132 effect sizes from 76 published and unpublished articles - [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/). One summary puts the pooled sample at over 21,000 participants - [OnFiction research bulletin](https://www.onfiction.ca/2014/02/research-bulletin-summarizing-over.html).
- Story-side antecedents that reliably raised transportation: easily identifiable characters; a plot that evokes rich mental imagery ("imaginable plot"); and verisimilitude, meaning events that seem both possible and plausible - [OnFiction summary of van Laer et al.](https://www.onfiction.ca/2014/02/research-bulletin-summarizing-over.html) (secondary summary; effect sizes not reported there).
- Receiver-side antecedents: familiarity with the story's genre or territory, attention paid to the story, trait transportability, higher education, and being female - [OnFiction summary](https://www.onfiction.ca/2014/02/research-bulletin-summarizing-over.html).
- Green (2004, Discourse Processes 38(2): 247-266), N = 152 undergraduates reading a story about a gay man at a fraternity reunion: transportation correlated positively with perceived realism, and readers with prior knowledge or experience relevant to the story's themes (gay friends or family, knowing about fraternities) were more transported - [Narrative Studies bibliography, abstract](https://narrativestudies.lib.unb.ca/bibcite/reference/17215) (abstract only).
- Green (2004): the link between transportation and story-consistent beliefs held both for readers with and without relevant prior experience - [abstract](https://narrativestudies.lib.unb.ca/bibcite/reference/17215).
- Breaking it through self-awareness: in Kaufman and Libby's (2012, JPSP) experience-taking studies, reminders of one's own identity blocked readers from taking on a character's experience; most students who read in a cubicle with a mirror could not get into the story - [ScienceDaily press release](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/05/120507131948.htm) (press coverage, full paper not read).
- Pronouns: Hartung, Burke, Hagoort and Willems (2016, PLOS ONE 11(5): e0154732) found first-person stories produced higher immersion than third-person versions, mainly in the transportation and mental imagery subscales. Electrodermal arousal was only tentatively higher for third person, and appreciation ratings did not change - [PMC4883771](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4883771).
- The same paper's background: single-sentence studies show third-person pronouns build a mental model from an observer's perspective, while second-person pronouns support an actor's perspective - [PMC4883771](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4883771) (as reported in the abstract; the underlying sentence studies were not read).
- Interactivity: Green and Jenkins (2014, Journal of Communication 64(3): 479-500) is a theoretical review. It names user control and a looser narrative structure as the defining features of interactive narratives and predicts these could shape transportation, identification and perceived realism. It is a framework, not a test - [University at Buffalo research portal](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/interactive-narratives-processes-and-outcomes-in-user-directed-st/).
- A 2020 Games for Health Journal randomized study compared linear and interactive versions of one narrative. Need for cognition predicted transportation in one study and identification in the other, and transportability's effect on perceived realism did not replicate - [PubMed mirror listing, 31934790](https://api-pubmed-ncbi.laibokeji.com/31934790) (seen only in a search snippet; the direction of any linear vs interactive difference was not visible).

### Inferences
- The three story-side levers (identifiable "you", picturable scenes, plausibility) map directly onto this story. Plausibility is the easiest one to break by accident, through a detail that does not fit the player's world or the story's own timeline.
- "Attention" as an antecedent, together with the mirror result, suggests that anything that turns the player into an observer of themselves breaks transportation: instructions, a question about their own life, a meta view. The "life lines" view is an observer's, bird's-eye device. That is good for reflection after the story and risky during it.
- Second-person "you" is, on the sentence-level evidence, an actor's perspective. That is the mode Hartung found best for imagery and immersion. The choice of "you" is supported, but nothing guarantees it beats first person.

### Gaps
- The exact van Laer effect sizes (r values for each antecedent, the consequences, moderators such as commercial vs non-commercial or print vs film) could not be read; the PDF came back as binary. Check Tables 2 and 3 of the original.
- I found no source in this session that directly measures interruptions or embedded instructions breaking transportation. The claim is plausible and is often attributed to Green and Brock (2000), but it is unverified here.
- No published study was found that measures transportation in a second-person condition directly (see section 6).
- No study was found on interactive stories made of taps that do not change the plot, which is what this story is. Green and Jenkins frame interactivity as user control over the story, which this story mostly does not give.

## 2. Identification and self-referential processing: when readers map a story onto their own life, and specific versus generic detail

### Takeaway
Readers get "remindings" of their own past from literary text about as often as from expository text. Literary text, though, brings back memories in which the reader acted, not just received. Those remindings cluster early in a text and come from descriptive passages, not from action or dialogue. Prior relevant experience raises transportation. Identifying with a "you" or character first and learning about differences later keeps identification intact. No study was found that directly compares a specific sensory detail with a generic one as a cue for readers' own memories.

### Cited Findings
- Seilman and Larsen (Poetics 18, 1989, "Personal resonance to literature"): 20 psychology students marked the text whenever they thought of something they had experienced (self-probed retrospection) and were interviewed afterward. A short story and an expository text of equal length elicited a similar number of remindings - [Miall reading page summarizing Seilman and Larsen 1989](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/LiteraryReading/Readings/Seilman_Larsen_1989.htm).
- The literary text produced about twice as many memories in which the reader had been an active participant (actor) as the expository text did - [same page](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/LiteraryReading/Readings/Seilman_Larsen_1989.htm); also [Seilman and Larsen 1989 PDF listing](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/Cognitive/Readings/Seilman_Larsen_1989.pdf).
- Remindings were more frequent at the beginning of the texts, which the authors linked to readers building a representation of the text's world - [same page](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/LiteraryReading/Readings/Seilman_Larsen_1989.htm).
- Purely descriptive passages elicited many remindings; passages about action and communication elicited almost none - [same page](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/LiteraryReading/Readings/Seilman_Larsen_1989.htm).
- A later study in this line (European Journal of Social Psychology, DOI 10.1002/ejsp.2420200505, likely Larsen and Laszlo 1990) found that cultural proximity strongly influenced how readers located and evaluated the story, and the nature of their remindings - [Zendy listing](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/ejsp.2420200505) (seen only in a search snippet; the authors are not confirmed by the page).
- Prior knowledge or experience relevant to a story's themes raised transportation (Green 2004) - [abstract](https://narrativestudies.lib.unb.ca/bibcite/reference/17215).
- Experience-taking (Kaufman and Libby 2012): readers effectively swap themselves out for a character, unlike perspective-taking, which keeps the reader's identity in place. It happens spontaneously and without awareness - [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/05/120507131948.htm). Shared group membership and first-person narration increased it - [The Dartmouth](https://www.thedartmouth.com/article/2012/05/study-links-literature-and-behavior).
- Readers who identified with a character before learning the character's sexual orientation or race held more favourable views of that group and fewer stereotypes - [Ohio State News](https://news.osu.edu/losing-yourself-in-a-fictional-character-can-affect-your-real-life/). Behavioural effects (for example, voting) lasted up to a week - [The Dartmouth](https://www.thedartmouth.com/article/2012/05/study-links-literature-and-behavior) (press coverage only).

### Inferences
- Remindings come from description and from the opening. So the first scenes (birth, grandma's garden, jam) carry the most weight for pulling the player's own memories in, and they should be descriptive rather than eventful.
- Literary text produces "I was there, I did it" memories. Second-person action lines ("you run", "you hold the jar") may strengthen that further. This is untested, but consistent with the actor-perspective finding in section 1.
- On specific versus generic: no direct test was found. The indirect evidence (descriptive passages trigger remindings; prior familiarity raises transportation; odour cues reach old, vivid memories, see section 5) points to concrete sensory detail that many people share (a sticky jar lid, a gate latch, the smell of cut grass). The risk is identity-specific detail that excludes people (a brand, a city, a named sibling). A useful working rule, labelled as inference: be specific in the senses, generic in identity.
- From Kaufman and Libby's order effect: let the player become "you" before the story states anything that might not match their life.

### Gaps
- No experiment was found that compares a highly specific detail (for example, dill and floor polish) with a generic one ("the kitchen smelled nice") on the number or vividness of readers' own memories. This is the most important open question for the author and could be tested directly (see the final section).
- Exact counts from Seilman and Larsen (number of remindings, actor/observer split, recent vs old memories, reader age effects) were not on the page reached.
- Larsen and Seilman 1988 (Text 8(4): 411-430, "Personal remindings while reading literature") was seen only as a listing; its findings were not read.

## 3. Nostalgia: triggers, content, the bittersweet mix, effects, and evoking it in strangers

### Takeaway
Nostalgic memories typically put the self at the centre with close others or at momentous events. They contain more positive than negative feeling and often show a redemption arc, something bad later made good. Negative mood and loneliness trigger nostalgia, and nostalgia then raises social connectedness, self-regard, positive feeling and a sense of meaning. People in 18 countries share the same core idea of what nostalgia is, so it can be evoked in strangers if the story uses its prototypical ingredients rather than one culture's props.

### Cited Findings
- Wildschut, Sedikides, Arndt and Routledge (2006, JPSP 91(5): 975-993), seven studies. Content: nostalgic descriptions typically featured the self as protagonist interacting with close others (for example, friends) or in momentous events (for example, weddings) - [Southampton ePrints 40445](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/40445) (abstract).
- The same descriptions contained more expressions of positive than negative affect and often showed negative life scenes redeemed by later triumphs (redemption sequences) - [Southampton ePrints 40445](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/40445).
- Triggers: nostalgia arose in response to negative mood and to the discrete state of loneliness - [Southampton ePrints 40445](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/40445).
- Functions: nostalgia bolstered social bonds, increased positive self-regard and generated positive affect - [Southampton ePrints 40445](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/40445).
- A later review (Sedikides, Wildschut, Arndt, Routledge 2008, Current Directions in Psychological Science) adds that nostalgia alleviates existential threat - [Southampton PDF](https://southampton.ac.uk/~crsi/Sedikides%20Wildschut%20Arndt%20%20Routledge%202008%20CDir.pdf) (seen via search summary).
- Pancultural nostalgia (Hepper et al. 2014, Emotion 14(4): 733-747): students in 18 countries on five continents (N = 1,704) rated how prototypical 35 features of nostalgia are. Rankings were broadly consistent and the central features stood out in every country. The factor structure matched in all samples except the African ones, and the strength of the patterns varied a little between groups of countries - [Utrecht University research portal](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/pancultural-nostalgia-prototypical-conceptions-across-cultures/).
- Scent-evoked nostalgia exists as its own research line (Reid, Green, Wildschut and Sedikides 2015, Memory) - [Southampton PDF listing](https://southampton.ac.uk/~crsi/Reid,%20Green,%20Wildschut,%20&%20Sedikides,%202015,%20Memory.pdf) (seen only as a title in search results; findings not read).

### Inferences
- The story's frame (old, alone, waiting, a little afraid) is close to a textbook nostalgia trigger: low mood plus loneliness. Each return to the waiting frame should "release" a memory. That is the natural mechanism, so the frame should come back between the life chapters rather than appear only at the start and end.
- The final reply works as a redemption sequence for the whole life. The teenage "text me when you get home" left on read is the negative scene, and the grown kid's reply is the redemption. The research says redemption is the typical shape of nostalgic narratives, so this echo is the story's strongest structural asset and should be made unmistakable, but without being spelled out.
- To evoke nostalgia in strangers, use the prototypical ingredients the research shows are shared (close others, momentous events, the self as protagonist, warmth with loss), not culture-bound props. Concrete props (jam jars) still work as long as they stand for a close other (grandma) rather than for a place or a nation.
- The bittersweet mix is in the content data: more positive than negative, but negative present. A scene that is purely sad or purely sweet is less nostalgic than one that holds both.

### Gaps
- The exact percentages from Wildschut et al. 2006 (objects of nostalgia, frequency of redemption vs contamination sequences, trigger categories such as sensory inputs, social interaction, tangibles and anniversaries) could not be read; the PDF fetch returned empty. These numbers are left out rather than filled in from memory.
- Batcho's nostalgia inventory work was not reached in this session.
- No source was found on music as a nostalgia trigger within budget (music chills were covered by earlier research and excluded).
- The 35 features in Hepper et al. and their ranking were not visible in the abstract.

## 4. Autobiographical memory: the reminiscence bump, cultural life scripts, and which of the story's moments are universal

### Takeaway
People expect, and best remember, a small set of culturally scripted positive transitions concentrated between about 15 and 30: falling in love, leaving home, first job, marriage, children. Negative events (parents' deaths, illness) are expected later and with no fixed timing. The bump in memory holds for positive events, not negative ones. The story's partner, kid, leaving home and parents dying are script events and the most likely to land for anyone. The specific childhood props are less certain across cultures.

### Cited Findings
- Berntsen and Rubin (2004, Memory and Cognition): life scripts are culturally shared representations of the timing of major transitional life events. In a Danish survey of 1,485 people, only the number of positive events increased between ages 15 and 30 - [Springer PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758%2FBF03193303.pdf) (seen via search summary).
- Rubin and Berntsen (2003): there is a reminiscence bump for positive but not for negative events; scripts are thought to help preserve highly positive memories but not highly negative ones - [Springer PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758%2FBF03193303.pdf); also restated in [PMC3972131](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3972131).
- When students listed events for a newborn's life, they mostly named normative events (graduation, marriage, retirement), and most were positive. Positive events were expected in early adulthood; negative events could come at any time - [Springer PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758%2FBF03193303.pdf) (search summary).
- A Dutch life-script study (n = 595; [PMC3972131](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3972131), authors not confirmed in my fetch, possibly Janssen and Rubin) gives the most frequent events, with the share of people naming each and the mean expected age:
  - having children: 72.1%, 28.9 years
  - begin school: 60.5%, 4.5
  - marriage: 53.1%, 27.1
  - parents' death: 43.5%, 49.7
  - fall in love: 40.8%, 13.8
  - first full-time job: 32.4%, 22.2
  - leaving home: 19.0%
  - high school: 18.2%
  - high school graduation: 16.6%
- In the same study, positive events were 73.3% of nominations. Of these, 55.4% were expected between ages 16 and 30, 20.0% in the first six years, and only 8.4% after 30. About 51.7% of negative and neutral events were expected at 31 or later. Events expected before 36 were on average positive; events expected after 35 were on average negative - [PMC3972131](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3972131).
- Positive script events have tight expected ages (SD about 2.7 years); negative ones are spread out (SD about 10.1). The most negative events were death of a spouse (-2.33), serious illness (-2.00) and parents' death (-1.97) - [PMC3972131](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3972131).
- Cross-culture: Turkish data (Erdogan et al. 2008) show the same pattern, with positive events in the third decade and negative events spread across life. Culture-specific items appear (confirmation in Danish scripts, circumcision in Turkish ones), while religious events were rarely named by the Dutch - [PMC3972131](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3972131).
- Most cultures lack emotionally negative events with known expected dates and sequencing - [PMC3972131](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3972131).
- Competing accounts of the bump include novelty, identity formation and general memory enhancement in adolescence. One study found the bump held relatively fewer novel, emotional or important events, and the general-enhancement account has not been tested directly - [search summary of Berntsen and Rubin related literature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3972131) (search summary, contested area).

### Inferences
- Likely to land for almost any adult player, because they are script events: the kid (having children, the top script event), the partner (falling in love, marriage), parents ageing and dying (the most negative expected event, at about 50), leaving home (the first night alone in a city).
- Universal in structure but not as a script event: grandparents (a close other in early childhood, the odour-memory decade); the dog's death at 16 (pet loss is common but not universal); the school best friend drifting away (very common, but not named in scripts).
- Culturally or generationally narrow: grandma's garden and jars of jam (rural or Eastern European flavour; the close other generalises, the props may not); dad running behind the bike (common where kids learn to cycle, not everywhere); "text me when you get home" as a teenager (only for people whose teens had mobile phones, roughly born after the late 1980s; older players had landlines or nothing); the online friend (generational).
- Positive script events have tight expected ages, so the story's positive milestones should sit near the expected ages: love in the early to mid teens, leaving home and first job in the early twenties, partner and child in the late twenties. Off-script timing reads as less lifelike. That is inference from verisimilitude plus script data, not a tested story effect.
- The reminiscence bump is for positive events. So the 15 to 30 stretch of the story should be mostly warm (first night alone, the online friend, the partner). The dog dying at 16 sits inside the bump as a negative event: it will land through attachment, not through the script.

### Gaps
- No cross-cultural source was found listing which concrete childhood moments (first bike, first pet, grandparents, a friend moving away) are shared across cultures. The scripts studied focus on transitions, and the only childhood transition they name often is "begin school".
- The full Berntsen and Rubin 2004 Danish event list with percentages was not read.

## 5. Sensory and concrete detail: smells, sounds, textures as memory cues, and what this means for text, sound and pictures

### Takeaway
Odour-cued autobiographical memories are older (clustered in the first decade of life), more emotional, more vivid and rarer than memories cued by words or pictures. Those peak in the teens and early adulthood. A text story cannot deliver smells, but it can name them for its earliest scenes and use sound and picture cues for the later ones. Whether a written smell word works like a real odour is unknown.

### Cited Findings
- Larsson, Willander and colleagues (2014 review, Frontiers in Psychology) sum up odour-evoked autobiographical memories with the acronym LOVER: Limbic, Old, Vivid, Emotional, Rare - [Frontiers in Psychology 2014.00312](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00312/full); [PMC3990043](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3990043/).
- Odour-cued memories cluster in the first decade of life (under 10 years), which is distinct from the typical 10 to 30 reminiscence bump seen with verbal cues - [Frontiers review](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00312/full).
- In the underlying study of 93 older adults given word, picture or odour cues, most odour-cued memories came from the first decade, while word- and picture-cued memories peaked at 11 to 20 years - [Frontiers review summarizing Willander and Larsson](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00312/full).
- Descriptive passages elicit readers' own remindings much more than action and dialogue do (Seilman and Larsen 1989) - [Miall page](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/LiteraryReading/Readings/Seilman_Larsen_1989.htm).
- An imaginable plot, one that evokes rich mental imagery, raises transportation (van Laer et al. 2014) - [OnFiction summary](https://www.onfiction.ca/2014/02/research-bulletin-summarizing-over.html).

### Inferences
- Match the cue type to the age in the story. Childhood scenes (birth to about 10: grandma's garden, jam, the bike, the dog as a puppy) are where smell cues have the strongest pull on real memories, so name a smell there. Teen and young-adult scenes (the dog's death at 16, mom's text, the first city night, the online friend, meeting the partner) sit where word and picture cues peak: use a visual object, an on-screen message, or a sound.
- Sound design without voice can do some of the work odour cannot: small diegetic sounds attached to close others (a gate latch, a bike chain, a jar lid popping, a phone vibration). That is inference; no study here tested sound cues against odour cues.
- Pictures: a faceless style leaves room for projection. Objects (a jar, a gate, a phone screen) can be drawn concretely without fixing anyone's identity.

### Gaps
- No study was found on whether reading a smell word (for example, "dill") evokes memories as strongly as smelling the odour. The LOVER pattern is for real odours.
- No source was reached on sound cues versus odour cues for autobiographical memory.
- The emotionality and vividness findings are cited from the 2014 review, not the original 2009 data.

## 6. Second person and the faceless "you": identification, and readers whose life differs from the "you"

### Takeaway
There is little direct experimental work on second-person narration. The best direct comparison found no difference between first and second person in identification, but present tense beat past tense. Sentence-level work places "you" in an actor's view. For players whose life differs from the "you", the most useful evidence is indirect: identify first, learn differences later (Kaufman and Libby), and avoid anything that makes readers self-conscious.

### Cited Findings
- Macrae (Diegesis), an experiment with four variants of a short text: no significant difference between first- and second-person narration in readers' conceptual identification with the narrator or focaliser, while present tense elicited more identification than past tense - [Diegesis article](https://www.diegesis.uni-wuppertal.de/index.php/diegesis/article/download/214/294?inline=1) (seen via search summary).
- Second-person pronouns support an actor's perspective in sentence-level studies, third person an observer's - [Hartung et al. 2016, PMC4883771](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4883771).
- Wimmer et al. (2021, Frontiers in Communication) compared first person, internally focalised third person and externally focalised third person: perspective had no effect on social and moral cognition, and transportation and identification did not mediate any effect. There was no second-person condition - [Frontiers in Communication](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2020.611935/full).
- A Dutch student thesis on persuasive health narratives in first, second or third person found stronger intentions from second person among female than among male participants - [Radboud thesis repository](https://theses.ubn.ru.nl/items/c9a672f8-2e25-4c3b-a309-bac749b3f89c) (thesis, not peer-reviewed; health persuasion context).
- Being female was a receiver antecedent of transportation in the van Laer meta-analysis - [OnFiction summary](https://www.onfiction.ca/2014/02/research-bulletin-summarizing-over.html).
- Experience-taking increases with shared group membership and is blocked by self-reminders (the mirror study). Identifying before learning a difference (the character's orientation or race) kept the identification and shifted attitudes - [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/05/120507131948.htm); [Ohio State News](https://news.osu.edu/losing-yourself-in-a-fictional-character-can-affect-your-real-life/).

### Inferences
- Present tense matters more than "you" against "I". Keep every "you" line in the present, including the memories.
- For players without a pet, siblings, kids, or with divorced or absent parents: the order effect suggests that a mismatch revealed after the player has stepped in costs less than a mismatch at the start. Open with things almost everyone had: a body, a smell, a close adult, a place. Hold back the details that exclude.
- A "you" can be specific without being demanding if the line describes a sensation or an action rather than a fact about the player's biography. "You smell the jam" is easier to accept than "your grandma, who lived in the village". This is inference, consistent with the description-triggers-remindings finding.
- The "life lines" view shows the life from the outside, as an observer. If it is reachable mid-story, it acts like the mirror: a reminder that this is a constructed life being looked at.

### Gaps
- No study was found that measures how readers handle a second-person "you" whose biography contradicts theirs (no siblings, no kids, a parent absent), or whether such contradictions reduce transportation. This is the second key open question and is testable (see below).
- No published study was found that measures narrative transportation (the Green and Brock scale) in a second-person condition. The literary-theory side of "you" (addressee and protagonist at once, in Macrae's article) was seen only in a snippet.
- The Kaufman and Libby details come from press releases; effect sizes and the exact first-person manipulation were not read.

## What this means for Text me when you get home

Each change is tied to a finding above and written so it can be checked or tested. Where a change rests on inference rather than a direct result, it says so.

1. **Make the opening scenes descriptive, not eventful.** Readers' own remindings cluster at the start of a text and come from descriptive passages, almost never from action or dialogue (Seilman and Larsen 1989, section 2). Check: in birth and grandma's garden, at least two of every three lines are sensory description (what is seen, smelled, felt), and no line is dialogue.
2. **Name one smell in every scene before age 10, and none after age 12 unless it is a callback.** Odour memories cluster in the first decade and are the most vivid and emotional (LOVER, section 5). After the teens, word and picture cues peak instead. Check: a scene-by-scene list showing one smell line in each childhood scene (garden, jam, the bike evening, the puppy) and a visual or sound cue as the anchor in each later scene.
3. **Be specific in the senses, generic in identity.** Concrete shared sensations raise imagery and remindings, while identity-bound details (a brand, a city, a named sibling, a religion) exclude players (sections 2 and 6; inference from indirect evidence). Check: scan every line for proper nouns, place names and family facts; keep only the ones the story cannot live without.
4. **Hold back details that exclude until after the first three scenes.** Identifying first and learning differences later keeps identification (Kaufman and Libby, sections 2 and 6). Check: the first three scenes state nothing a meaningful share of players would not share (siblings, a father at home, a garden). Note: dad running behind the bike assumes a present father, so consider placing it after the grandma scenes, not first.
5. **Keep every "you" line in the present tense, memories included.** Present tense raised identification; first versus second person did not differ (Macrae, section 6). Check: grep the story text for past-tense verbs in "you" lines and convert them.
6. **Do not let the player see the life lines view until the final reply has landed.** It is an observer's view, and self-reminders block experience-taking (Hartung; Kaufman and Libby mirror study, sections 1 and 6). Test: A/B test the view available throughout against unlocked only after the ending, and compare a short transportation scale or a single "I forgot I was on my phone" item.
7. **No instructions, questions about the player's real life, or meta text after the first screen.** Attention is an antecedent of transportation, and self-awareness breaks it (van Laer; Kaufman and Libby). Lines like "Did you have a dog?" or "tap to continue" act as mirrors. Check: count UI and instruction strings after the intro and aim for zero; a tap target should look like part of the scene (the gate, the phone), not like a button with a label.
8. **Return to the waiting frame between every life chapter, briefly.** Low mood and loneliness are documented nostalgia triggers (Wildschut et al. 2006, section 3). The old "you", alone and waiting, is the trigger, and each memory is the nostalgic answer to it. Check: the phone screen with no new message appears before each chapter; test whether players report being "more moved" with or without the returns.
9. **Make the redemption echo exact: the mom scene and the final reply should mirror each other in wording and picture.** Nostalgic narratives typically redeem a negative scene through a later one (Wildschut et al., section 3). The teenage text left on read is the negative scene, and the kid's reply redeems it. Check: the same message bubble layout and the same five words; the player's teenage "seen" state contrasts with the final reply's arrival. Do not explain the echo in text.
10. **Give each loss a quiet later echo of the same close other.** The same redemption pattern (inference): the dog waiting at the gate could be echoed by the "you" waiting by the phone, and the drifted best friend by the online friend. Check: map every negative beat to a later warm beat that rhymes with it, and cut negative beats that have no echo.
11. **Put a close other in every scene except the first night alone in the city.** Nostalgic memories centre on the self with close others (section 3). The city night is the one scene where absence is the point, and it lands by contrast. Check: a cast list per scene; the city scene should be the only one with nobody in it.
12. **Place positive milestones near their scripted ages, and let losses fall later and less precisely.** Positive script events have tight expected ages (love about 14, first job about 22, partner about 27, child about 29), negative ones are spread out, and parents' death is expected at about 50 (PMC3972131, section 4). Check: tabulate the age of "you" in each scene against the script ages; move outliers or give them a reason.
13. **Keep the 15 to 30 stretch mostly warm.** The reminiscence bump holds for positive events, not negative ones (Rubin and Berntsen 2003, section 4). Check: between the dog's death (16) and the kid, at least two clearly warm scenes (the city night with freedom in it, the online friend, the partner) for each heavy one.
14. **Fix one hidden birth year and audit the technology against it.** Verisimilitude is a core antecedent of transportation (van Laer, section 1), and the teenage text means the "you" had a mobile phone as a teen. So the old "you" lives decades from now. Check: pick a birth year (for example, about 2000), then confirm every device, school detail and online-friend medium fits its decade, and that the old-age present does not show today's phone as if it were 50 years later.
15. **Offer a non-phone version of the teenage beat for older players, or accept the narrowing knowingly.** Players whose teens had no mobile phones had no "text me" to leave on read (section 4 inference). One option: make the object the mother's request at the door ("call me when you get there") and keep the phone only for the present-day frame. Test: ask players over 40 and under 30 whether the mom scene felt like theirs.
16. **Swap culturally narrow props for their universal function, or pair them.** Close others and momentous events travel across 18 countries (Hepper et al.), while specific props depend on cultural proximity (Larsen and Laszlo snippet, section 2). Jam jars stay, since they are grandma's hands, but the line should lead with grandma (the close other), not with the jam. Check: each childhood prop line names or implies the person it belongs to.
17. **Run the specificity test the literature has not run.** No study was found comparing a specific sensory detail with a generic one (section 2 gap). Test: two versions of the grandma scene (for example, "dill and floor polish" against "the house smelled like summer"). Afterwards ask players "did this remind you of something from your own life? write it in one line" and compare how often and how vividly they answer.
18. **Run the mismatch test for "you".** Section 6 gap. After play, ask players to tick which story facts were not true for them (no dog, no siblings, no kid, a parent absent) and rate transportation. If mismatched facts early in the story hurt more than late ones, that confirms change 4 for this story.

### Moments most likely to land (ranked; inference from the findings above)
1. The final reply from the kid: having children is the most-named script event (72%), and the reply closes the story's redemption arc.
2. Parents ageing and dying: among the most negative expected events (-1.97), expected at about 50 and named by 44%.
3. Grandma's garden and the jam: a close other in the odour-memory decade, and a descriptive opening scene where remindings cluster. Strongest if a smell is named; narrowest in its props.
4. Mom's "text me when you get home" left on read: close other plus the redemption setup. Strong for players under about 35; weaker for older players without that teenage experience.
5. The partner: falling in love and marriage are both top script events.
6. The first night alone in the city: leaving home is a script event. It lands by its contrast with the close-other scenes.
7. The dog at the gate and its death at 16: very strong for pet owners, absent for others, and a negative event inside the positive-event bump.
8. The school best friend drifting: common, but not a script event.
9. The online friend: the most generational and the least likely to be shared.
