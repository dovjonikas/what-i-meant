# Engagement science: why people get pulled into stories and keep coming back

Scope: psychology, media and game studies on what hooks a reader at the start (HOOK), keeps them through (HOLD), brings them back for the next episode (RETURN) and makes a story stay with them (STAY), turned into rules for a solo creator making short personal interactive stories on the web, phone first. Researched 2026-10-29 with web search; most sources were read at abstract or summary level, not full text (noted where it matters).

Evidence tags used below: [META] meta-analysis; [MULTI] several studies or a large dataset; [SINGLE] one study or a small sample; [CORR] correlational or text-mining only; [CONTESTED] conflicting results or failed replications; [WEAK] creator, vendor or blog claims, not peer reviewed.

## 1. Narrative transportation and engagement: what increases or breaks it (identification, perspective taking, empathy)

### Takeaway
Transportation (being "lost" in a story) is one of the best-supported constructs in media psychology, and the levers a writer controls are an identifiable character, a plot you can picture (sequence, climax, outcome) and believability. Engagement breaks when the story contradicts itself or the known world; similarity between reader and character helps identification only a little (g about 0.19), and psychological similarity beats demographic similarity. Narrative perspective (first, second, third person) shows weak or null effects.

### Cited Findings
- [META] Van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti and Wetzels (2014, Journal of Consumer Research 40(5)) meta-analysed 132 effect sizes from 76 published and unpublished articles (over 21,000 participants) and proposed the Extended Transportation-Imagery Model covering antecedents and consequences of transportation - [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/); [OnFiction summary](https://www.onfiction.ca/2014/02/research-bulletin-summarizing-over.html)
- [META] Story-side antecedents of higher transportation in that meta-analysis: easily identifiable characters, an imaginable plot (a temporal sequence of events, a climax and an outcome) and verisimilitude (events that seem possible and plausible). Receiver-side antecedents: familiarity with the genre, attention directed at the story, general transportability, higher education and being female. Consequences: affective responses, fewer critical thoughts, more narrative thoughts, and changes in beliefs, attitudes and intentions - [OnFiction summary](https://www.onfiction.ca/2014/02/research-bulletin-summarizing-over.html); [OnFiction 2014](https://www.onfiction.ca/2014/06/transportation-into-narrative-worlds.html). Exact effect sizes per antecedent could not be extracted (PDF not machine-readable here).
- Green and Brock (2000, JPSP) built the 15-item transportation scale; items ask whether the reader could picture the events, whether the story affected them emotionally and whether events felt relevant to their life. Transported readers are less likely to counterargue the content (counterarguing is treated as a mediator) - [Wikipedia: Transportation theory (aggregator)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_theory_(psychology))
- [META] Braddock and Dillard (2016) meta-analysis is reported as showing familiarity with a story's content and alignment with its beliefs change the strength of post-story attitudes, intentions and beliefs (no figures given in the source seen) - [Wikipedia: Transportation theory (aggregator)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_theory_(psychology))
- What breaks engagement: Busselle and Bilandzic (2008, Communication Theory) argue that violations of external realism (mismatch with the real world) and narrative realism (incoherence inside the story) create inconsistencies in the reader's mental model, which can disrupt engagement and weaken persuasive power. Note: theory paper; the empirical tests of the disruption hypothesis were not checked - [Communication Theory 18(2)](https://academic.oup.com/ct/article/18/2/255/4098709)
- Busselle and Bilandzic (2009) Narrative Engagement scale has four dimensions: narrative understanding, attentional focus, emotional engagement and narrative presence - [Measuring narrative engagement](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/39512). A later study questioned whether the transportation measure behaves the same for audio-visual narratives - [Transportation into Audio-visual Narratives: A Closer Look](https://profiles.umsl.edu/en/publications/transportation-into-audio-visual-narratives-a-closer-look/). A 2026 meta-analysis titled "Engagement is not one thing" re-examines the scale (only metadata seen) - [Augsburg OPUS](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/132636)
- [META] Similarity and identification, meta-analysis 1: Chen, Dong and Wang (2024, Communication Research), 19 articles, 123 effect sizes. Similar characters produced small gains in identification (d = 0.14) and self-referencing (d = 0.16); effects on transportation and resistance were only marginal. Similarity via occupation or living place had the strongest impact; effects were larger with first-person narration and social-issue topics - [Bohrium record](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/654deccc939a5f4082e8f4bb); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/00936502231204834)
- [META] Similarity and identification, meta-analysis 2: Current Psychology (2023), 39 studies, k = 50, N = 11,077: small robust similarity effect on identification (g = 0.19), little sign of publication bias; psychological similarity beat objective (demographic) similarity - [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-023-04842-4)
- [SINGLE x2] Perspective: two experiments comparing first-person internal, third-person internal and third-person external focalisation found no effects on social and moral cognition and no mediation through transportation or identification - [Frontiers in Communication 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2020.611935)
- [SINGLE] Present tense produced more identification with the narrator than past tense; first versus second person made no significant difference - [Macrae, Diegesis](https://www.diegesis.uni-wuppertal.de/index.php/diegesis/article/download/214/294?inline=1)
- [SINGLE, qualitative, n = 17] In an interactive story, readers let the text decide who "you" refers to, and accepted identifying with "you" more readily when that "you" had a positive role - [Reading Digital Fiction](https://readingdigitalfiction.com/second-person-narration/)
- [SINGLE x2] Interactive vs linear versions of the same story: higher need for cognition predicted more identification (Study 1) and more transportation (Study 2) in the interactive version, i.e. interactive stories reward readers willing to spend effort - [Need for Cognition, Transportability, and Engagement with Interactive Narratives (Games for Health Journal 2020)](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/need-for-cognition-transportability-and-engagement-with-interacti/)
- Green and Jenkins (2014, Journal of Communication 64(3)) frame interactive narratives by greater user control and looser structure and propose effects on transportation, identification, perceived realism, responsibility and enjoyment. It is a framework, not an experiment - [UB record](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/interactive-narratives-processes-and-outcomes-in-user-directed-st/)
- A University of Vienna study is titled "Character identification is predicted by narrative transportation, immersive tendencies, and interactivity" (only the title and record seen) - [Univie record](https://genderandagency.univie.ac.at/forschung/detailansicht/pure/9731b204-d677-4ed6-94dc-2b202248dba7/show/publ/Pure)

### Inferences
- The strongest writer-controlled levers are concrete: a specific person the reader can picture, wanting something, in events that could really happen. Personal true stories have a built-in verisimilitude advantage that is easy to lose with invented polish.
- Demographic matching ("make the hero like the audience") buys very little; shared feelings and situations matter more, and even then the effect is small. Writing the universal emotional situation is a better bet than tailoring the profile.
- Grammatical person is not a lever worth agonising over; tense may be (present tense showed more identification in one study).
- Interactive choice raises the effort cost; readers low in need for cognition may engage less. On a phone, with casual readers, each choice must pay for itself.

#### Rules for short interactive phone stories
1. [HOOK] Put one nameable person with a visible want or problem on the first screen. No world-building or title cards before the person.
2. [HOOK] Write the opening so it can be pictured: one concrete sensory detail beats a summary sentence (imagery is part of how transportation is measured).
3. [HOLD] Guard internal consistency like a budget: a continuity slip, an off-voice line or a detail that rings false is exactly what the disruption account says pulls readers out. Keep the real details of personal stories; they carry believability.
4. [HOLD] Aim for psychological similarity (a feeling everyone has had) instead of demographic similarity.
5. [HOLD] Present tense is a cheap, low-risk default for immediacy. "You" is fine but not magic; if the reader plays "you", give "you" a role they are glad to own.
6. [HOLD] Keep choices light and in the story's voice; interactivity costs mental effort and that cost is not evenly spread across readers.

### Gaps
- Exact effect sizes for each antecedent in van Laer et al. (2014) and the overall Braddock and Dillard (2016) figures were not obtained (full text not readable in this session).
- No study found that measures transportation specifically for text stories read on phones versus other devices.
- Empathy and perspective-taking as distinct outcomes (for example, the "fiction builds empathy" literature and its replication debate) were not covered here for lack of tool budget.

## 2. Curiosity, suspense, surprise, open loops and cliffhangers; episodic structure and the Zeigarnik effect

### Takeaway
Curiosity is best modelled as an information gap the reader feels can be closed; it peaks when the reader half-knows the answer, and high-curiosity states improve memory even for incidental material. Cliffhangers raised the desire for the next installment in two written-story studies (about 500 people) but not in a video study (133 people), and suspense is felt as unpleasant: the pleasure is in its resolution. The famous Zeigarnik memory advantage for unfinished tasks does not hold up in a 2025 meta-analysis; what is robust is the Ovsiankina effect, the urge to resume an interrupted task. Spoiler research is contested.

### Cited Findings
- Loewenstein (1994, Psychological Bulletin) "The Psychology of Curiosity: A Review and Reinterpretation" treats curiosity as cognitively induced deprivation arising from a perceived gap in knowledge; the gap gives curiosity its direction - [Stafforini record](https://stafforini.com/works/loewenstein-1994-psychology-curiosity-review/); [Nautilus](https://nautil.us/?p=235803). Practical corollaries described in popular summaries: curiosity needs some prior knowledge, and the gap must feel neither too big nor too small - [PBS NOVA](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/how-to-stimulate-curiosity/)
- [SINGLE + replication] Kang et al. (2009): with 40 trivia questions, curiosity followed an inverted U as a function of confidence (highest at moderate confidence, i.e. partially known answers); Spitzer et al. (2023, Psychological Research) replicated the pattern with new materials, and found that when information is judged important the curiosity-confidence relation can turn negative - [Spitzer et al. 2023 PDF](https://opendata.uni-halle.de/bitstream/1981185920/117064/1/s00426-023-01841-9.pdf)
- [SINGLE] Kang et al. (2009): after an 11 to 16 day delay, participants recalled answers better for questions they had been highly curious about; curiosity was associated with caudate activation - [Gruber and Ranganath review, Cardiff](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/114430/1/Gruber.%20Curiosity-driven%20memory.pdf)
- [SINGLE, fMRI] Gruber, Gelman and Ranganath (2014, Neuron): memory improved for answers people were curious about and also for unrelated incidental material (faces) shown during high-curiosity states, on immediate and one-day-delayed tests; midbrain and nucleus accumbens activity rose during high curiosity - [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494/). The dopamine-hippocampus mechanism is a hypothesis (PACE framework), not directly measured - [Cardiff review](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/114430/1/Gruber.%20Curiosity-driven%20memory.pdf)
- Ely, Frankel and Kamenica (2015, Journal of Political Economy) formalise suspense as the variance of next-period beliefs and surprise as the size of the change from the previous belief; entertainment value comes from belief movement - [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jpolec:doi:10.1086/677350)
- [CORR] A 2025 football paper argues suspense and surprise measures are better targets than outcome uncertainty, since prior research has not consistently linked outcome uncertainty to attendance or viewership - [arXiv 2506.21253](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.21253)
- [MULTI, nearly 500 participants, written stories] Schibler, Green and Hahn (Media Psychology, 2023 and 2024): readers got a cliffhanger, a protagonist-victory or an antagonist-victory ending. Cliffhanger readers wanted a future installment more than protagonist-victory readers (and more than antagonist-victory readers in Study 2); enjoyment of cliffhangers sat between the positive and negative endings, not significantly lower. Hahn: "We don't like suspense. We like when suspense is resolved." - [UB news release](https://www.buffalo.edu/news/releases/2023/06/hahn-cliffhangers.html); [Media Psychology DOI](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/15213269.2023.2219456); [UB record, "To pause with a cliffhanger or a temporary closure"](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/to-pause-with-a-cliffhanger-or-a-temporary-closure-the-differenti/)
- [SINGLE, n = 133, video] Wirz et al. (2023, Psychology of Popular Media): viewers watched three to four drama episodes; cliffhanger endings raised arousal but did not increase enjoyment or intention to continue watching - [Wirz et al. PDF](https://sonar.ch/documents/319990/files/2022_Wirz_Role.pdf)
- [META] Ghibellini and Meier (2025, Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, doi 10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w), "Interruption, recall and resumption": no memory advantage for unfinished tasks (Zeigarnik effect lacks universal validity) but a general tendency to resume interrupted tasks (Ovsiankina effect). One summary gives 59 publications reviewed. The authors suggest the original effect depended on situational factors such as experimenter authority, task demands and task involvement - [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html); [Uni Bern PDF](https://www.kog.psy.unibe.ch/e48276/e1785865/s41599-025-05000-w1_ger.pdf)
- Earlier replication failures of the Zeigarnik recall effect exist, for example Van Bergen (1968) - [Wikipedia: Zeigarnik effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeigarnik_effect)
- [CONTESTED] Spoilers: Leavitt and Christenfeld (2011) found spoiled mystery, ironic-twist and literary stories were enjoyed more; Levine, Betzner and Autry (215 students) and Johnson and Rosenbaum (2015) found spoilers reduced enjoyment; differences in spoiler type and format are the leading explanation; no meta-analysis found - [OnFiction 2017](https://www.onfiction.ca/2017/04/research-bulletin-spoiler-alert-not-all.html); [University of Arkansas news](https://news.uark.edu/articles/38085/fulbright-college-research-team-explores-how-spoilers-affect-reading-enjoyment)

### Inferences
- "Open loops make people remember" is the wrong mechanism; "open loops make people want to go back and finish" is the supported one (Ovsiankina). That shifts the design job from memory tricks to making resumption effortless.
- The cliffhanger evidence that best matches a web text story is the written-story evidence (positive for desire to continue); the null video result is a warning that cliffhangers are not universal and that arousal alone does not create intention.
- Since suspense is unpleasant and resolution is the reward, a next episode should pay off the previous hook quickly; a cliffhanger that stays unresolved is a debt, not an asset.
- Curiosity's memory boost spills onto incidental content, so the moment just before a reveal is the best slot for the line you most want remembered (inference from lab trivia studies, not tested in stories).

#### Rules for short interactive phone stories
1. [HOOK] Open with a question the reader can almost answer: give enough context to guess (moderate confidence), not a vague mystery and not a solved one.
2. [HOLD] Keep beliefs moving: every screen should either change what the reader thinks will happen (surprise) or raise what rides on the next screen (suspense). A screen that moves no belief is a candidate for cutting.
3. [HOLD] Put the sentence you want remembered right before a reveal, while curiosity is high.
4. [RETURN] End an episode on an unresolved question about a character the reader likes, then resolve it early in the next episode.
5. [RETURN] Design for resumption, not for nagging memory: save the reader's place automatically, make "continue where you left off" one tap, and make the link to the next episode the most obvious thing on the last screen.
6. [HOOK/HOLD] Revealing the ending first is a legitimate option (shift the gap from "what happens" to "how and why"), but the spoiler evidence is mixed, so test it rather than assume it.

### Gaps
- A Wimbledon minute-by-minute TV-audience study (reported as 8,563 observations from 80 matches, finding both suspense and surprise raised audience size, surprise more) appeared in search results, but its authors and link could not be confirmed, so it is not cited above.
- No study found on cliffhangers or episode endings in interactive or choice-based stories specifically, or on phone-first web fiction release schedules.
- Kang et al. (2009) and Loewenstein (1994) were read through secondary summaries, not the originals.

## 3. Emotion and memory: peak-end rule, emotional arcs, being moved (kama muta), enjoying sad stories (eudaimonic entertainment)

### Takeaway
How an experience is remembered is dominated by its most intense moment and its ending, while its length is largely ignored (meta-analysis, r = 0.58). Text-mining studies associate a fall-then-rise ("Man in a Hole") arc with downloads and box office, but these are correlational. "Being moved" is a cross-culturally valid positive emotion (kama muta) triggered by a sudden intensification of closeness, and people seek sad or mixed stories for meaning (appreciation), which is a different reward from fun.

### Cited Findings
- [META] Alaybek, Dalal and colleagues (2022, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 170, 104149) pooled 174 effect sizes: the peak-end effect on retrospective evaluations was large (r = 0.581, 95% CI 0.487 to 0.661), held across boundary conditions, was comparable to the overall average and stronger than trend and variability; duration's effect was essentially nil (duration neglect) - [OUCI record](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4NG6JZvl); [GMU dissertation defense](https://psychology.gmu.edu/defenses/1259)
- [CORR] Reagan et al. (2016, EPJ Data Science): sentiment arcs of 1,327 Project Gutenberg fiction works are dominated by six shapes: rags to riches (rise), tragedy (fall), man in a hole (fall-rise), Icarus (rise-fall), Cinderella (rise-fall-rise), Oedipus (fall-rise-fall). The most downloaded were Icarus, Oedipus and two sequential Man in a Hole arcs - [EPJ Data Science](https://epjds.epj.org/articles/epjdata/abs/2016/01/13688_2016_Article_93/13688_2016_Article_93.html); [Full PDF](https://cdanfort.w3.uvm.edu/research/2016-reagan-epj.pdf); [Pacific Standard](https://psmag.com/?p=18237)
- [CORR] Del Vecchio, Kharlamov, Parry and Pogrebna (2021, Journal of the Operational Research Society): emotional arcs of 6,174 film scripts; Man in a Hole films had the highest box office regardless of genre or budget; these films were the most talked about, not the best liked; the authors warn against treating this as a recipe - [Birmingham PDF](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/95602489/Del_Vecchio_et_al_2020_Improving_productivity_in_Hollywood_Journal_of_the_Operational_Research_Society.pdf); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.02221)
- [CORR] Boyd, Blackburn and Pennebaker (2020, Science Advances): across about 40,000 traditional narratives, three language processes recur: staging (articles and prepositions early), plot progression, and cognitive tension (words like think, realize, because rising toward the end). Adherence to the normative structure did not predict popularity - [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7413736); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/08/200807153641.htm)
- [MULTI] Zickfeld et al. (2019, Emotion 19(3)): kama muta tested in 19 countries, 15 languages, 3,542 participants; five facets (appraisal, valence, bodily sensations, motivation, labels) were highly correlated in every sample. Kama muta is a positive social-relational emotion evoked by experiencing or observing a sudden intensification of communal sharing, with warmth in the chest, moist eyes or tears, chills and a lump in the throat - [Tilburg record](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/kama-muta-conceptualizing-and-measuring-the-experience-often-labe/)
- [MULTI] Seibt et al. (2018, Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology): across seven samples in five countries, responses to touching videos were predominantly positive and led to feeling communal with the characters - [Haifa record](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/kama-muta-similar-emotional-responses-to-touching-videos-across-t/)
- [MULTI, pre-registered, n = 1,049] Four studies on moving climate messages: felt kama muta correlated with pro-environmental intentions (r = 0.48) and weakly with behaviour (r = 0.10), but the moving-versus-neutral message manipulation had no direct experimental effect; effects ran only through how moved people actually felt - [Frontiers in Psychology 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1112910)
- [MULTI] Oliver and Raney (2011, Journal of Communication 61(5)): four studies show hedonic (pleasure) and eudaimonic (meaning, insight into the human condition) motivations for entertainment are distinct, common, invariant across gender and consistent across the life span - [UB record](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/entertainment-as-pleasurable-and-meaningful-identifying-hedonic-a/); [Oliver chapter summary](https://is.muni.cz/el/fss/jaro2021/PSYb2990/um/doporucena_literatura/10.4324_9780429491146-17_chapterpdf.txt?lang=en)
- Oliver and Bartsch introduced "appreciation" for serious, poignant media experiences that "enjoyment" fits poorly; entertainment eliciting negative and mixed emotions is perceived as more meaningful and is sought partly for that reason - [Oliver chapter summary](https://is.muni.cz/el/fss/jaro2021/PSYb2990/um/doporucena_literatura/10.4324_9780429491146-17_chapterpdf.txt?lang=en); [Oliver and Raney citations](https://typeset.io/papers/entertainment-as-pleasurable-and-meaningful-identifying-2a8lir1cco)
- Oliver et al. (2018, Journal of Communication 68(2)) "self-transcendent media experiences": content that makes people look beyond their own concerns, recognise moral beauty and feel unity with others; later work splits this into self-focused mixed affect and contemplation versus other-oriented connectedness and prosociality - [VU record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/self-transcendent-media-experiences-taking-meaningful-media-to-a-/)

### Inferences
- Because duration is neglected and peak and end dominate, a short story loses nothing by being short; it loses a lot by having a flat peak or a weak final screen.
- For personal stories, the natural kama muta trigger (a sudden moment of closeness: kindness, reunion, sacrifice, being let in) is the most reliable "peak" to aim for; it is also the emotion most tied to feeling close to the characters.
- Framing something as "moving" does nothing by itself (the 2023 manipulation null); only a scene that actually moves people works, which argues for testing with real readers.
- Arc-shape findings are correlational and based on automatic sentiment scoring; treat "fall then rise" as a sensible default, not a law (Boyd et al. found structural conformity did not predict popularity).

#### Rules for short interactive phone stories
1. [STAY] Design the peak and the last screen first; they decide how the whole story is remembered. Do not spend effort padding length.
2. [STAY] Make the peak a moment of sudden closeness between people (the kama muta trigger), shown, not announced.
3. [HOLD] Default to fall then rise (trouble early, recovery late). If the story must end sad, make it meaningful rather than merely sad; appreciation is a real reward.
4. [STAY] Let the ending turn reflective ("I realised", "because"): narratives naturally end with sense-making, and that is where meaning lands.
5. [STAY] Never rely on labels like "a touching story"; check with a few real readers whether the peak actually moved them.
6. [STAY] In branching stories, every path needs its own peak and a strong end, since each reader remembers only their own path's peak and end.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests the peak-end rule on stories or interactive narratives specifically (the meta-analysis covers experiences generally).
- No meta-analysis on enjoyment of sad media or eudaimonic effects was found in this session.
- Whether being moved increases return visits or sharing of stories (as opposed to prosocial intentions) was not established.

## 4. Agency in games: choice, the illusion of choice, player investment (Telltale, Life is Strange), and when authored non-branching stories work better

### Takeaway
What matters is perceived agency, not the amount of branching: a linear story that clearly acknowledges the player's choices produced similar reported agency to a branching one, and choices feel meaningful when they lead to noticeably different content or to outcomes the player cares about. Adding choice does not by itself raise transportation or identification (null in a 216-person experiment); it helped only after choices were made meaningful. Autonomy predicts enjoyment and future play in games generally, but there are no large controlled experiments on Telltale or Life is Strange; qualitative work says players want visible feedback that their choice mattered.

### Cited Findings
- [SINGLE] Fendt, Harrison, Ware, Cardona-Rivera and Roberts (2012), "Achieving the Illusion of Agency": three versions of a text story (branching with feedback, linear with immediate feedback, linear without feedback); in most cases there was no significant difference in reported agency between the branching story and the linear story that explicitly acknowledged choices - [Fendt et al. PDF](https://www.cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/fendt2012achieving.pdf)
- [SINGLE, n = 88] Cardona-Rivera et al. (AIIDE 2012), "Foreseeing Meaningful Choices": participants in a choose-your-own-adventure felt more agency when choices led to situationally different content - [AAAI](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12716)
- [SINGLE, n = 141] Thue et al. (PaSSAGE work): perceived agency depends on how much the player wants the outcomes of their decisions; with equal actual agency, the group whose story was adapted by the AI reported higher perceived agency (read via search summary) - [AAAI PDF](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12437/12437-52-15965-1-2-20201228.pdf)
- [SINGLE x2] Agency in an educational depression narrative: Experiment 1 (N = 216) found no effects of agency on cognitive learning, intrinsic motivation, identification or transportation; after the choices were made more meaningful in Experiment 2 (N = 155), agency improved transformative learning but not cognitive learning (link appears to be this paper; read via search summary) - [Frontiers in Communication 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2022.966944)
- [MULTI] Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski (2006, Motivation and Emotion 30(4)): across four studies, perceived in-game autonomy and competence were associated with enjoyment, preference and short-term well-being; in Study 4 (online multiplayer community) autonomy, competence and relatedness each independently predicted enjoyment and future play; intuitive controls supported these needs and presence - [Rochester PDF](https://www.rochester.edu/warner/lida/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/02bfe513dd59366750000000.pdf); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/12/061226134706.htm)
- Iten, Steinemann and Opwis (CHI PLAY 2017, work in progress): game choices can be emotional, morally ambivalent and highly personally meaningful, but it is unclear which characteristics make them meaningful - [edoc Basel](https://edoc.unibas.ch/57939/)
- Telltale: an academic analysis argues that the claim that Telltale choices "don't matter" overlooks how they function, as philosophical thought experiments that test whether the player has internalised the series' themes - [Eludamos, "No Going Back"](https://septentrio.uit.no/index.php/eludamos/article/view/vol9no1-2). Industry commentary argues the illusion of choice is a deliberate trade-off against tracking too many variables (opinion, not data) - [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/the-illusion-of-player-choice-in-the-walking-dead/)
- [SINGLE, dissertation] Arrambide (University of Waterloo): in cinematic choice-based adventure games (including Life is Strange) feelings of agency rose when participants made decisions, but they often felt they got no meaningful feedback that the decision mattered - [Waterloo PDF](https://uwaterloo.ca/games-institute/sites/default/files/uploads/documents/karinaarrambide_player-agency-decision-making-and-morality-in-ceinemetic-choice-based-adventure-games.pdf)
- Life is Strange: work in progress using the public dataset of recorded player choices suggests players subverted expectations within the affordances developers built - [Hodge, Leeds Beckett](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/11312). A science-journalism piece reports a growing body of research that emotionally complex games prompt self-reflection - [Undark 2021](https://undark.org/2021/11/03/the-lure-of-emotionally-complex-video-games/)
- [SINGLE, n = 5, pilot] Players who interpreted constrained choices in character kept a sense of agency, while those focused on mechanics lost it - [GCU pilot study](https://researchonline.gcu.ac.uk/en/publications/player-agency-under-constraint-a-pilot-study-on-the-forced-choice/)
- A 2022 article cites Green and Jenkins (2014) for the idea that making decisions that matter to the plot may make readers more likely to identify with a character (proposal, not a tested result) - [Frontiers in Communication 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2022.966944)

### Inferences
- For a solo creator, the evidence favours "branch and bottleneck" or a linear spine with acknowledged choices over a wide branching tree: perceived agency comes from feedback and from choices the reader cares about, not from the number of endings.
- Choices that do not change anything the reader can notice, and that the story never acknowledges, are the worst case: they add effort (see need for cognition, section 1) without adding agency.
- An authored, non-branching story likely works better when the emotional payload depends on a precise sequence (a fixed peak and ending, section 3) and when the choices on offer would be cosmetic; interaction can then be limited to pacing, revealing and expressive (non-plot) choices.
- Telltale-style moral choices seem to earn investment by being personally meaningful and by the story reflecting them back, which is consistent with the lab findings, but this is interpretation, not controlled evidence.

#### Rules for short interactive phone stories
1. [HOLD] Acknowledge every choice on the very next screen (a line of dialogue, a changed detail, a remembered word). Acknowledgement buys most of the felt agency of real branching.
2. [HOLD] Offer only choices the reader has a stake in: about people they care about, with outcomes they would actually want or fear. Cut cosmetic choices.
3. [HOLD] Make choices lead to noticeably different next moments, even if the paths reconverge later (branch and bottleneck).
4. [STAY] Reflect the reader's choices back at the end (a closing line or summary that names what they chose); feedback that the choice mattered is what players report missing.
5. [HOLD] Use expressive choices (how the character says or feels something) when plot choices would threaten the authored peak; keep the emotional spine authored.
6. [HOLD] Keep controls invisible and instant on a phone (big tap targets, no ambiguity): intuitive controls supported autonomy and presence in the SDT studies.

### Gaps
- No controlled experiment specifically on Telltale's The Walking Dead or Life is Strange player investment was found; the Telltale and Life is Strange evidence is critical analysis, dissertations and qualitative work.
- No meta-analysis of agency or interactivity effects on narrative enjoyment or transportation was found.
- Regret, guilt and responsibility after choices (a likely driver of "it stayed with me") were not covered with citable studies in this session.

## 5. Attention in the short-video era: what holds attention in the first seconds and minutes on a phone

### Takeaway
There is no good peer-reviewed evidence for the specific "first 3 seconds" or "1.7 seconds" swipe claims; those numbers come from creators and vendors. The solid evidence says: people screen a page fast and, once past the first seconds, are much more likely to stay; visual appeal is judged in about 50 ms and that judgement matches slower ones; and every second of load delay beyond about 2 seconds drives abandonment, worst for short content. The "8-second attention span" is a myth; what has changed is how often people switch screens. Reading on screens costs a little comprehension (g = -0.21), mostly for expository text and under time pressure.

### Cited Findings
- [MULTI, large log data] Liu, White and Dumais (SIGIR 2010) modelled web dwell time with a Weibull distribution and found "negative aging": users screen a page first and, if it survives the initial screening, they keep reading ("screen-and-glean"); dwell-time distributions could be predicted from low-level page features - [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/understanding-web-browsing-behaviors-through-weibull-analysis-of-dwell-time/). Nielsen Norman Group's reading of it (205,873 pages, over 2 billion dwell times): the first seconds are where users decide, so the value must be clear within about 10 seconds (the 10-second figure is NN/g's interpretation) - [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/)
- [SINGLE] Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek and Brown (2006, Behaviour and Information Technology 25(2)): visual-appeal ratings of homepages shown for 50 ms tallied well with ratings after longer viewing; Lindgaard cautioned the data speak only to the speed of judging the image - [Nature news](https://www.nature.com/news/2006/060109/full/news060109-13.html); [WebSiteOptimization summary](https://websiteoptimization.com/speed/tweak/blink/)
- [MULTI, quasi-experimental, 6.7 million viewers] Krishnan and Sitaraman (ACM IMC 2012): viewers began abandoning videos that took more than 2 seconds to start, each additional second raised the abandonment rate by 5.8%; short videos were less forgiving of slow starts than long ones; a video frozen for 1% of its duration was watched about 5% less - [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/11/121113134805.htm); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/803145)
- [WEAK] The "8-second human attention span, less than a goldfish" claim traces to a 2015 Microsoft Canada report citing Statistic Brain, which pointed to no study; the BBC could not find a source - [Freakonomics "No Stupid Questions" transcript](https://rosetta.to/u/freakonomics/220-is-your-attention-span-shrinking-no-stupid-questions). Gloria Mark's screen-tracking work (book "Attention", 2023) is reported as showing average time on one screen before switching fell from about 2.5 minutes to about 47 seconds; this is task switching, not lost capacity (secondary sources only) - [Medium summary](https://medium.com/write-rise/the-attention-span-stat-everyone-keeps-getting-wrong-91c418793ccc)
- [META] Delgado, Vargas, Ackerman and Salmerón (2018, Educational Research Review): 54 studies, over 170,000 participants, comprehension was poorer on screen than on paper (g = -0.21); the disadvantage was larger for expository than narrative texts and under time constraints than with self-paced reading, and grew from 2000 to 2017 - [Delgado et al. PDF](https://www.uv.es/lasalgon/papers/Delgado%202018%20dont%20throw%20away%20your%20printed%20books.pdf); [TUM summary](https://www.edtech.tum.de/?p=2157). A secondary source reports Clinton (2019) found a similar effect (g = -0.25) limited to expository texts - [Leiden Psychology Blog](https://www.leidenpsychologyblog.nl/articles/screen-vs-paper-battle-of-the-e-books)
- [SINGLE, n = 16, preprint] Eye tracking of TikTok viewing found three attention modes (stable, segmented, fragmented); fast-paced content produced the most fragmented pattern, with shorter fixations and more frequent shifts - [Communication Today](https://communicationtoday.sk/watching-tiktok-an-eye-tracking-study-of-visual-attention-paid-to-short-form-videos/)
- [SINGLE, preprint] Randomly selected (non-personalised) short videos disrupted event segmentation and memory encoding - [bioRxiv 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.17.608429v1.full)
- [WEAK] Creator claims: one creator reports a 0.84 correlation between first-3-second retention and performance across 120 of his own videos and cites an unsourced "1.7 seconds to decide" figure - [dev.to](https://dev.to/bulkdl/hook-engineering-the-science-of-the-first-3-seconds-in-short-form-video-2026-2jp9); another reports that smaller drop-off between seconds 3 and 5 tracked views in a handful of client videos - [Josh Ryan newsletter](https://joshryan.kit.com/posts/the-3-5-second-rule-for-short-form-video)

### Inferences
- The first-seconds pressure is real but the mechanism is screening, not a shrunken attention span: readers decide fast whether something is worth it, then sustain attention if it is. That fits the transportation findings (section 1): get the person and the question on screen immediately, then let the story hold.
- For web stories, technical speed is part of the hook: the strongest causal number in this whole area is about load delay, not content.
- Narrative text is relatively robust to the screen-reading penalty, but timed or auto-advancing presentation makes screen comprehension worse; self-paced tapping is the safer pattern.
- Very fast pacing fragments visual attention (small study), so variation in pace is probably better than constant speed.

#### Rules for short interactive phone stories
1. [HOOK] The first screen must appear essentially instantly: no splash screens, intro animations or heavy assets before the first line; preload the next scene; never let a scene stall mid-story.
2. [HOOK] The first screen is judged as an image before it is read: clean, legible, one focal element.
3. [HOOK] Within the first screen or two, show what the story is about (the person and the question); readers who get past the screening phase tend to stay.
4. [HOLD] Let the reader control the pace with taps; avoid timed text or auto-advance.
5. [HOLD] Vary pace: alternate quick beats with a held moment instead of cutting fast throughout.
6. [HOLD] Do not design for an "8-second attention span"; design against switching: no outbound links, pop-ups or permission prompts mid-story.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that measures swipe-away or abandonment timing in the first seconds of short videos or interactive web stories; platform data (TikTok, YouTube, Instagram) are proprietary, and the circulating figures are creator or vendor claims.
- Small studies linking heavy short-video use to sustained-attention lapses exist (for example a 41-person CHI EA 2024 study reported in search summaries) but were not verified at source.
- Gloria Mark's exact figures were seen only through secondary sources; her book or papers should be checked before quoting.
