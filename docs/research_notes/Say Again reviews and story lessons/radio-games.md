# Radio, signal and voice-at-the-other-end narrative games: what critics and players praise and criticise

Scope: Killer Frequency (2023), Firewatch (2016), Stories Untold (2017), Oxenfree (2016), Radio Commander (2019), plus Signal Simulator, The Signal State, Mindcop, and a few neighbours found on the way (Not for Broadcast, Iron Lung, Lifeline 2015, 911 Operator, Voices of the Void, Signalis, Oxenfree II). Researched 2026-11-01.

Reading notes: every quote is verbatim from the fetched page unless marked "(search snippet, unverified)" or "(via Wikipedia)". Dashes inside quotes were normalised to a plain hyphen. Access limits: eurogamer.net, rockpapershotgun.com, ign.com, polygon.com, theguardian.com, theverge.com, telegraph.co.uk and gamesindustry.biz block the fetch tool, pcgamer.com and gamesradar.com return only a headline, the Steam review API redirected to the store front, and no Reddit thread could be read. Quotes from those outlets come second-hand through Wikipedia, Metacritic, OpenCritic or Steam store blurbs, and are marked. Steam voices come from Steam discussion threads, the Vaporlens Steam summary, GOG user reviews and Metacritic user reviews instead.

## Q1. Which concrete moments do reviewers and players name as the ones they remember, and why do they work?

### Takeaway
The remembered moments are almost always a voice doing something to the player: a whistle heard down a phone line, a stranger on a radio who "always seems to know the right thing to say" and is never seen, a broadcast "from another time", a text parser that starts to describe the room you are sitting in. The moments work because the game withholds the picture and makes the player build it from sound and a small set of instruments.

### Inferences
- The strongest remembered moments are diegetic sounds that double as decisions (the whistle, the frequency that "starts to shake"). For Say Again, a Morse rhythm or a carrier that breaks up should itself be the cue that forces a choice, not just set dressing.
- A voice that is never seen is remembered as a person, not a mechanic, when it is given its own wants (Delilah's "own life", the son's reasons for calling). Reviewers reward restraint: the unseen stays unseen.
- Stories Untold shows the instrument can be the twist carrier: the thing you operated all night turns out to mean something else. A chart and bearing lines that the player draws for four shifts could become the evidence in shift four.

### Gaps
- Direct text of the Eurogamer, Rock Paper Shotgun, PC Gamer, IGN, Polygon, Guardian and Edge reviews was not readable (crawler blocked or page truncated); their lines here are second-hand. No Reddit thread could be fetched.
- No reviewer names a single "moment" in Radio Commander or Signal Simulator beyond atmosphere in general.

## Q2. How do these games use the radio or the interface itself to create emotion or tension, and what did players find tedious about those mechanics?

### Takeaway
Tension comes from friction that is in character: a clunky 80s machine, a dial you have to hunt with, a timer that runs while a voice is still talking, a map you must keep by hand from what you hear. The same friction is what players call tedious once it stops carrying information: unskippable dialogue, slow walking with no sprint, repeated tuning, pinging every unit one by one, and interfaces that "lack audio feedback" or need "30 seconds of rotating the mouse wheel".

### Cited Findings

Stories Untold (the clearest design statement on friction)
- "In any other game, this would just be bad UI/UX. But here, it is essential." and "Each device in the game is method-acting. They are all permanently in character." - Teaching by repetition: "we want players to gradually discover the interface and workings of each system" and "There's a lot of deliberate repetition at play, so that when the time comes, you know roughly what to do already." - same source
- The cost admitted: "Some of the machines are clunky the point of being straight up difficult to use", "It can turn people off.", "The annoying part of repetitive use is just a thing that happens on the journey to the narrative truth" - same source

### Inferences
- The pattern across all of them: friction is welcome exactly as long as it carries meaning (a dial that finds a lost voice, a timer that forces a real choice) and resented the moment it becomes a chore (tuning for a door, pinging unit 3 again). Say Again's knob, hold-to-talk and Morse should each pay off in story at least once per shift, and have a fast path afterwards.
- "No skip" is the single most-counted Steam complaint for Killer Frequency and recurs for Radio Commander and Stories Untold. A replay or a failed shift needs a skip or a short path.
- Hand-kept state (Radio Commander's tokens, Iron Lung's coordinates) is praised for immersion and criticised for drudgery; the fix reviewers imply is small: a status display, or a way to ask once for all positions.
- Oxenfree's thread shows the exact failure mode of timed talk: the player cannot tell whether waiting is allowed. A visible cue (fading bubble, a carrier light) that says "you may still answer" is what players asked for.
- Stories Untold's rule "deliberate repetition so that when the time comes, you know roughly what to do already" is a procedure-teaching rule: drill the knob and the hold-to-talk in shift one so shift four can run on instinct.

### Gaps
- No GDC Vault transcript of "Do You Copy?" was accessible, so the specifics of how Firewatch handled silence, timers and restarts come from reviews, not the talk.
- The PC Gamer interview with McKellan on the numbers-station episode could not be read; only the snippet survives.
- Steam review text for Oxenfree, Radio Commander and Signal Simulator could not be pulled; the Steam voice is limited to discussion threads and third-party summaries.

## Q3. How do they handle the ending and the twist: what was praised, what was called a letdown, and what did reviewers wish had been different?

## Q4. Pacing: where do players say they got bored or stuck, and what did the games do to keep momentum?

### Takeaway
Boredom comes from waiting with nothing to do (waiting for calls, walking between places, unskippable talk) and from being stuck on an unclear instruction (a parser, an unexplained Morse beep, a dial you cannot read). Momentum came from interruptions and timers (the next caller, the ticking choice), from short runtimes that do not "outstay their welcome", from humour between scares, and from the game "keeping rolling" after a failure instead of making you retry.

### Inferences
- Four short shifts are the right unit: every game here is praised at 2-4 hours and criticised when stretched (Hon's "half as long"). A shift should end while the player still wants the next call.
- Idle time must have texture or be cut. Killer Frequency's idle filler (records, paper balls) reads as filler; Firewatch's idle time reads as atmosphere because the walk shows you something. Idle time at a coast station should be weather, traffic on other channels, the log to fill, not nothing.
- Interruptions are the momentum engine in all of them: the next caller, a timer, a whistle. A second voice breaking into the frequency is cheaper and stronger than a new location.
- The teaching burden should be front-loaded and short; both Killer Frequency and Not for Broadcast get dinged for long or overwhelming tutorials.

## Q5. What do reviewers say about dialogue choices that are only flavour versus choices with consequences, and about being able to fail?

### Takeaway
Reviewers accept flavour-only choices when the flavour is the relationship (Firewatch) and the game never pretends otherwise, and they punish games whose choices look weighty but resolve opaquely (Oxenfree's "fundamentally wrong", Killer Frequency's "not at all clear what criteria they're judged on"). Being able to fail is praised when the failure is a person dying and the night keeps going; it is criticised when it is a hidden test with arbitrary answers.

### Cited Findings

- "nothing you do during Firewatch actually affects the overall plot." yet choices "ripple forward to alter your relationship radically from one playthrough to the next." - [Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/227607/firewatch-review-get-lost-in-the-woods-find-yourself.html)
- "the story ends up in the same place regardless of your choices." and choices "never really change the outcome in any concrete way", a second playthrough reveals how little they matter; still, "The snarky dialogue between Henry and Delilah feels natural no matter what choices you make." - [Anigamers](https://anigamers.com/reviews/firewatch-gone-anywhere-but-home/)

### Inferences
- Two honest designs are accepted: Firewatch's "the plot is fixed, the relationship is yours" and Killer Frequency's "someone dies and the night goes on". The dishonest middle (choices that look decisive but resolve by hidden rules) is what gets called "fundamentally wrong".
- For a Morse-only caller, the choice set is naturally small and the consequence should be visible on the chart or in the log, not in a hidden score. Hon's "not at all clear what criteria they're judged on" is the trap to avoid: a bearing is right or wrong in a way the player can see.
- Silence is a documented, liked option in Firewatch, Oxenfree and Killer Frequency; a hold-to-talk button not pressed is a legitimate answer.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies how many Firewatch or Killer Frequency players replayed to change outcomes; only Impulsegamer and Metacritic users mention replay value.
- The Verge's and IGN's full reasoning on choices could not be read.

## Q6. Killer Frequency specifically: how it teaches its procedures, what players loved about being the voice on the radio, and what they found clunky

### Takeaway
Killer Frequency teaches by premise and props: a producer explains that 911 is being rerouted to the station, the desk is analogue and self-explanatory, and each call is a contained problem solved with an item in the building (a map, a hot-wiring article, a code). Players love the voice-acting-led intimacy of being the host and the guilt and relief of a caller living or dying; they find the walking, the non-skippable talk, the fiddly crosshair on small buttons, the easy multiple-choice puzzles and the two-item inventory clunky.

### Inferences
- The teaching pattern that worked: one explained premise (911 rerouted), an analogue desk that reads at a glance, and one prop per call. The pattern that failed: a long tutorial, props that do nothing (the soundboard), and answers chosen from bubbles. Say Again's knob, PTT and Morse should all do something real from the first call, and the chart should be the one prop that returns every shift.
- The "voice" praise is about performance and intimacy, not about the mechanics of broadcasting. A Morse-only caller shifts that intimacy into rhythm and timing; the operator's own voice (log notes, what he says into the mic) becomes the audible side.
- The most repeated clunk is dead time plus no skip. A station with weather, other traffic and a log gives dead time a job.

## Q7. Which other radio- or signal-centred narrative games are well reviewed, and what do their reviews add?

### Inferences
