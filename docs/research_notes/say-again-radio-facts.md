# Say again: radio facts checked

Checked 2026-11-01. Each claim has a verdict (right, right with small rewording, needs rewording, wrong), the wording to use in the game, and the source.

## 1. Channel 16

Verdict: right.

Correct wording: Marine VHF channel 16 is for distress, urgency, safety and calling. Once you have made contact on 16, both stations move to a working channel and leave 16 free.

Sources:
- USCG Navigation Center channel table (channel 16: "International Distress, Safety and Calling"): https://www.navcen.uscg.gov/us-vhf-channel-information
- US rule 47 CFR 80.116 (after call and reply on 156.800 MHz, ships must change to a working frequency; transmissions on 16 must not exceed 1 minute): https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/80.116

## 2. Mayday, pan-pan, sécurité

Verdict: right, small rewording.

Correct wording: "Mayday" said three times means a ship, aircraft or person is in grave and imminent danger and needs immediate help. "Pan-pan" said three times means a very urgent message about the safety of a ship or a person, short of grave and imminent danger. "Sécurité" said three times comes before an important navigation or weather warning.

Note: the rules do not say "no danger to life". The test for pan-pan is "urgent, but not grave and imminent".

Sources:
- 47 CFR 80.314 (Mayday, "grave and imminent danger", spoken three times): https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/80.314
- 47 CFR 80.1131 (pan-pan, "very urgent message ... concerning the safety of a mobile unit or a person"): https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/80.1131
- 47 CFR 80.1133 (sécurité, "important navigational or meteorological warning"): https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/80.1133
- Plovput, Croatian state coast radio (pan-pan "if there is no justification for sending a distress call"): https://plovput.hr/en/radio-service/emergency-procedures

## 3. Over and out

Verdict: right.

Correct wording: "Over" means "this is the end of my transmission, a reply is needed, go ahead". "Out" means "this is the end of my transmission, no reply is needed or expected". Every transmission ends with one of them, never both, so "over and out" is not proper procedure.

Source: ACP 125(G) Radiotelephone Procedures (2016), Annex A proword list and para 536: https://web.cs.dal.ca/%7Ejamie/Words/Notes/ACP%20125%20(G)%20Radio%20Telephone%20Procedures%20NOV%202016.pdf

## 4. Say again

Verdict: right, small rewording.

Correct wording: To ask for a repeat you say "say again" (repeat all of your last transmission), and the sender answers "I say again". In military procedure "repeat" is avoided, because on artillery nets it asks for the same fire to be fired again.

Note: ACP 125(G) has no "repeat" proword at all. I could not open the artillery supplement itself (ACP 125 US Supp-2), so the artillery meaning rests on secondary sources.

Sources:
- ACP 125(G), Annex A (SAY AGAIN, I SAY AGAIN): link as in claim 3
- Wikipedia, Procedure word (cites ACP 125 US Supp-2, Radiotelephone Procedures for the Conduct of Artillery and Naval Gunfire): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procedure_word

## 5. Roger and wilco

Verdict: right.

Correct wording: "Roger" means "I have received your last transmission satisfactorily". "Wilco" means "I have received your message, I understand it and I will comply", and only the station it was sent to uses it. Wilco already includes roger, so the two are never said together.

Source: ACP 125(G), Annex A and the note on ROGER and WILCO: link as in claim 3

## 6. Radio check

Verdict: right.

Correct wording: In ACP 125, answering a radio check with just "roger" means loud and clear. Reports like "five by five" are not used; you say plain words such as "weak but readable" or "loud but distorted".

Note: ACP 125 also assumes a station hears well unless told otherwise, so signal reports are only exchanged when one station cannot hear another clearly.

Source: ACP 125(G), paras 631 to 633: link as in claim 3

## 7. Figures

Verdict: needs rewording.

Correct wording: In aviation (ICAO) the digits are said ZE-RO, WUN, TOO, TREE, FOW-er, FIFE, SIX, SEV-en, AIT, NIN-er, and military ACP 125 uses the same sounds (it writes nine as NINE-ER). The ITU and IMO maritime figure code is a different set: NADAZERO, UNAONE, BISSOTWO, TERRATHREE, KARTEFOUR, PANTAFIVE, SOXISIX, SETTESEVEN, OKTOEIGHT, NOVENINE.

Correction (from say-again-coast-station-practice.md): NADAZERO and the rest are the signal-book code for when language is a problem, not what is spoken on VHF. The IMO Standard Marine Communication Phrases (SMCP 2.2) have ships say digits one at a time as WUN, TOO, TREE, FOWER, FIFE, SIX, SEVEN, AIT, NINER, ZEERO, much like aviation and the army.

Sources:
- ICAO Annex 10 Vol II, 5.2.1.4.3, in an extract published by the Polish Civil Aviation Authority: https://ulc.gov.pl/_download/personel_lotniczy/ICAO_LPR/sarps.pdf
- ACP 125(G), Table 3-2: link as in claim 3
- International Code of Signals, Pub 102 (US edition 1969, revised 2003), figure spelling table: https://archive.org/details/pub-102

## 8. Keying without speaking

Verdict: needs rewording.

Correct wording: Pressing the transmit button without speaking sends a carrier with no voice on it. A receiver hears its hiss go quiet (or its squelch open) while the button is held, and often a short burst of noise when it is let go. Clicking the button as a yes or no signal is an informal trick that people agree on beforehand; it is not in ACP 125 or marine procedure.

Note: the physics part of the claim is right. The "click twice for yes" part is real but informal; I found it only in first-hand accounts, not in any manual.

Sources:
- Ham Radio School, Practical signal reports ("full quieting": a strong FM signal with no background noise): https://www.hamradioschool.com/post/practical-signal-reports
- US patent 5303407 (squelch tail circuits mute the receiver after a signal "to avoid annoying bursts of noise"): https://patents.google.com/patent/US5303407
- First-hand Vietnam account of a guard pressing the squelch button twice to show he was awake (not doctrine): https://vnwarstories.com/vn.ObservationPost.html
- Cruising World, 2009-03-04: Coast Guard receivers can take a bearing "even if the caller only keys the microphone for half a second": https://www.cruisingworld.com/?p=42046

## 9. Morse

Verdict: right.

Correct wording: SOS is sent as one unbroken signal, ...---..., with no gaps between the letters, so it works like a prosign. Among radio amateurs, 73 means "best regards", 88 means "love and kisses", and HI (often sent as HI HI) is laughter.

Note: hams say "73", never "73s" or "best 73".

Sources:
- ITU News, Pioneers' page, 2006 (SOS is "a continuous group of Morse dots and dashes, rather than separate letters"): https://www.itu.int/itunews/manager/display.asp?lang=en&year=2006&issue=06&ipage=pioneers&ext=html
- Ethics and Operating Procedures for the Radio Amateur (ON4UN, ON4WW), hosted by ARRL, section II.9.28: https://www.arrl.org/files/file/DXCC/Eth-operating-EN-ARRL-CORR-JAN-2011.pdf

## 10. Direction finding

Verdict: needs rewording.

Correct wording: Some coastguards can take a bearing on any VHF call they hear. The US Coast Guard's Rescue 21 towers do this, and bearings from two or more towers crossing give a position. Three bearings rarely meet in one point; the small triangle they make is the "triangle of error", called a "cocked hat" in British usage.

Note: not every coastguard has shore stations for this. The UK switched off its shore-based VHF direction finding at the end of 2010 and kept it on its rescue boats and aircraft. Check the country before the game names one.

Sources:
- USCG Rescue 21 fact sheet (a line of bearing from the tower to any VHF call it receives): https://dcms.uscg.mil/Portals/10/CG-9/Acquisition%20PDFs/Factsheets/R21.pdf
- Maritime Executive, 2023-12-20 (watchstanders "used bearings of the vessel's last VHF transmission to triangulate"): https://maritime-executive.com/article/coast-guard-uses-vhf-direction-finding-to-rescue-two-fishermen
- IALA dictionary, Triangle of error ("Cocked hat (G.B.)"): https://iala.int/wiki/dictionary/index.php/Triangle_of_error
- Yachting Monthly, 2010-10-05, MCA ends shore-based VHF DF: https://www.yachtingmonthly.com/news/mca-axe-vhf-direction-finder-6154

## 11. VHF range and night

Verdict: needs rewording (the HF part).

Correct wording: VHF reaches roughly as far as line of sight, so antennas go as high as possible, and height matters more than power. Night does not stretch VHF, because VHF does not use the ionosphere. Medium wave and the lower HF frequencies do reach farther at night, because the D layer that soaks them up by day fades after sunset; the higher HF frequencies do the opposite, passing through the thinner night ionosphere into space, so those bands close.

Note: now and then VHF does go far past the horizon, through temperature inversions or sporadic E.

Sources:
- Australian Bureau of Meteorology, Space Weather Services, Introduction to HF radio propagation (sections 1, 2.9 and 2.10): https://sws.bom.gov.au/Category/Educational/Other%20Topics/Radio%20Communication/Intro_HF_Radio.pdf
- IMO resolution MSC.509(105), Annex, sea area A1 (VHF range "is likely to be limited by propagation factors rather than lack of radiated power"): https://wwwcdn.imo.org/localresources/en/KnowledgeCentre/IndexofIMOResolutions/MSCResolutions/MSC.509(105).pdf

## 12. Cold batteries

Verdict: right.

Correct wording: A cold battery gives less: one that gives full capacity at 27 °C may give only about half at -18 °C. The loss lasts only while it is cold, so warming the battery (in a pocket, against the body) brings some runtime back.

Source: Battery University, BU-502 Discharging at high and low temperatures: https://batteryuniversity.com/article/bu-502-discharging-at-high-and-low-temperatures

## 13. Mast lights

Verdict: right, small rewording.

Correct wording: Tall masts carry aviation obstruction lights. At night these are red, flashing or steady, and towers usually have flashing red. Many tall masts also have white flashing lights for day and twilight, and some use white lights day and night at lower brightness after dark.

Sources:
- FAA AC 70/7460-1M, Obstruction Marking and Lighting: https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/Advisory_Circular_70_7460_1M.pdf
- Replaced on 2026-08-11 by AC 70/7460-1N (not read in full): https://www.faa.gov/regulations_policies/advisory_circulars/index.cfm/go/document.current/documentnumber/70_7460-1

## 14. Polaris

Verdict: right.

Correct wording: Polaris is the star at the end of the Little Dipper's handle. It sits less than a degree from the north celestial pole, so its height above the horizon is close to your latitude. This works only in the Northern Hemisphere.

Sources:
- EarthSky, Polaris (end of the handle; not usable from the Southern Hemisphere): https://earthsky.org/brightest-stars/polaris-the-present-day-north-star/
- EarthSky, Big Dipper to Polaris (less than a degree from the pole): https://earthsky.org/tonight/use-big-dipper-to-find-polaris-and-little-dipper/
- Michigan State University, AST 101 handout (height of the celestial pole equals the latitude): https://web.pa.msu.edu/courses/2012fall/AST101/LatIntro.pdf

## 15. 1750 Hz tone burst

Verdict: right.

Correct wording: Many handheld amateur radios have a button that sends a short 1750 Hz tone burst, the old way to open repeaters in the UK and Europe. In the UK the burst is 1750 Hz plus or minus 25 Hz, accepted after 200 to 400 ms; new repeaters must take CTCSS since 2004, and the tone burst is still allowed alongside it.

Source: UK repeater site of the RSGB repeater committee (ETCC), Access: https://www.ukrepeater.net/access.htm

## More facts

1. SOS does not stand for "save our souls" or anything else. It was picked because the pattern is simple and hard to mistake; it first appeared in German rules in 1905 and became international in Berlin in 1906. Source: ITU News, 2006: https://www.itu.int/itunews/manager/display.asp?lang=en&year=2006&issue=06&ipage=pioneers&ext=html
2. The distress words come from French. Mayday is said like "m'aider" (help me), pan-pan like the French word "panne" (breakdown), and sécurité as in French. Sources: 47 CFR 80.314, 80.1131, 80.1133: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/80.314
3. The station running a distress can order everyone else off the channel with "Seelonce Mayday" (French "silence, m'aider") and lift it with "Seelonce feenee" ("silence fini"). Source: 47 CFR 80.1125: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/80.1125
4. When reception is bad, military operators say "words twice" and then send every phrase twice. Source: ACP 125(G), Annex A and para 639: https://web.cs.dal.ca/%7Ejamie/Words/Notes/ACP%20125%20(G)%20Radio%20Telephone%20Procedures%20NOV%202016.pdf
5. In Morse a dash lasts three dots, the gap between letters three dots and the gap between words seven. A mistake is marked with eight dots in a row, and the @ sign has its own code, .--.-. Source: ITU-R M.1677-1: https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/m/R-REC-M.1677-1-200910-I!!PDF-E.pdf
6. The IMO has a formula for VHF coverage: range in nautical miles = 2.5 × (√H + √h), with antenna heights in metres. A coast antenna 100 m up and a boat antenna 4 m up give about 30 nm; even the highest coastal hills rarely give much beyond 45 nm. Sources: IMO MSC.509(105): https://wwwcdn.imo.org/localresources/en/KnowledgeCentre/IndexofIMOResolutions/MSCResolutions/MSC.509(105).pdf and BoM, Introduction to HF radio propagation, 2.9: https://sws.bom.gov.au/Category/Educational/Other%20Topics/Radio%20Communication/Intro_HF_Radio.pdf
7. Medium wave carries so far at night that most US AM stations must turn their power down or go off the air after sunset, or they would drown each other out. Source: FCC: https://www.fcc.gov/media/radio/am-stations-at-night
8. Polaris is only around the 48th brightest star, and it will not stay the North Star. Earth's axis wobbles once in about 26,000 years, and in about 12,000 years Vega will be the North Star. Sources: EarthSky: https://earthsky.org/brightest-stars/polaris-the-present-day-north-star/ and NASA APOD, 2015-05-08: https://science.nasa.gov/image-article/apod-2015-may-8-when-vega-is-north
