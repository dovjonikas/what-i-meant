# Why AI writing sounds like AI: the causes behind the tells

Scope note: research done 2026-10-29 with WebSearch and WebFetch only. Where a figure comes from a secondary summary rather than the paper itself, that is said next to it. Each "Inferences" block turns the causes into practical implications for someone who uses an AI to help write but wants the result to sound like one specific human.

## 1. Training and decoding: why the default output drifts to the most typical phrasing

### Takeaway
The generic voice is mostly a post-training effect layered on top of next-token prediction: preference tuning (RLHF, DPO) sharpens a model toward the most typical, familiar answer, partly because human raters themselves prefer familiar text. The result is measurably lower diversity per prompt, and writers who co-write with tuned models end up sounding more like each other.

### Cited Findings
- Kirk et al. (ICLR 2024) compared supervised fine-tuning, reward modelling and RLHF on OPT and LLaMA (summarisation, instruction following): RLHF generalises better to new inputs but "substantially decreases" the diversity of outputs sampled for a given input compared with SFT, and on some metrics also across inputs. The authors call it an inherent tension between generalisation and diversity with current methods: [Kirk et al., arXiv 2310.06452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06452)
- Zhang et al., "Verbalized Sampling" (2025, ICML 2026) locate mode collapse in the preference data, not only the algorithm: "typicality bias", where annotators systematically favour familiar text, grounded in the mere-exposure effect, the availability heuristic, processing fluency and schema congruity (people prefer text that is "familiar, fluent, and predictable"): [Zhang et al., arXiv 2510.01171 v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01171v2)
- They model reward as true utility plus alpha times the base model's log-likelihood of the response. On HelpSteer they estimate alpha = 0.57 plus or minus 0.07 (Llama 3.1 405B Base) and 0.65 plus or minus 0.07 (GLM 4.5 Base), p below 10^-14: [Zhang et al. v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01171v2)
- Their formal argument: even with a perfect reward model, the bias term sharpens the optimum (an effective exponent gamma = 1 + alpha/beta above 1, like lowering temperature), so among responses of equal true quality "typicality bias acts as a tiebreaker that sharpens the output of the aligned model into the mode of the base model": [Zhang et al. v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01171v2)
- A secondary AI-written review reports typicality bias rates of 54% to 62% across OpenAI TL;DR, UltraFeedback, HelpSteer-v2 and Skywork; I could not confirm this from the paper text I read: [Moonlight review](https://www.themoonlight.io/es/review/verbalized-sampling-how-to-mitigate-mode-collapse-and-unlock-llm-diversity)
- Diversity loss through the training pipeline (Tulu-70B): base model 45.4% diversity; direct prompting falls from 20.8% after SFT to 10.8% after DPO: [Zhang et al. v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01171v2)
- Padmakumar and He (ICLR 2024): people writing argumentative essays with InstructGPT (feedback-tuned) produced essays that were more similar to each other, with lower lexical and content diversity; writing with base GPT-3 did not produce a statistically significant drop. The homogenisation came mainly from the model's own contributions, not from the human-written parts: [Padmakumar and He, arXiv 2309.05196](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05196)
- Doshi and Hauser (Science Advances, July 2024, about 500 Prolific participants): access to AI story ideas made individual stories rated more creative, better written and more enjoyable, with the biggest gains for the least creative writers, but AI-assisted stories were more similar to each other. The authors frame it as a social dilemma: individuals gain, the collective produces a narrower range of content: [Doshi and Hauser, PMC11244532](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532)
- Anderson, Shah and Kreminski (Creativity and Cognition 2024, 36 participants): ChatGPT users and Oblique Strategies users were similarly homogeneous as individuals, but ChatGPT users were more homogeneous as a group; ChatGPT users produced more, more detailed ideas yet felt less responsible for them: [Anderson et al., arXiv 2402.01536](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01536v2)
- Reinhart et al. (PNAS, Feb 2025), using Biber's lexical, grammatical and rhetorical features on parallel human and LLM corpora (Llama 3 variants, GPT-4o): the gaps from human style are larger for instruction-tuned models than base models and persist as models scale; instruction-tuned models write in a noun-heavy, informationally dense style "even when prompted to match the style of informal speech and writing"; present participial clauses appear at 2 to 5 times the human rate: [Reinhart et al., arXiv 2410.16107](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)
- Shaib et al. (EMNLP 2024): models produce repeated part-of-speech "templates" more often than human references; 76% of template instances in model output also occur in pre-training data versus 35% for human text, and these templates survive fine-tuning and RLHF: [Shaib et al., EMNLP 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.368.pdf)

### Inferences
- Two layers cause the voice: pre-training supplies stock syntactic templates (Shaib), and preference tuning collapses the choice among them toward the most familiar one (Kirk, Zhang). Fixes therefore have to change either the distribution the model samples from (a different model, fine-tuning, a prompt that asks for a spread of options) or the selection step (a human picking the less typical option), not just the words.
- Because typicality bias is a tie-breaker among equally good answers, the generic version is often not "worse" by a rater's measure. The person has to supply the tie-breaker: their own taste, deciding which of several candidate phrasings is theirs.
- Practical implication: never accept the first draft of a sentence as the voice. Ask for several genuinely different versions (or use a Verbalized Sampling style prompt, section 5) and choose; the first sample is, by construction, the mode.
- Homogenisation studies (Padmakumar and He, Doshi and Hauser, Anderson) suggest the risk is greatest when the AI supplies ideas and content, not only phrasing. Someone who wants to sound like themselves should bring the content (the memory, the opinion, the detail) and use the AI for structure or polish, not the reverse.
- Reinhart shows that telling a tuned model to "write casually" does not remove the noun-heavy, participial-clause style. Instructions about register are weak; examples and editing do more (section 5).

### Gaps
- No study I found isolates how much of the AI voice is due to next-token prediction alone (base models) versus post-training; Reinhart and Padmakumar and He both indicate post-training is the larger contributor, but neither gives a clean split.
- The 54% to 62% cross-dataset typicality figure is unverified (secondary source only).

## 2. Where specific tics come from: raters, annotator dialects, safety and helpfulness training, sycophancy

### Takeaway
Many specific tics trace back to what preference data rewards: raters (and reward models trained on them) favour longer, more agreeable, more polished-sounding answers, and some favour particular words. The "delve" story points to rater preferences, with the Nigerian-annotator dialect explanation being a plausible but unproven hypothesis.

### Cited Findings
- Juzek and Ward (COLING 2025) identified 21 words, including "delve", whose rise in scientific abstracts they attribute to LLM use. They found no evidence that the overrepresentation comes from model architecture, algorithm choices or training data; their tests were consistent with RLHF playing a role, though not conclusive: [Juzek and Ward, COLING 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.426)
- A 2025 follow-up emulated the human-feedback procedure with Llama and showed that participants systematically prefer text variants containing certain of these words, tying preference-based training more directly to the overuse: [arXiv 2508.01930](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.01930v1)
- The annotator-dialect hypothesis: Alex Hern (Guardian TechScape, April 2024) proposed that "delve" is more common in Nigerian business English and that AI firms outsource feedback annotation to countries such as Nigeria with strong English and low wages. Reported via Simon Willison; I did not read the Guardian original: [Willison on Hern](https://simonwillison.net/2024/Apr/18/delve)
- Sharma et al. (Anthropic, ICLR 2024): five state-of-the-art assistants were consistently sycophantic across four free-form tasks; in existing preference data, responses that agree with the user's views are more likely to win; both humans and preference models prefer convincingly written sycophantic answers over correct ones "a non-negligible fraction of the time"; the authors conclude sycophancy is a general behaviour of RLHF models "likely driven in part by human preference judgments": [Sharma et al., arXiv 2310.13548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)
- OpenAI's April 2025 GPT-4o rollback: an update made the model noticeably more sycophantic ("validating doubts, fueling anger, urging impulsive actions"); sycophancy was not explicitly tracked in deployment evaluations, and the model shipped on positive user signals despite expert testers saying it "felt slightly off". A former OpenAI researcher noted it was the first time an extra reward from thumbs up and thumbs down data was used (secondhand): [Georgetown Law tech brief](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/tech-institute/insights/tech-brief-ai-sycophancy-openai/); [Simon Willison, May 2025](https://simonwillison.net/2025/May/2/)
- Singhal et al. (COLM 2024): much of the reward gain from RLHF is driven by longer responses; a purely length-based reward reproduces most RLHF improvements over SFT; reward models are "non-robust and easily influenced by length biases in preference data" and capture only superficial aspects of human preference: [Singhal et al., arXiv 2310.03716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03716)
- The Wikipedia editors' field guide documents style-level tells beyond words: undue emphasis on significance and legacy ("broader trends"), a promotional tone LLMs struggle to drop even when asked for encyclopedic style, negative parallelisms correcting imagined misconceptions, "superficial analyses" in trailing present-participle clauses, vague attributions ("weasel wording"), and formulaic "Challenges" sections ending in a vaguely positive assessment. Newer models are "more subtly positive" than older ones, which were "more blatantly positive": [Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)

### Inferences
- Length bias (Singhal) explains padding: restating the question, recap summaries at the end, "it is important to note" scaffolding. A practical counter is a hard length budget per piece and cutting every sentence that restates what was already said.
- Sycophancy and positivity (Sharma, OpenAI rollback, Wikipedia on promotional tone) mean an AI editor will tend to praise the draft and soften the writer's sharper opinions. Someone wanting their own voice should explicitly tell the model to keep their bluntness and not to "balance" a one-sided view they hold, and should treat the model's praise of a draft as low-information.
- The "significance" and upbeat-conclusion tics map onto what raters reward: answers that sound complete, helpful and positive. A human voice often ends without a moral. Deleting the last paragraph of an AI draft is a cheap, frequently correct edit (practitioner inference, not tested).
- Since the tics come from aggregate rater taste, the remedy is to replace the aggregate rater with one person's taste: their examples, their rejections, their edits.

### Gaps
- I found no primary study isolating safety training as the source of hedging and "on the other hand" balance specifically; the hedging link is plausible but in these notes rests only on the length and sycophancy work and the Wikipedia guide's observations.
- The Nigerian annotator hypothesis remains unproven; Juzek and Ward and the 2025 follow-up support "preference training" as the channel but do not identify whose dialect.
- I did not reach the OpenAI postmortem directly; its details here are secondhand.

## 3. Why AI fiction feels flat: missing lived detail, resolved tension, explained subtext, averaged styles

### Takeaway
Studies of AI fiction converge on the same causes: generic rather than specific detail, over-explanation of what should be implied, tidy single-track plots that resolve tension, and outputs that cluster together because they average many styles. Expert writers fail AI stories on originality and craft tests at several times the rate of professional fiction.

### Cited Findings
- Chakrabarty et al., "Art or Artifice?" (CHI 2024): a Torrance-derived test of 14 binary craft criteria (fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration); ten creative writers judged 48 stories; LLM stories passed 3 to 10 times fewer tests than professional stories; no LLM used as a judge correlated positively with the experts: [Chakrabarty et al., arXiv 2309.14556](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14556)
- Chakrabarty, Laban and Wu, "Can AI writing be salvaged?" (CHI 2025): professional writers edited 1,057 LLM paragraphs (8,035 edits, about 8 per paragraph) under a seven-part taxonomy: Cliche (17% of edits), Unnecessary or Redundant Exposition (18%), Purple Prose (more frequent in GPT-4o), Poor Sentence Structure (20%), Lack of Specificity and Detail, Awkward Word Choice and Phrasing (28%, the most common), Tense Inconsistency. GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Llama 3.1 70B did not differ in writing quality, pointing to shared limits across model families. Preference order: writer-edited above LLM-edited above raw LLM text: [Chakrabarty et al., arXiv 2409.14509](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509)
- The same paper's background summarises prior findings that LLM text is "hackneyed and rife with clichés, lacks nuance, subtext, and rhetorical complexity": [Chakrabarty et al., arXiv 2409.14509](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14509)
- StoryScope (Russell, Rajendhran, Iyyer, Wieting, April 2026; 61,608 stories from 10,272 prompts, each answered by a human and five LLMs): narrative features alone separate human from AI at 93.2% macro-F1. AI stories lean on heavy thematic explanation and tidy single-track plots; human stories show more morally ambiguous protagonist choices and more temporal complexity; AI stories cluster together while human stories vary more. Per-model fingerprints include flat event escalation (Claude), dream sequences (GPT) and external character description (Gemini): [StoryScope, arXiv 2604.03136](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03136v1)
- Begus (Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 2024): 250 crowdworker stories versus 80 GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 stories on the same Pygmalion prompt; AI stories occasionally had innovative twists but "less imaginative scenarios and rhetoric"; motifs such as loneliness, loss replaced by doppelgangers and societal disapproval appeared only in the human stories: [Begus, arXiv 2310.12902](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12902v2); [Harvard GSAS write-up](https://gsas.harvard.edu/news/gauging-pygmalion-effect)
- Doshi and Hauser and Padmakumar and He (section 1) show the same clustering effect at the level of whole populations of writers: [Doshi and Hauser](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532); [Padmakumar and He](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05196)

### Inferences
- "Explaining subtext" (StoryScope's thematic explanation, LAMP's redundant exposition) is the single most actionable fiction tic: cut any sentence that tells the reader what a scene meant or what a character felt after it was already shown.
- "Lack of specificity" is a data problem, not a prompt problem: the model has no memories. The specific detail (the street, the smell, the exact thing someone said) must come from the human. A story told in one person's voice needs that person's details fed in, otherwise the model fills the gap with the average detail.
- Begus's human-only motifs (loneliness, loss, disapproval) suggest AI defaults away from uncomfortable, unresolved material; keeping the real ending, even if unresolved, is part of keeping the person's voice.
- Model-specific fingerprints (StoryScope) mean switching models changes which tics appear but not that tics appear.
- LLMs as judges did not track expert taste (Art or Artifice), so asking the same model "does this sound human?" is a weak check; the person's own read-aloud test is better.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve exact TTCW pass rates per test or StoryScope's full 30-feature list; only the abstract-level summaries above.
- No study I found tests flatness for personal, non-fiction storytelling (memoir, a person telling their own anecdote) as opposed to invented fiction.

## 4. Why word blacklists fail

### Takeaway
The tells live at the level of syntax, structure and rhetoric, not vocabulary, so banning words leaves the underlying patterns intact and the overused words shift over time and across models. Research-grade banning works only with heavy machinery (backtracking samplers, targeted fine-tuning), and even its authors warn plain token banning breaks down.

### Cited Findings
- Reinhart et al.: the persistent differences are grammatical and rhetorical (noun density, participial clauses at 2 to 5 times human rates), which no word list touches: [Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)
- Shaib et al.: repetition shows up as part-of-speech templates that frequent n-gram (word) measures miss: [Shaib et al.](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.368.pdf)
- StoryScope: narrative-structure features alone keep over 97% of the detection performance of models that also use stylistic cues, so the "AI-ness" of fiction survives even if every word were human-like: [StoryScope](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03136v1)
- Wikipedia's guide: the overused words "have changed over time"; "delve" was heavy in 2023 to early 2024 and "dropped off sharply in 2025"; different chatbots overuse different words; models replace simple "is"/"are" with constructions like "serves as" and direct claims with vague links like "associated with". The guide warns that fixing surface symptoms only "could just make detection harder" while the deeper problems remain, and that a single listed word "may be coincidental": [Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)
- Geng and Trotta (Findings of ACL 2025): after "delve" was publicised as a ChatGPT word in early 2024, its frequency in arXiv abstracts dropped, along with several other flagged words, while others such as "significant" and "additionally" kept rising; they suggest authors adapt by choosing among or editing outputs: [Geng and Trotta, arXiv 2502.09606](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09606v2)
- Antislop (Paech et al., ICLR 2026): some slop patterns occur over 1,000 times more often in LLM output than in human text; plain token banning breaks down at around 2,000 patterns, so they built a backtracking sampler (rewind to where a banned string started, down-weight, resample) handling over 8,000 patterns, with a soft ban strength so a word can still appear when the prompt calls for it; their FTPO fine-tune reports 90% slop reduction while DPO "degrades writing quality and lexical diversity": [Antislop, arXiv 2510.15061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15061); [ICLR 2026 poster](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/poster/10008156); [Thoughtworks summary](https://research.thoughtworks.com/library/antislop-framework-identifying-eliminating-repetitive-patterns-language-models)

### Inferences
- A "never use: delve, tapestry, testament" list in a prompt treats the symptom; the model keeps the sentence shape and substitutes a neighbour. The Wikipedia guide's "serves as" for "is" example is exactly that kind of substitution at the model level.
- Word lists are still useful as a cheap last-pass detector (a lint step after drafting), as long as the fix is to rewrite the sentence in the person's own words rather than swap the word.
- Better targets for constraints are structural: no closing summary, no "not X but Y" reversals, no tricolons, no sentence that explains the previous one, no participial "-ing" tails that comment on significance. These map to the documented causes (Reinhart, Wikipedia, LAMP).
- The tic list is a moving target across model versions (Wikipedia, Geng and Trotta). A blacklist written for one model generation goes stale; examples of the target person's writing do not.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study that bans a word list in a prompt and measures near-synonym substitution directly; the substitution claim rests on Wikipedia's observational guide and on the Antislop authors' engineering rationale, not on a dedicated experiment I could read.
- I did not read the Antislop paper's own discussion of what replaces banned phrases after banning.

## 5. What reliably moves output away from the generic, and what is overrated

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence is for fine-tuning on the person's own corpus, which in one preregistered study flipped expert preference and made outputs nearly undetectable; few-shot examples help but leave a clear gap, especially for informal writing; temperature is a weak lever; prompts that ask for a distribution of answers recover diversity; human editing remains the most dependable fix for the residual tics.

### Cited Findings
- Chakrabarty, Ginsburg and Dhillon (2025, preregistered; MFA-trained experts and lay readers; blind pairwise comparisons with MFA writers imitating award-winning authors): with in-context prompting (20 sample excerpts from the author, style descriptions, detailed content specs), experts strongly preferred human text: odds ratio 0.16 for stylistic fidelity (95% CI 0.08 to 0.29) and 0.13 for quality (0.06 to 0.28). After fine-tuning GPT-4o on each author's complete works, experts preferred the AI: OR 8.16 for fidelity (4.69 to 14.2) and 1.87 for quality (1.16 to 3.02); lay readers 16.65 and 5.42: [Chakrabarty et al., arXiv 2510.13939 v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13939v4)
- Same study: the Pangram detector flagged 97% of in-context outputs as AI but only 3% of fine-tuned outputs (GPTZero: 91% detected in-context, fine-tuned evaded 100%). Cliche density mediated 16.4% of the detector effect on preference for in-context outputs but only 1.3% for fine-tuned ones, which the authors read as fine-tuning removing the detectable AI quirks. Median cost $81 per author (range $25 to $276), excluding human editing: [Chakrabarty et al. v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13939v4); [U-M School of Information news](https://si.umich.edu/about-umsi/news/when-ai-learns-authors-voice-even-experts-prefer-it)
- Version note: summaries describe the study as covering 50 authors, while v4 describes 30 author-specific fine-tuned models; figures differ slightly across arXiv versions: [search-level summary via arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13939v2); [v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13939v4)
- Wang, Tripto et al., "Catch Me If You Can? Not Yet" (2025): few-shot prompting with a handful of the author's samples plus a content summary beats zero-shot on every metric, but imitation of everyday authors stays weak, better for formal domains (news, email) than informal blogs and forums; going from 2 to 10 examples adds very little: [Wang et al., arXiv 2509.14543](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.14543). A secondary review reports blog authorship-verification accuracy around 17% to 21% at 5-shot and few-shot outputs still mostly flagged by GPTZero; not verified against the paper: [Moonlight review](https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/catch-me-if-you-can-not-yet-llms-still-struggle-to-imitate-the-implicit-writing-styles-of-everyday-authors)
- Peeperkorn et al. (ICCC 2024, best student paper): in a fixed narrative task, temperature was only weakly correlated with novelty, moderately correlated with incoherence, and unrelated to typicality or cohesion; the authors argue against treating temperature as "the creativity parameter": [Peeperkorn et al., arXiv 2405.00492](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.00492v1)
- Verbalized Sampling: asking the model for several responses with probabilities (for example "Generate 5 jokes about coffee and their corresponding probabilities") raised creative-writing diversity 1.6 to 2.1 times over direct prompting with comparable quality; human-rated diversity rose for poems (1.90 to 2.39), stories (2.74 to 3.06) and jokes (1.83 to 3.01); after DPO it retained about 66.8% of base-model diversity versus 23.8% for direct prompting; more capable models benefit more: [Zhang et al., arXiv 2510.01171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01171); [v2 full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01171v2)
- Editing passes: in LAMP, writer-edited text beat LLM-edited text, which beat raw LLM text; automatic editing "shows promise" but LLMs detecting their own problem spans reached only 0.46 general precision against 0.57 expert agreement: [Chakrabarty et al., arXiv 2409.14509](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509)
- Explicit style instructions are weak on their own: instruction-tuned models kept their noun-heavy style even when asked to match informal speech: [Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)
- Targeted training beats generic preference training for removing slop: Antislop's FTPO cut slop by 90% while DPO reduced quality and lexical diversity: [Antislop ICLR poster](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/poster/10008156)

### Inferences
- Ranking by evidence for "sound like one specific human": (1) fine-tuning on that person's real writing, strongest evidence, but needs a sizeable corpus and is the study of published authors, not casual writers; (2) the person's own edit pass on every draft, consistent evidence; (3) few-shot examples of the person's writing, real but limited gains, and more than a few examples adds little; (4) prompts that request several distinct options and let the person choose (Verbalized Sampling style), good for escaping the mode; (5) explicit style rules, weak alone; (6) raising temperature, overrated, mostly buys incoherence.
- For a person with little written corpus, the realistic path is: their spoken or chat words as raw material, a few examples of their actual messages as few-shot anchors, several candidates per passage, and a final pass where only the person's own sentences survive. Recording or transcribing how they tell a story out loud may be a better corpus than anything the model writes.
- Fine-tuning works partly by removing cliche density (the mediation result), which suggests the in-context equivalent is a cliche-hunting edit pass, not more instructions.
- The in-context condition in the strongest study already included 20 author excerpts plus style descriptions and still lost badly with experts; so "paste a few samples and say write like me" should be expected to produce text that experts and detectors still read as AI.
- Lay readers already rated in-context AI text as higher quality (OR 1.82) while experts rejected it; "it reads fine" from a casual reader is not evidence that it sounds like the person.

### Gaps
- No study found compares these levers head to head for an ordinary person's informal voice (chat messages, spoken storytelling) rather than published authors or everyday online text.
- The fine-tuning result rests on one model (GPT-4o) and literary authors with large corpora; I found no evidence on how small a corpus still works.
- Exact numbers for the "Catch Me If You Can" blog accuracy and GPTZero rates come from a secondary summary.
- I found no rigorous study of min-p or other newer samplers on perceived voice; only the temperature study above.
