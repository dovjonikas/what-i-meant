# Keeping one real person's voice when an AI helps with the writing

Scope: a creator who writes their story games in English themselves (native Lithuanian, casual and direct English), with an AI coding agent that builds the game and sometimes edits text. Researched 2026-10-29 with WebSearch and WebFetch only. Where a page could not be fetched, the note says so. Inferences are labelled as such and kept apart from cited findings.

## 1. How ghostwriters and editors capture and protect a voice, and what goes wrong when it gets "polished"

### Takeaway
Practitioners capture a voice from raw material (recorded, transcribed interviews and the person's earlier writing, read for rhythm, not content) and protect it by intervening as little as possible: fix errors and facts, do not reword prose just because it might "sound better". The documented failure is the editor's voice replacing the author's; the research on AI suggestions shows the same failure happening at scale, toward a generic, Western-default style.

### Cited Findings
- Memoir ghostwriting practice starts with a series of recorded and transcribed interviews; the writer then listens to the audio against the transcript and takes notes. One practitioner says the interview is also for "tuning your ear" to the client's modes of speech, and that reproducing a storyteller's voice requires either doing the recordings yourself or listening closely to someone else's. The same writer uses AI transcription but re-checks every transcript by hand because AI cannot manage every accent ([LifeBook Memoirs, "When Ghostwriters Hear Voices"](https://www.lifebookmemoirs.com/en-gb/when-ghostwriters-hear-voices/), via search snippets; practitioner company blog).
- An executive ghostwriting method: never interview cold; read everything the client has written before, listening for rhythm rather than content, and note recurring phrases, sentence length, and whether the client asks questions or makes statements ([ghostwriting-blog.pages.dev, voice capture method 2026](https://ghostwriting-blog.pages.dev/posts/voice-capture-interview-method-2026.html), via search snippet; low-authority blog, treat as practitioner opinion).
- Andrew Crofts, a prolific British ghostwriter, describes his method as detached listening and stresses curiosity, the need to keep asking questions ([As Told To podcast, episode 55](https://goodpods.com/podcasts/as-told-to-246003/episode-55-andrew-crofts-40940060); [WriterAccess podcast summary](https://www.writeraccess.com/blog/a-really-great-adventure-andrew-crofts-discusses-ghostwriting-on-the-writeraccess-podcast/)), via search snippets. No direct Crofts quote on "voice" was found.
- J.R. Moehringer (ghostwriter of Prince Harry's *Spare*) wrote a New Yorker essay, "The Ghostwriter" (May 2023), describing a 2 a.m. fight: Harry wanted to end the captivity-exercise scene with a comeback line to his captors to show he "had his wits about him"; Moehringer thought it unnecessary and argued against it; Harry eventually conceded. Moehringer credits himself with the advice to "trust the book" ([NBC Washington coverage](https://www.nbcwashington.com/entertainment/entertainment-news/prince-harrys-spare-ghostwriter-recalls-shouting-at-him-amid-difficult-edits/3344744/)). The New Yorker page itself could not be fetched (tool blocked), so the essay's fuller remarks on voice are not verified here.
- Copyediting guidance: leave as few traces as possible; a heavy editorial hand is a sign the author's voice was lost; prioritise grammar, spelling, syntax and consistency over reshaping ([Gareth Dyke, "Understanding the Author's Voice"](https://garethdyke.substack.com/p/understanding-the-authors-voice-and)).
- Resist rewording a passage just because it might "sound better"; unless a change adds clarity, the editor's voice should yield to the author's ([BookMachine, 5 copyediting tips](https://bookmachine.org/2016/07/07/the-world-of-words-5-copyediting-tips)).
- Newsroom version of the same rule: check facts and fix grammar, but do not flatten a writer's distinctive style ([Western Producer, "Writer's voice must always be respected"](https://www.producer.com/opinion/writers-voice-must-always-be-respected)).
- Counterpoint: an Editors Canada blog argues "respect" for the author can be overdone and editors sometimes must save authors from weak writing, but every change must be justifiable ([Editors Canada blog](https://blog.editors.ca/?p=4886)). An editor who taught copyediting adds that not every author's voice needs to be polished or "beautiful" ([Lev Raphael, Substack comments](https://levraphael.substack.com/p/finding-the-right-editor/comments)).
- Polishing by AI shifts style, not just content: in a CHI 2025 experiment with 118 participants from India and the US, AI suggestions led Indian participants to adopt Western writing styles and to describe their own food and festivals "from a Western gaze"; efficiency gains were also larger for Americans ([Agarwal, Naaman, Vashistha, CHI 2025, arXiv 2409.11360](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11360); [Cornell News](https://news.cornell.edu/node/330798)).

### Inferences
- The ghostwriter's raw material for this project already exists: the author's own chat messages, voice-note transcripts and earlier game text. That corpus plays the role of the interview transcripts. The agent should quote from it, not invent "the author's kind of line".
- The Moehringer episode shows an editor may argue, hard, but the decision and the name on the work stay with the author. For an agent: flag and explain, never silently decide.
- "Polish" is the failure mode named by editors and measured by the HCI work: every unjustified smoothing moves text toward the model's default register.

### Gaps
- No written ghostwriter or editor source was found that describes a formal per-client "voice style sheet" (as distinct from the copyediting style sheet for spellings and usage). The practice is inferred from the interview and rhythm-noting methods above.
- The New Yorker essay could not be read directly.

## 2. How game and narrative writers keep a character or author voice consistent

### Takeaway
Game studios hold voice steady with a written house guide (word caps, banned clichés, a spoken-aloud test for dialogue), with per-character word lists (ten words the character says, ten they never would), and by auditing for generic lines that any character could say. Supergiant keeps one writer on all text, and ties voice to reacting to what the player just did.

### Cited Findings
- Failbetter's Fallen London writer guidelines (Part III) set hard word caps: root descriptions 30 words, branch descriptions 20, results 100, with exceptions only a couple of times per commission ([Failbetter Games, Writer Guidelines Part III](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/fallen-london-writer-guidelines-part-iii)).
- The same guide requires direct speech to pass the "say-this-shit test", credited to Harrison Ford's remark to George Lucas that you can write a line but cannot say it; direct speech is preferred over reported speech ([Failbetter, Part III](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/fallen-london-writer-guidelines-part-iii)).
- Failbetter also: do not tell the player what they feel ("Don't tell them they're scared; scare them"); vague phrases like "all across London" signal telling; named clichés to avoid (diaphanous gowns, black as pitch, "Too quiet."); single-sentence paragraphs used only for pacing read as precious; period style is "seasoning, not an ingredient"; the guide exists to keep "a consistent tone and quality" across many writers ([Failbetter, Part III](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/fallen-london-writer-guidelines-part-iii)).
- Alexis Kennedy (Fallen London, Cultist Simulator) said Fallen London started very concise but snippet word counts crept up as writers tried to deliver value, which is why Cultist Simulator was meant to be "pithy, minimal and evocative" ([GameDaily.biz interview, "Value pithiness over lushness"](https://gamedaily.biz/?p=1750), via search snippet).
- Richard Dansky's technique (from *The Video Game Writer's Guide to Surviving an Industry That Hates You*): write ten words the character likes to say and ten they would never say, and write from those lists. Alex Epstein reports an editor found about 20% of a few hundred lines he wrote for a character's sound set were generic, lines any character could say ([Alex Epstein, Complications Ensue, Aug 2025](https://complicationsensue.blogspot.com/2025/08/danskys-book.html)).
- A bark is a brief line triggered by an event or player action; every bark should respond to its trigger, give the player some information, and accurately reflect the speaking character ([How To Write a Game, "How To Write Video Game Barks"](https://howtowriteagame.substack.com/p/how-to-write-video-game-barks), via search snippet). Bark tools store lines as data with weights and conditions and export to CSV for spreadsheet review and voice-actor scripts ([Talenode docs, bark dialogue](https://talenode.readthedocs.io/en/latest/user-guide/bark-dialogue/)).
- Greg Kasavin does the writing for Supergiant's games ([RPG Site interview 2026](https://www.rpgsite.net/interview/20348-supergiant-games-interview-2026-greg-kasavin-darren-korb-hades-2-ending-music-future-coffee)). For Hades he focused writing on characters with clear personalities who comment on the player's behaviour, acknowledging small aspects of what the player did, which he felt made them more alive ([ScreenHub on Kasavin's LudoNarraCon talk](https://www.screenhub.com.au/news-article/features/digital/jini-maxwell/hades-greg-kasavin-breaks-down-supergiants-unique-approach-to-narrative-262459)). A GDC talk with audio director Darren Korb covers finding voices for the characters through recording and implementation ([GDC Vault listing](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1026975/contactUs), description only).

### Inferences
- The project's equivalent of a voice bible is small and concrete: (a) a file of the author's real sentences, unedited; (b) the author's "words I use / words I never use" lists in the Dansky form; (c) a few house rules in the Failbetter form (length caps per text type, banned phrases, a say-it-aloud test).
- The 20% generic-line audit transfers directly: for any line the agent touched, ask whether another game or another person could have written it word for word.
- Because one person writes everything (the Kasavin model), consistency comes from the author's corpus, not from a style the agent infers. The agent should not "even out" variation between the author's lines.

### Gaps
- No GDC talk transcript specifically on "voice bibles" or "bark sheets" was retrieved; the GDC evidence is listing-level only. Disco Elysium and other studios' voice documents were not found in this pass.

## 3. Non-native English as a voice: when to correct and when to leave it

### Takeaway
Second-language writing scholarship treats a writer's other language as a resource, not a deficit, and warns that "native check" gatekeeping erases authentic voice; at the same time it acknowledges real errors still need fixing. LLMs specifically over-correct (they rewrite toward fluency rather than making minimal edits) and pull non-Western writers toward Western norms, so the default AI behaviour is the wrong one for this writer.

### Cited Findings
- Translingual scholarship challenges monolingual writing norms and treats additional languages and varieties as resources; it argues for writer agency in shaping their own language. It is contested: critics raise concerns about pedagogy and language development ([Yachao Sun, Purdue dissertation, "A Translingual Approach and its Implications for L2 Writing"](https://hammer.purdue.edu/articles/thesis/A_Translingual_Approach_and_Its_Implications_for_L2_Writing/12170121)).
- A study of three plurilingual writers in Toronto described their translanguaging on five dimensions: lexical, syntactic, rhetorical, conceptual, presentational, which supports treating some "non-standard" phrasing as deliberate ([TESL Canada Journal](https://teslcanadajournal.ca/index.php/tesl/article/download/1473/1311/2089), via search snippet).
- A Japanese ELT chapter argues that gatekeepers (native-check services, co-authors, reviewers, editors, proofreaders) tend to enforce standard English and can erase the authentic voice of non-native writers ([Kobe City University of Foreign Studies annual report](https://kobe-cufs.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2619/files/nenpo62-03.pdf), via search snippet).
- The CCCC Statement on Second Language Writing and Multilingual Writers (2001, revised 2020) frames campuses as multilingual spaces and notes discourse and audience expectations vary across contexts ([CCCC statement](https://cccc.ncte.org/?p=281); [NCTE print version](https://ncte.org/statement/secondlangwriting/print/)). Its specific editing guidance on voice was not retrieved.
- LLM correction departs from minimal editing: Fang et al. found ChatGPT's grammatical error corrections very fluent but with a noticeable tendency to over-correct, especially on short sentences, and weak on cross-sentence agreement, coreference and tense ([Fang et al., arXiv 2304.01746](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01746v1)). These were GPT-3.5-era models.
- AI suggestions homogenised Indian writers' style toward Western norms (CHI 2025, 118 participants) ([Agarwal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11360)).
- Common GPT detectors consistently misclassified non-native English writing as AI-generated while classifying native writing correctly; the authors link this to penalising more limited linguistic range ([Liang et al., "GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers", arXiv 2304.02819](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819); [Stanford listing](https://scale.stanford.edu/publications/gpt-detectors-are-biased-against-non-native-english-writers)).
- Lithuanian-specific: Nangle et al. (2024) studied the most common L1 interference errors in written English by Lithuanian students ([Sustainable Multilingualism, DOI 10.2478/sm-2024-0004](https://ejournals.vdu.lt/index.php/SM/article/view/4892)); Daukšaitė (2019) studied negative transfer in Lithuanian students' English forum posts ([lituanistika.lt](https://www.lituanistika.lt/content/21003)). The snippets did not show which error categories dominated.

### Inferences
- Correct (real errors, a native reader stumbles): spelling and typos; a missing or wrong article where it changes or blurs meaning; subject-verb agreement; tense that contradicts the scene; a wrong preposition that changes meaning; a word that means something else in English (false friend). Each correction should be the smallest change that fixes it.
- Leave (style): short blunt sentences; direct statements with no softening; slang and swearing; unusual but understandable word order; literal idioms that carry meaning; repeated words the author chose; missing transitions; no hedging. These are what make the text sound like a person, and they are exactly what an LLM "improves" first (Fang et al., Agarwal et al.).
- Lithuanian has no articles, so article slips are the most likely real error class (background knowledge, not established by the sources found here). Even then, a dropped article in casual dialogue may be voice; ask rather than fix when it is inside a character's speech.
- Never run the author's own text through an AI detector to judge it: the Liang result predicts false flags on exactly this kind of writer.

### Gaps
- No source found that gives an editor's protocol specifically for keeping a second-language writer's phrasing in fiction or games. The correct/leave split above is an inference from copyediting practice plus the GEC and HCI findings.
- No study retrieved that names the dominant error types for Lithuanian writers of English; the article claim is unverified background knowledge.

## 4. AI-assisted workflows that keep the human voice, and what studies say about loss of voice and ownership

### Takeaway
The evidence is consistent: the more text the AI generates, the more homogeneous the result and the weaker the writer's sense of ownership; writers feel the work is theirs when they make the key decisions ("content gatekeeping"). So the workflow that protects voice is: the author writes first, the agent only flags or makes minimal, shown edits, and the author decides.

### Cited Findings
- Writing with InstructGPT (not base GPT-3) significantly reduced diversity: essays became more alike across authors with lower lexical and content diversity, driven mainly by the model-generated text; the human-written parts were unaffected ([Padmakumar and He, ICLR 2024, arXiv 2309.05196](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05196)).
- With AI story ideas, stories were rated more creative and better written (especially for less creative writers) but were more similar to each other; individually better, collectively narrower. 293 writers, 600 evaluators ([Doshi and Hauser, Science Advances, July 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532); [Dryad data](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.qfttdz0pm); [PsyPost](https://psypost.org/new-study-on-ai-assisted-creativity-reveals-an-interesting-social-dilemma)).
- "AI Ghostwriter Effect": across two studies (30 and 96 participants), people did not feel they owned AI-generated text yet still declared themselves authors; more personal influence on the text raised ownership; personalisation did not change the effect ([Draxler et al., arXiv 2303.03283, ACM TOCHI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03283v2); [LMU press release](https://www.lmu.de/en/newsroom/news-overview/news/who-wrote-it-the-ai-ghostwriter-effect-80ece661.html)).
- Next-paragraph AI suggestions improved essay quality and speed but moderately decreased ownership and satisfaction (131 participants, CHI 2024) ([Dhillon et al., arXiv 2402.11723](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11723)).
- 19 professional writers co-writing with personalised and non-personalised tools located authenticity in the process, not the final text; they counted contribution as "content gatekeeping", choosing what enters the text, rather than word count; they feared AI could shift them away from their usual tone and voice; writers who began with a clear vision felt better protected; 30 avid readers could not tell AI-assisted from solo work ([Hwang, Liao, Blodgett, Olteanu, Trischler, "It was 80% me, 20% AI", CSCW 2025, arXiv 2411.13032](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.13032v1)).
- An MIT Media Lab preprint (54 students, LLM vs search vs brain-only) reported lower felt ownership and more homogeneous vocabulary and structure in the LLM group; it was not peer reviewed at the time and the sample is small ([LIRNEasia copy of the paper](https://lirneasia.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Your-Brain-on-ChatGPT_Final.pdf); [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/mit-study-shows-cognitive-debt-through-chatgpt-heres-what-it-means-in-real-world-practice/); [Time via Pitt](https://www.research.pitt.edu/sites/default/files/assets/ChatGPT%20May%20Be%20Eroding%20Critical%20Thinking%20Skills%2C%20According%20to%20a%20New%20MIT%20Study.pdf)). Precise percentages circulating in blogs were not verified.
- The blader/humanizer Claude Code skill (MIT licence) rewrites text against a catalogue of patterns drawn from Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" and lets the user supply a few paragraphs of their own writing so the rewrite follows their rhythm and word choices ([blader/humanizer mirror](https://github.com/c-b-g-m/blader-humanizer); [wey-gu fork](https://github.com/wey-gu/humanizer); [Gigazine, May 2026](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20260530-humanizer)).
- Wikipedia's AI Cleanup guide warns against treating the signs as "the problems to be fixed", since stripping tells only makes detection harder without fixing the underlying text ([Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)).

### Inferences

#### What the agent must never do with the author's text
- Never write story text, dialogue, choices or endings in the author's voice from scratch, and never "fill in" a missing line with its own. If a line is needed, leave a visible placeholder and ask.
- Never rewrite for flow, tone, rhythm, "clarity" or "impact"; never merge, split or reorder the author's sentences; never add transitions, hedges, adjectives, metaphors, feelings, morals or summary lines.
- Never swap the author's words for "better" synonyms, never make the English more standard or more formal, never Westernise Lithuanian names, places, food or references (Agarwal et al.).
- Never edit silently: no change to the author's text without showing old and new.
- Never run the author's text through an AI detector or a humanizer and treat the score as a verdict (Liang et al.; Wikipedia guide).

#### What the agent may do
- Flag, not fix: list possible real errors (typo, spelling, agreement, tense, a missing article that blurs meaning, a false friend, a game-state contradiction such as a name or item that does not exist) with line, quote, the smallest proposed fix and a one-line reason; the author accepts or rejects each.
- Apply only typo-class fixes directly when the author has said so for that file, and show the diff.
- Mechanics around the text: put the author's text into data files byte for byte, wire it, check lengths against the UI, find duplicated or orphaned lines, check consistency of names and terms across scenes, build a list of the author's recurring words.
- Ask questions that help the author write (what does this character want here, what did the player just do), in the Hwang et al. "help me think, do not become the text" zone.
- When the author explicitly asks for a draft, label it DRAFT, keep it outside the shipped text, and mark every line as the agent's until the author rewrites it in the author's own words.

#### Review checklist before any text ships (run on every line the agent touched, and on new text the author asks it to check)
1. Diff test: every changed word maps to a named real error. Anything without a reason is reverted.
2. Length test: the edited line is not longer than the author's original. Fixes rarely add words.
3. Say-it-aloud test (Failbetter): read the line out; if the author would not say it to a friend, it fails.
4. Would-the-author-say-this test: compare with the author's corpus and the author's "never says" list (Dansky). Any word from the never list fails.
5. Generic-line test (Epstein's 20% audit): could this line appear unchanged in another game or another person's text? If yes, flag it to the author.
6. AI vocabulary scan (Wikipedia list): delve, tapestry, testament, pivotal, crucial, vital, underscore, highlight, intricate, showcase, landscape, foster, align with, additionally, vibrant, rich, profound, nestled, groundbreaking, boasts, serves as, stands as, a reminder that. One hit is a question; several in one passage is a strong tell.
7. Structure scan: "not X, but Y" or "it's not X, it's Y"; tidy lists of three; trailing "-ing" clauses that add meaning-sounding commentary (highlighting, reflecting, ensuring); a closing line that sums up or states the moral; vague attribution (some say, experts argue).
8. Formatting scan: em or en dashes, curly quotes, title-case headings, bold labels, emoji used as formatting.
9. Feeling test (Failbetter): does the line tell the player what they feel instead of showing it? Did the edit add an emotion the author did not write?
10. Culture test (Agarwal et al.): did any Lithuanian or personal detail become a generic or Western one?
11. Ownership test (Hwang et al.; Draxler et al.): can the author point at every line and say they chose it? If not, it goes back to the author.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found that directly compares "human writes first, AI only flags" against AI-drafting for voice preservation; the recommendation rests on the dose-response pattern across Padmakumar and He, Doshi and Hauser, Dhillon et al., and Draxler et al.
- No source on showing diffs specifically as a voice-protection practice was retrieved; it is an inference from the copyediting "leave few traces" principle and the EditLens finding (section 6) that the degree of AI editing is measurable.

## 5. Plain technical writing for the agent's own README, comments, commit messages and docs

### Takeaway
The established guides already describe the human version: commit subjects in the imperative that say what changes and a body that says why; comments that explain why and never restate code; a README that says what the thing is and why it exists, how to run it, and stops; a tone that is friendly and plain, without exclamation marks, "simply", "please note" or "let's". The AI flavour in repos (emoji headings, bold-label feature lists, "not X, just Y" lines, uniform sections, everything summarised) is the same set of tells Wikipedia lists for prose.

### Cited Findings
- Chris Beams's seven rules: blank line between subject and body; subject at most 50 characters; capitalise the subject; no period at its end; imperative mood; wrap the body at 72; use the body to explain what and why, not how. Test: the subject should complete "If applied, this commit will ..."; the diff shows what changed, only the message can say why ([cbea.ms, How to Write a Git Commit Message](https://cbea.ms/git-commit/)).
- Linux kernel: describe changes in imperative mood ("make xyzzy do frotz", not "[This patch] makes xyzzy do frotz"); explain the underlying problem that motivated the change. The kernel now asks contributors who used an AI coding tool to acknowledge it with an Assisted-by tag ([kernel.org, Submitting patches](https://docs.kernel.org/process/submitting-patches.html)).
- Conventional Commits: `<type>[optional scope]: <description>`, optional body and footers; `fix` and `feat` as the main types; breaking changes marked with `!` or a `BREAKING CHANGE:` footer ([Conventional Commits 1.0.0](https://www.conventionalcommits.org/en/v1.0.0/)).
- Code comments, nine rules including: comments should not duplicate the code; good comments do not excuse unclear code; explain unidiomatic code; link the source of copied code; add comments when fixing bugs; comments that add no information have negative value. Citing Jeff Atwood: code tells you how, comments tell you why ([Stack Overflow Blog, Best practices for writing code comments](https://stackoverflow.blog/2021/12/23/best-practices-for-writing-code-comments/)).
- Google developer documentation style: conversational, friendly and respectful, like a knowledgeable friend; not the way you speak (speech is wordier); avoid buzzwords and jargon, exclamation points, "please" in instructions, "simply", "it's easy", "quickly", "let's", "please note", being cutesy or super-entertaining ([Google developer documentation style guide, Voice and tone](https://developers.google.com/style/tone)).
- Write the Docs: a README should say what the project does and why it exists, give a small example, install steps in a couple of lines, where to file issues, how to get help, how to contribute, and the licence; "start simple"; give users what they need "but not too much"; it should make sense as plain text ([Write the Docs, A beginner's guide to writing documentation](https://www.writethedocs.org/guide/writing/beginners-guide-to-docs/)).
- AI-generated READMEs: the problem is that they look finished before anyone has edited them ([HackerNoon, "Treat AI-generated READMEs like first drafts"](https://hackernoon.com/treat-ai-generated-readmes-like-first-drafts)); emoji bullets, em dashes and generic openers are named as tells, and posting raw output unread is the real tell ([DEV Community, "Your AI slop bores me"](https://dev.to/eschmechel/your-ai-slop-bores-me-4g7k)); Hacker News commenters flagged "no X just Y" and "isn't A, it's B" wording, uniform sections and emoji everywhere as LLM signs ([HN thread mirror](https://hn.nuxt.dev/item/46580506)). Even the readme-ai generator says it is vital to review all generated text ([readme-ai on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/readmeai/0.5.3)). These are opinion and forum sources, not measurements.
- Wikipedia's tells that carry over to repo docs: title-case headings, overuse of bold, bulleted lists that open with bold labels, emoji as formatting, Markdown where it does not belong, skipped heading levels, and edit summaries with canned claims of having "preserved" information or followed policy ([Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)).

### Inferences
- Commit messages: imperative subject that names the change in the game's terms ("Fix the ending choice that skipped the last scene"), body only when the why is not obvious, two to four plain sentences, no bullet dump of every file touched, no "This commit", no "comprehensive", "robust", "seamless", "enhance", "streamline", no emoji, no summary of the summary. Follow whatever attribution line the repo already uses.
- Comments: only where the code cannot say it: why a value is what it is, a workaround and what it works around, a link to the source of borrowed code, a TODO with its condition. No comments that narrate the next line, no section banners, no docstrings that restate the function name.
- README: what the game is in one or two sentences in the author's framing, how to run it, how the files are laid out if non-obvious, what is unfinished. No "Features" list with bold labels, no badges or emoji, no "Why X?" marketing section, no closing "Contributing / Happy coding" boilerplate unless the author wants contributors.
- Docs and notes the agent writes: sentence-case headings, short paragraphs, plain "is" and "has" instead of "serves as" and "boasts", no rule-of-three padding, no "In summary". State uncertainty plainly once instead of hedging every sentence.
- Keep a hard line between the author's voice (story text, the author's README framing if the author writes it) and the agent's voice (engineering text). The agent's plain voice must never leak into the author's story text, and the author's story voice should not be imitated in the agent's commits.

### Gaps
- No measured study of AI-written commit messages or code comments was found; the AI-pattern list for repos is drawn from prose tell catalogues and developer opinion.
- Google's Technical Writing One course pages were not fetched; the style guide's tone page was used instead.

## 6. 2025 to 2026 tools and checklists that detect AI-sounding phrasing for editing, and how reliable they are

### Takeaway
The most useful editing aid is a checklist, not a classifier: Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" (with the humanizer skill built on it) and frequent-LLM-user judgment are good at spotting the patterns; automated detectors have real error rates and are biased against non-native writers. New research tools measure how much AI editing a text received (EditLens) or suppress slop at generation time (Antislop), but neither is a voice check.

### Cited Findings
- Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing (WikiProject AI Cleanup) is a descriptive field guide, explicitly "not prescriptive" and not community-reviewed policy. As fetched in October 2026 it groups tells into content (undue significance, promotional words, superficial "-ing" analysis, vague attribution, formulaic "challenges" endings), language (an "AI vocabulary" list that shifts by model era, avoiding plain "is/has", negative parallelisms, rule of three), style and formatting (title case, bold, bold-label lists, emoji, curly quotes, Markdown), and leftover chatbot communication. A cluster of AI vocabulary in one edit is called one of the strongest tells, while a single word may be coincidence. In the fetched version, em-dash overuse and elegant variation (synonym cycling) are filed as historical indicators (em dashes listed as 2022 to September 2026), not current ones ([Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing); [TechCrunch, Nov 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/11/20/the-best-guide-to-spotting-ai-writing-comes-from-wikipedia/)).
- The same page: do not rely solely on AI detectors, which have non-trivial error rates; humans in general are bad at telling human from LLM text (it cites a 2025 study at chance level); human writing is drifting toward LLM style, so the signs weaken over time ([Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)).
- Contrasting finding: people who frequently use ChatGPT for writing are accurate and robust detectors; a majority vote of five such annotators misclassified 1 of 300 articles, beating most commercial and open-source detectors tested, including against paraphrasing and humanizing. Their explanations relied on AI vocabulary plus harder-to-measure traits such as formality, originality and clarity ([Russell, Karpinska, Iyyer, ACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.267)). This conflicts with the "humans at chance" claim only on the surface: expertise with LLM output is the difference.
- Detector bias: GPT detectors consistently misclassified non-native English writing as AI-generated ([Liang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819)).
- EditLens (Pangram Labs with Mohit Iyyer; arXiv Oct 2025, ICLR 2026) predicts how much AI editing a text received, from the edited text alone; reported F1 94.7% binary and 90.4% for human / AI / mixed (another summary gives 95.6% binary, so figures vary by source); includes a Grammarly-edits case study; code at pangramlabs/EditLens ([arXiv 2510.03154](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.03154v1); [ICLR 2026 poster](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/poster/10008153); [papernotes summary](https://en.papernotes.org/ICLR2026/aigc_detection/editlens_quantifying_the_extent_of_ai_editing_in_text/)).
- Antislop (Paech et al., ICLR 2026) profiles model-specific "slop" (over-used phrases) against human baselines and suppresses over 8,000 patterns at inference time, with a fine-tuning method reported to cut slop by 90% without quality loss; MIT-licensed code ([arXiv 2510.15061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15061); [ICLR 2026 listing](https://mlanthology.org/iclr/2026/paech2026iclr-antislop/)).
- blader/humanizer (Claude Code skill) operationalises the Wikipedia list as a rewrite pass, with a voice sample option; sources disagree on its pattern count (24 to 25 in the README history, 29 per Gigazine), so it is changing quickly ([mirror](https://github.com/c-b-g-m/blader-humanizer); [Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20260530-humanizer)). A separate project, rbaddam/humanizer, adds modes for READMEs, design docs, code comments and emails ([rbaddam/humanizer](https://github.com/rbaddam/humanizer)).

### Inferences
- For this project the right tool is a grep-able checklist (section 4, items 6 to 8) run by the agent on its own edits, plus the author's ear. Detectors should not be used on the author's writing at all, and a humanizer should not be run on the author's text, because a rewrite pass is itself the voice-loss problem.
- A humanizer-style pass is reasonable on the agent's own repo prose (README, docs, commits), where there is no human voice to lose, as long as the output is checked against the plain-writing rules in section 5.
- EditLens-type scoring could in principle measure "how much did the agent change the author's text", but the simpler and exact measure is the diff against the author's original.

### Gaps
- No independent 2026 benchmark of commercial detectors (Pangram, GPTZero and similar) was retrieved in this pass, so current false-positive rates are not reported here.
- The 2025 study the Wikipedia page cites for "humans at chance" was not identified by name in the fetched text.
- The Wikipedia page is long (over 250,000 characters) and only the first 100,000 characters were read; the "collaborative communication" and "signs of human writing" sections were seen only as headings.
