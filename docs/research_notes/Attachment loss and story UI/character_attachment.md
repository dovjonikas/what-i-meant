# What makes players and readers attach to characters (people and pets) in short narrative games

Scope: mechanisms of attachment to fictional characters, how much setup a loss needs, pets, and faceless or minimal characters. Each section separates sourced findings from inference. Design implications for "Text me when you get home" (20 to 30 minute phone story, faceless one-colour characters, life lines, chats, captions, a few choices; deaths at 12, 16, 29, 58, 66) are in the Inferences blocks and are inference, not evidence.

## 1. Which psychological mechanisms build attachment to fictional characters fast?

### Takeaway
The best-supported levers are not screen time but intensity of the felt relationship: being addressed directly, perceived similarity and attraction, identification with the character's goals, escalating disclosure, a single specific named individual, and the person's own investment (effort, care, choices). Pure repeated exposure helps a little and saturates; doing things for someone and sharing laughter or rituals add closeness, but some of those effects rest on small or older studies.

### Cited Findings

**Parasocial interaction (PSI) and parasocial relationships (PSR)**
- Character attachment in games is treated in game studies as an extension of classic parasocial interaction (Horton and Wohl 1956) from non-interactive media. - [Mao, DiGRA extended abstract citing Bopp et al. 2019](https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/2787/2771/2830)
- Hartmann and Goldhoorn (2011), 2 x 2 between-subjects experiment, N = 198: viewers reported a more intense parasocial experience when a TV performer addressed them both bodily and verbally. Their EPSI scale defines PSI as an intuitive feeling of mutual awareness, mutual attention and mutual adjustment during exposure. - [Horton and Wohl revisited (Journal of Communication 61(6))](https://typeset.io/papers/horton-and-wohl-revisited-exploring-viewers-experience-of-2x3qk052xt); [EPSI scale description](https://psytests.org/cyber/epasien.html)
- Schramm and Hartmann (2008) built the PSI-Process Scales as a post-exposure measure of PSI (the in-the-moment process) as distinct from PSR (the lasting relationship). - [Communications, De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/COMM.2008.025/html)
- Tukachinsky, Walter and Saucier (2020) meta-analysis of about 120 studies over four decades: no significant link between social deficiency (loneliness etc.) and PSRs, so PSRs are an add-on to real relationships, not a substitute; PSRs are strongly associated with homophily (perceived similarity), identification and transportation; PSI and PSR correlate only moderately and should be kept apart; PSR is the stronger predictor of media involvement. - [Journal of Communication 70(6), record](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/40aDeVml); [Chapman repository](https://digitalcommons.chapman.edu/comm_articles/53/)
- Branch, Wilson and Agnew (2013), two studies: Rusbult's investment model predicts commitment to TV characters. Satisfaction and investment predicted commitment for both fictional (Homer Simpson, House) and real (Oprah) figures; having alternatives lowered commitment only for fictional characters. - [Purdue e-Pubs abstract](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/psychpubs/63); [full text PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/77936666.pdf)

**Identification**
- Cohen (2001), "Defining identification" (Mass Communication & Society 4(3)): identification is experiencing the text "from the inside", as if events happen to you, and is distinct from liking, similarity, imitation and parasocial interaction. Secondary descriptions say it has an affective-empathy component and a cognitive one in which the viewer takes on the character's goals and point of view, and that Cohen saw it as temporary and varying in intensity during a programme. - [APA call for papers on the 25th anniversary of Cohen 2001](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/ppm/getting-involved); [secondary summary](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2129552065)
- Tal-Or and Cohen (2010) showed empirically that identification and transportation are distinct experiences. - [secondary summary via synapsesocial](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2129552065)

**Mere exposure (repeated contact)**
- Bornstein's 1989 meta-analysis (208 experiments, Psychological Bulletin 106(2)) found an overall mere-exposure effect of r = 0.26. Secondary summaries say the effect typically peaks within 10 to 20 presentations and can decline with longer series, and is strongest for unfamiliar stimuli shown briefly. - [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mere-exposure_effect); [atticusli.com summary with citation](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/mere-exposure-effect/) (secondary; the 10 to 20 figure was not verified against Bornstein's paper)
- In Cohen's 2004 survey (N = 381), hours of TV viewing correlated only weakly with PSI (r = .150) and with anticipated breakup distress (r = .153), while PSI intensity correlated with breakup distress at r = .58, and that correlation did not change when viewing hours were controlled. - [Cohen 2004, Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, full PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/culture/2004-cohen.pdf)

**Specificity: one named, pictured individual**
- Kogut and Ritov (2005): a single identified victim (with picture and name) drew considerably more contributions than a single unidentified one; identifying the individual members of a group of eight had essentially no effect. Reported distress was highest for the single identified victim. - [Kogut and Ritov PDF](https://pluto.huji.ac.il/~msiritov/KogutRitovIdentified.pdf); [Hebrew University record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-identified-victim-effect-an-identified-group-or-just-a-single-2/)
- Darling, Nandy and Breazeal (MIT Media Lab): participants asked to strike small HEXBUG robots hesitated longer when the bug had a personified backstory (e.g. "Frank", friendly, easily distracted, a longtime lab resident who likes to play and sometimes escapes but never gets far). People high in empathic concern hesitated most; Darling said they "didn't much care about the object when it was just an object". - [MIT record, "Empathic concern and the effect of stories in human-robot interaction"](https://libmast.utm.my/Record/mit-1721.1%2F109059); [Yes! Magazine interview](https://www.yesmagazine.org/health-happiness/2015/07/15/should-we-be-kind-to-robots-kate-darling)
- Ken Wong (Florence): the two leads began as blank slates with ambiguous personalities; specificity gave the story more to work with. - [Game Informer, GDC 2019](https://gameinformer.com/gdc-2019/2019/03/20/florence-started-development-as-3d-rubiks-cube-of-human-heads)

**Disclosure and vulnerability**
- Aron et al. (1997): a 45-minute procedure of gradually escalating self-disclosure produced more closeness between strangers than matched small talk (Study 1). Matching on attitude agreement, telling pairs to expect mutual liking, or making closeness an explicit goal had no significant effect (Studies 2 and 3, adequate power). The authors doubted the procedure creates loyalty, dependence or commitment, which take longer. - [Aron et al. 1997 PDF](https://stafforini.com/docs/Aron%20et%20al%20-%20The%20experimental%20generation%20of%20interpersonal%20closeness.pdf)
- Levin, Arluke and Irvine (Society & Animals, 2017; N = 240 students): an adult human beating victim drew less empathy than a one-year-old child, a puppy or a six-year-old dog; child vs puppy did not differ significantly. Authors' explanation: dependency and vulnerability, "age seems to trump species". - [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/472644); [repository record](https://www.wellbeingintlstudiesrepository.org/socatani/14)

**Caretaking, effort and investment (IKEA effect, Ben Franklin effect)**
- Norton, Mochon and Ariely (2012), "The IKEA effect: when labor leads to love": people valued things they built themselves (IKEA boxes, origami, Lego) more; origami builders were willing to pay about five times what non-builders would; secondary summaries report 63% more for a self-assembled storage box. The effect disappeared when people failed to complete the item or destroyed it. - [Harvard DASH paper](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/12136084/norton%20mochon%20ariely%20third%20round[1].pdf); [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IKEA_effect)
- Jecker and Landy (1969): people who did a favour for the experimenter liked him more (the "Ben Franklin effect", explained via dissonance). The study is old, small and not replicated at scale. - [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Franklin_effect); [ReachLink note on replication](https://www.reachlink.com/advice/attraction/benjamin-franklin-effect-and-affection/)

**Humour**
- Kurtz and Algoe (2015, Personal Relationships), 71 couples: couples who laughed together more reported more closeness and support, saw the partner as more similar, and were more satisfied, controlling for solo laughter. Correlational. - [Greater Good summary](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_laughter_brings_us_together); [DOI record 10.1111/pere.12095](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/pere.12095)

**Rituals**
- Norton and Gino (2014, JEP: General), three experiments: people who recalled or performed rituals after a loss (loved one, partner, lottery money) reported less grief, mediated by regained feelings of control; it worked for non-believers too. In the lottery study only performing the ritual helped; reading about it did not. - [PubMed 23398180](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23398180/); [Harvard DASH PDF](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/1/10683152/1/norton%20gino_rituals-and-grief.pdf); [Mannheim summary (German)](https://www.uni-mannheim.de/forschung-erleben/artikel/trauerbewaeltigung-handeln-statt-aussitzen)

**Isolation**
- Portal writer Erik Wolpaw drew on interrogation research: "isolation leads subjects to begin to attach to inanimate objects". - [Shacknews, GDC 08](https://www.shacknews.com/article/51474/gdc-08-portal-creators-on)

### Inferences
- The "deaths do not hurt enough" problem is most likely an intensity problem, not a minutes problem (Cohen 2004: r = .58 for bond intensity vs r = .15 for viewing hours). More scenes per person will help less than making each scene a two-way moment.
- Direct address is the cheapest PSI lever on a phone. Chats where Grandma, Dad, the dog's "behaviour" or goose talk to "you" (second person, questions that wait for a tap) mimic the bodily plus verbal address that raised parasocial experience in Hartmann and Goldhoorn.
- Specificity rule from Kogut and Ritov and Darling: every person needs a name or nickname, one concrete object and one quirk the player can recognise in a single line (Grandma's jam jars and "come visit more" already are this; Grandpa needs his equivalent). A generic "Grandpa" is closer to the unidentified victim.
- Escalating disclosure (Aron): each person should, before they die, say one thing they would not say to everyone (a fear, an apology, a small secret). In a 25-minute game this can be a single caption or chat bubble per person, escalating with the player's age.
- Investment and alternatives (Branch; Simpson 1987 as cited in Cohen 2004: distress is lower when alternatives are easy): make each person's role irreplaceable. If nobody else ever gives jam, the jar stops when Grandma dies. Visible accumulation of the player's own investment (jars stacked on a shelf, a count of visits chosen) uses the IKEA logic; the IKEA effect fades if the task is not completed, so do not leave caretaking actions unfinished by design unless the unfinished-ness is the point.
- Humour and shared rituals are cheap closeness builders: one running joke per key relationship (goose especially), repeated at least twice before it is broken.
- After a death, a short player-performed ritual (place the jar, close the gate) would, per Norton and Gino, give a sense of control and closure. That reduces raw grief, so it trades sting for meaning; use it for the deaths that should feel resolved, and withhold it where the story wants the ache to stay open.

### Gaps
- No primary effect sizes were retrieved for Aron 1997 or for Kogut and Ritov's contribution amounts (full texts not parsed).
- The 10 to 20 presentation peak for mere exposure comes from Wikipedia, not verified in Bornstein 1989. A 2017 re-analysis by Montoya et al. was mentioned only by a secondary site.
- No study was found specifically on nicknames or running gags in fiction and attachment; those implications are extrapolated from naming (Darling, Kogut and Ritov) and shared laughter (Kurtz and Algoe).
- The Ben Franklin effect is weakly evidenced; treat it as a hypothesis.

## 2. Game-specific research and designer evidence on companion attachment

### Takeaway
Game research confirms that players form real attachments to NPCs and value the sadness of losing them; designers who succeeded made the companion useful and never a burden, gave the player physical or mechanical acts of care (holding a hand, carrying a cube, feeding a horse), made the companion vulnerable, and tied the loss to a mechanic the player had learned.

### Cited Findings

**Academic studies**
- Bopp, Müller, Aeschbach, Opwis and Mekler (CHI PLAY 2019), qualitative survey of 213 players: seven forms of attachment, from admiration of skill and role-model status to "deep concern for characters' well-being". - [Aalto record](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/6cef35fe-1653-4627-912d-79a8947070fe); [Basel record](https://edoc.unibas.ch/73889)
- The seven forms, as listed by a later DiGRA paper: Cool and Capable; Admired Paragon; Crush; Concern for one's Protégé (worry, responsibility); Sympathetic Alter Ego (empathy based on one's own experiences); Trusted Close Friend; Respected Nemesis. The first three rest on the character's functionality, the next three on emotional qualities. Bopp et al. define attachment as "the sense of liking, connection, and closeness a player feels to any in-game character". - [Mao, DiGRA](https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/2787/2771/2830)
- Bopp, Mekler and Opwis (CHI 2016), 121 players' accounts of emotionally moving game moments: most players enjoyed and appreciated sadness and other negative emotions; triggers included in-game loss, character attachment, (lack of) agency and personal memories, often with self-reflection. - [Aalto research portal](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/negative-emotion-positive-experience-emotionally-moving-moments-i/)
- Coulson, Barnett, Ferguson and Gould (2012, Psychology of Popular Media Culture), 74 players rating ten NPCs they had interacted with extensively (Dragon Age: Origins per a citing paper): players form "real and authentic emotional attachments" to virtual characters, driven by player personality and motivation plus the NPC's attractiveness, friendliness or hostility, and practical usefulness. - [Middlesex repository](https://repository.mdx.ac.uk/item/83q53)
- Eum et al. (CHI 2021), interviews with six Spiritfarer players: their grief process in and around the game resembled Stroebe and Schut's Dual Process Model of bereavement. - [KAIST record](https://koasas.kaist.ac.kr/handle/10203/312348?mode=full)
- Gibbs (DiGRA): after voice actor Lance Reddick died in 2023, hundreds of Destiny players gathered around his NPC, Commander Zavala, kneeling, saluting and using candle-like effects. - [DiGRA extended abstract](https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/2910/2894/2953)

**Designers and postmortems**
- Portal, Weighted Companion Cube (GDC 2008): playtesters would not carry a cube through a long puzzle. The level was changed from one where the box was easy to destroy (and simply left behind) to one where it could not be destroyed and featured in more events; Wolpaw's GLaDOS lines presented it as the player's only friend. Kim Swift: after that, "no one ever forgot the box". - [Game Developer, Best of GDC: Portal](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/best-of-gdc-the-secrets-of-i-portal-i-s-huge-success); [Shacknews](https://www.shacknews.com/article/51474/gdc-08-portal-creators-on)
- The Last of Us, Ellie (Max Dyckhoff, GDC 2014): the game was "centered on the player's relationship with her", so systems had to match the narrative; if Ellie could jump out of cover and expose the player, "we'd broken the relationship between Ellie and the player". - [GDC Vault session page](https://gdcvault.com/play/1020364/Ellie-Buddy-AI-in-The); [Game Developer video post](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/video-ellie-s-buddy-ai-in-i-the-last-of-us-i-)
- Red Dead Redemption 2, the horse: Phil Hooker (director of technology): "To cement the strength of the bond we had to make it vulnerable so you respect and cared for it as you play, so it feels personal and real"; dead horses stay dead. Aaron Garbut (art director): the team iterated on horses for years and wanted it to upset you when it is hurt; a dying horse gives a limited window to get medicine. Rockstar's GDC 2021 talk (Tobias Kleanthous) framed the horse as "a trusted friend with whom a deep connection is forged". - [Wccftech on permadeath](https://wccftech.com/red-dead-redemption-2-horse-permadeath/); [Wccftech on Garbut](https://wccftech.com/rockstar-iterated-rdr2-horses-for-years/)
- Florence (Ken Wong, GDC 2019): "For a level to work, it needs to have an interesting interaction that evokes a feeling or tells a story", and the feeling "has to come from the interactions, not from the images"; a key revision was realising "we hadn't shown why these characters are good for each other", so scenes showing the bond were added; "small interactions could have a lot of emotional impact"; quiet everyday moments (breakfast, the couch, placing objects on a shared shelf); each lead got a voice (cello for Krish, piano for Florence); "there's intentionally not much animation in the game"; more than half the tested levels did not work. - [Game Developer, Designing Florence](https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/designing-i-florence-i-to-convey-the-ineffable-feeling-of-being-in-love); [GDC Vault, Letting Go: A Florence Postmortem](https://gdcvault.com/play/1025694/Letting-Go-A-Florence)
- Ico (Fumito Ueda): the player holds a button to keep holding Yorda's hand; the mechanic came from wanting characters who touch instead of fight; combat, extra characters and areas were cut ("design by subtraction") to isolate the bond; the two leads do not share a language. Ueda says the emotion arose rather than being aimed at. - [Game Developer interview](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/-i-ico-i-creator-ueda-on-his-design-ethos-and-development-style); [Design by Subtraction transcript](https://amara.org/subtitles/RzpUvbByVapH/en/2/download/Ico%20and%20Design%20by%20Subtraction%20%20Game%20Designer%20Philosophy.en.txt)
- Brothers: A Tale of Two Sons: each brother is mapped to one stick and one trigger. After the older brother dies, the younger one, afraid of water, can only swim when the player uses the dead brother's stick together with his own (blog analysis by Ian McCamant, not a developer quote). Josef Fares said the near-silence was deliberate so players read body language. - [Game Developer blog, All the feels in one input](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/brothers-all-the-feels-in-one-input); [Game Informer, Afterwords](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2013/09/27/afterwords-brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons)
- Spiritfarer: Nicolas Guérin wanted a game where "everything centered around care". Reviewers note that by the time you bid a spirit goodbye you have learned their likes and dislikes, their past, hopes and regrets, and that you have cared for each one on your boat. - [PC Gamer review](https://pcgamer.com/spiritfarer-review); [GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/spiritfarer-offers-a-rewarding-heartfelt-experience-about-moving-on-and-learning-to-let-go)
- What Remains of Edith Finch (Ian Dallas, GDC 2018): 13 sub-stories, each with its own art style and mechanic, built from more than 13 prototypes; each death becomes a reward and the engine of progression. - [Game Developer, 13 prototypes](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-weaving-13-prototypes-into-1-game-for-i-what-remains-of-edith-finch-i-); [Game Developer, repeated deaths as reward](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/making-repeated-deaths-a-reward-in-i-what-remains-of-edith-finch-i-)
- BioShock Infinite, Elizabeth (Shawn Robertson, GDC 2014): every department shaped her; the team worried players would tire of her repeated lines. - [Game Developer video post](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/video-how-irrational-gave-life-to-i-bioshock-infinite-i-s-elizabeth); [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1020545/Creating-BioShock-Infinite-s)

### Inferences
- The Portal fix is the closest analogue to this game's problem: testers did not care until the object could not be lost carelessly, kept appearing in events and was framed by another voice as "your only friend". For each family member, other characters can name the bond out loud (Mom: "Grandpa only laughs like that with you").
- Ellie's rule ("never break the relationship") translates to: a character should never be annoying, preachy or a chore in the scenes before their death. Usefulness and warmth (Coulson: friendliness, usefulness) beat tragedy-signalling.
- The Brothers move is directly portable: give one recurring tap or gesture to each person (tap to hold Grandpa's hand on the walk, tap to throw the dog's ball, tap to pass Grandma the jar), use it several times, then after the death let the same gesture appear once more and do nothing, or do something only as memory.
- The Bopp forms suggest variety: the dog and the player's own kid are "Concern for one's Protégé"; Grandpa and Dad can be "Admired Paragon"; the best friend and goose "Trusted Close Friend"; the partner "Crush" turning to friend. Losses of protégés and of paragons hurt differently, which is a reason not to write every death the same way (Edith Finch: one distinct form per death).
- Florence's warning applies: if the game has not shown why a person is good for the player, the death has nothing to remove. One scene per person whose only job is "why they are good for you".

### Gaps
- The Bopp 2019 full text could not be retrieved, so frequencies per form and the most-mentioned characters are missing.
- I found no published GDC talk by Spiritfarer's designers on the goodbyes; the Spiritfarer evidence above is reviews plus one six-person study.
- I could not verify the commonly repeated claim that Elizabeth throwing ammo and coins was what made players like her.
- No player-reaction data on RDR2 horse deaths were found, only design intent.
- To the Moon: only weak sources (fan essays) were found on Kan Gao's detail choices; nothing citable beyond a PC Gamer essay noting the origami rabbits that recur through Johnny's memories ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/a-journey-to-the-moon-and-back)).

## 3. How much exposure before a loss lands; wants, habits, and the player's agency in caring

### Takeaway
There is no study giving a minimum number of interactions before an NPC loss lands. The best available evidence says bonds form largely at first exposure and then grow slowly, that bond intensity and closeness (not time) predict grief, and that investment and caring actions raise commitment. For a 25-minute game, that argues for a few high-intensity, two-way, repeated moments per person rather than more minutes.

### Cited Findings
- A longitudinal thesis study (University of New Brunswick, 80 analysed participants watching a season at home over four weeks plus two post-viewing weeks): parasocial relationships were "moderately strong after initial exposure to the character", with only "small increases in subsequent viewing weeks"; breakup distress after the show ended was modest and declined slowly. Stronger relationships and distress came with higher perceived similarity, attraction, PSI, identification, wishful identification and transportation. - [UNB Scholar, "The developmental course of a parasocial relationship"](https://unbscholar.lib.unb.ca/items/27f818b0-0f5a-4902-bf8a-98a5aaba416b/full)
- Cohen (2004), N = 381: PSI intensity predicted anticipated breakup distress (r = .58), while hours of viewing barely did (r = .15); anxious-ambivalent viewers anticipated the most distress. Cohen also cites Simpson (1987): length and closeness of a relationship predict the intensity and duration of distress after break-up, and distress is lower when alternatives are easy to find. - [Cohen 2004 PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/culture/2004-cohen.pdf)
- Cohen (2003): teens expect to be more upset than adults by the loss of a favourite character; women report stronger PSRs but not stronger breakup reactions. - [Cohen 2003, Mass Communication & Society](https://www.csus.edu/faculty/m/fred.molitor/docs/parasocial-breakups.pdf)
- A survey of 1,289 fans of the soap Neighbours (76% female, mean age 45) found those who grieved most had formed strong bonds and engaged emotionally with favourite characters (reported by NBC; the original authors are not named in the article). - [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/health/health-news/feel-grief-favorite-tv-show-ends-re-not-alone-rcna156456)
- An experiment on binge-watching reported that watching in one sitting increased PSR strength and narrative transportation compared with spaced viewing (Social Sciences 8(1):19; full text was blocked, so numbers are not verified). - [MDPI Social Sciences](https://mdpi.com/2076-0760/8/1/19)
- Real grief: Eckerd, Barnett and Jett-Dias (2016, Death Studies; 211 pet-loss vs 146 human-loss students): human loss was rated slightly higher with small effect sizes, and closeness to the deceased was by far the strongest predictor of grief in both groups; most other predictors lost significance once closeness was included. - [Death Studies record](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c460t-ca8190e850401a790e69db87132f2c9cade08815d94dc7be77573052a6c01fb93/Details)
- Aron et al. (1997): 45 minutes of escalating disclosure creates closeness between strangers, but not loyalty, dependence or commitment. - [Aron et al. PDF](https://stafforini.com/docs/Aron%20et%20al%20-%20The%20experimental%20generation%20of%20interpersonal%20closeness.pdf)
- Agency in caring: the investment model (satisfaction plus investment predicts commitment to characters) - [Branch et al. 2013](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/psychpubs/63); the IKEA effect (own labour raises value, but not if unfinished or destroyed) - [Norton, Mochon and Ariely](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/12136084/norton%20mochon%20ariely%20third%20round[1].pdf); Ico's held button and Portal's carried cube (above); Spiritfarer's caring for each spirit before the goodbye (above); Bopp et al. 2016 list (lack of) agency among moving triggers - [CHI 2016](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/negative-emotion-positive-experience-emotionally-moving-moments-i/).
- Wants and habits: Cohen's identification includes taking on the character's goals (secondary description) - [APA](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/ppm/getting-involved); Spiritfarer reviewers single out learning each spirit's likes, dislikes, hopes and regrets before they leave - [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/spiritfarer-review); Darling's "Frank" had habits (likes to play, sometimes escapes, never gets far) and that alone raised hesitation - [Yes! Magazine](https://www.yesmagazine.org/health-happiness/2015/07/15/should-we-be-kind-to-robots-kate-darling).

### Inferences
- Rough budget per person who dies (inference from the above, not a measured threshold): one first-meeting moment that is specific and direct; a habit or ritual shown at least three times (enough to be predicted, well under the 10 to 20 where mere exposure saturates); one want the player learns (Grandpa wants to fix the old radio, Dad wants to see you play once, Mom wants the text); one disclosure; one or two acts the player does for or with them; then a last, slightly broken repetition of the ritual just before the death. That is 6 to 8 beats, which can be 60 to 120 seconds of play each for the central figures.
- Because closeness predicts grief more than time, the spacing of deaths matters less than whether the last scene before each death is close. Grandpa at 12 can hurt if the age 7 to 12 stretch has his habit, his want and one shared act; it will not hurt if he only appears in captions.
- Teens expect stronger reactions to character loss (Cohen 2003), and the player is a teen when the dog dies at 16. Framing that death through the teen's own reaction, not the adult narrator's summary, would match how teens are reported to experience such loss.
- Let the want stay unfinished at death (the radio still in pieces). An unfinished goal the player has adopted (identification) is a stronger loss than a completed life; a later choice to finish it for them is a legitimate small ritual.
- Binge evidence suggests one continuous sitting increases bond strength, which supports keeping the game short and unbroken rather than chaptered with exits.
- Choices should cost something tied to a person (visit Grandma vs go out with friends) so that the investment is the player's. The current "come visit more" line becomes far heavier if the player has, through their own taps, visited less.

### Gaps
- No experiment was found that varies the number of interactions with an NPC before its death and measures grief; this is the central open question and should be tested in playtests (for example, A/B: Grandpa with two scenes vs four, same death).
- The binge-watching study's sample and effect sizes were not retrieved (403 on fetch).
- The UNB thesis gives no effect sizes and does not name the show in the abstract.

## 4. Pets: the human-pet bond, pet death in fiction and games, and why a dog's routine works

### Takeaway
Pets borrow the parental caregiving system (neoteny, dependence, unconditional regard), and people often feel as much or more empathy for a dog as for an adult human. Pet grief is close to human grief and is driven by closeness. A daily greeting ritual is effective because it is the most visible form of the dog's unconditional attachment, it intensifies with absence, and its absence after death is itself the loss.

### Cited Findings
- Archer (1997, Evolution and Human Behavior 18(4)): pets exploit caregiving responses evolved mainly for parent-child bonds; neotenous features give the initial attraction, attributing mental states gives continuing satisfaction, and pets offer a kind of unconditional relationship usually absent with other humans. - [Scilit record](https://www.scilit.net/publications/c3fa3e57d0e0259ef4e21e130ae3c698); [PDF](https://accurateclinic.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Why-do-people-love-their-pets-1.pdf)
- Levin, Arluke and Irvine (N = 240): empathy for a beaten puppy or adult dog exceeded empathy for an adult human and equalled that for a one-year-old child. - [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/472644)
- Borgi et al. (2014, Frontiers in Psychology): manipulating baby-schema features in human, dog and cat faces changed cuteness ratings and gaze in 3 to 6 year olds; the response is early and not limited to human faces. Press coverage reported that children rated puppies and kittens as cuter than other babies. - [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00411/pdf); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/07/140721100119.htm)
- Eckerd et al. (2016): pet-loss grief was only slightly lower than human-loss grief (small effects); closeness was the dominant predictor. - [Death Studies record](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c460t-ca8190e850401a790e69db87132f2c9cade08815d94dc7be77573052a6c01fb93/Details)
- Rehn and Keeling (2011, Applied Animal Behaviour Science 129; 12 dogs filmed at home): dogs were mostly inactive while alone regardless of duration, but after 2 or 4 hours (vs 30 minutes) greeted the owner with more tail wagging, more contact-seeking, higher heart rate and more arousal behaviours; 2 h and 4 h did not differ much. - [Rehn and Keeling PDF](https://slunik.slu.se/kursfiler/DO0031/40007.1213/The_effect_of_time_left_alone_at_home_on_dog_welfare.pdf)
- Nagasawa et al. (2015, Science): dog-to-owner gazing raised owners' oxytocin, and longer mutual gaze went with larger rises; the species-specific "loop" claim has been criticised as confounded. - [Commentary, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4663257); [critique, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4826871)
- Virtual pets: in an SFU study, 51 Grade 4 and 5 children cared for a Nintendogs dog for three weeks; most said they formed an emotional bond, empathy scores rose, and stronger bonds went with higher empathy (quasi-experimental). Chesney and Lawson (80 adult Nintendog users responding): a Nintendog gives companionship, but significantly less than a real dog or cat; their paper contrasts Nintendogs, which cannot die, with Tamagotchi, whose deaths many users mourned. - [SFU project page](https://sfu.ca/education/research/research-projects/computer-simulated-animals-empathy.html); [Chesney and Lawson, Interaction Studies](https://benjamins.com/catalog/is.8.2.09che)
- RDR2 used vulnerability plus permadeath to make the horse bond feel "personal and real". - [Wccftech](https://wccftech.com/red-dead-redemption-2-horse-permadeath/)

### Inferences
- The gate ritual already does the right thing; it needs to be seen enough times, and to vary with the player's life, for its absence to register. Rehn and Keeling suggest the greeting grows with the length of absence: show a bigger greeting after a long day (first day of school, a school trip), so the dog visibly "counts" the player's absences.
- Age the dog visibly through the ritual: the puppy runs to the gate, the adult dog waits at it, the old dog is slow to stand, the last time it waits lying down. The death then lands on a ritual the player has watched degrade, which is the slow "dependency and vulnerability" cue from Levin and Archer.
- The day after the death, the player walks home from school and the gate is empty. No caption. This absence-of-routine beat is likely more effective than showing the death itself (inference, consistent with closeness and ritual findings, not directly tested).
- Puppy phase: because faces are absent, baby-schema cues must come from body proportions, size, clumsy motion and sound (see section 5 gap). Keep the puppy short and round, with bouncy, slightly uncoordinated movement.
- Give the player one recurring act of care for the dog (fill the bowl, throw the stick, open the gate), which after the death can be performed once into empty space.
- Teens and pets: the dog dies at 16, when the player character is old enough to feel responsible. One prior moment where the player could have done something (a walk skipped for friends) uses the "Concern for one's Protégé" form without making the death the player's fault.

### Gaps
- No primary study was found on audience reactions to pet death in films or novels specifically (beyond the Levin news-story experiment); claims that "the dog dying hurts more than humans" in fiction are mostly anecdotal.
- No research was found on dogs waiting at a gate on a schedule (anticipation of a daily return); the Rehn and Keeling greeting data are the closest.
- Nagasawa's sample sizes were not retrieved, and the finding is contested.

## 5. Faceless or minimal characters: can attachment form without faces?

### Takeaway
Yes. People spontaneously read intention, personality and emotion from moving shapes and from bodies with faces covered, and several celebrated games removed faces, names or language on purpose. What minimal characters need instead is clear motion, consistent identity cues, a name or story, and room for the player to project; the main loss is the facial baby schema for cuteness and fine-grained sadness.

### Cited Findings
- Heider and Simmel (1944): a short film of two triangles and a circle moving around a box; most viewers described it as a social story with intentions and personalities (114 Smith College students in three groups per secondary sources). A 2021 study reproduced the effect 75 years later with new coloured videos; participants spontaneously gave the shapes social meaning. - [Original paper PDF](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/research/papers/others/1944/heider1944a.pdf); [2021 replication, PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/33678806/)
- Atkinson, Dittrich, Gemmell and Young (2004, Perception 33): ten actors with covered faces portrayed anger, disgust, fear, happiness and sadness; emotions were readily recognised from body movement, even in point-light displays with almost no form information; exaggerated movement improved recognition, except for sadness. - [Perception, DOI 10.1068/p5096](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/p5096)
- Journey (Jenova Chen, GDC 2013): players are nameless, in identical robes, genderless and ageless, and can only communicate with a single chime; the goal was to "induce a new emotion between two strangers online", and the communication limit existed to create emotional connection. - [Game Developer, Designing a new emotional experience in Journey](https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/video-designing-a-new-emotional-experience-in-i-journey-i-); [Destructoid](https://vip-develop.destructoid.com/journey-chen-wanted-emotional-connections-from-mmos)
- Thomas Was Alone (Mike Bithell): coloured rectangles, each given a personality built on its unique ability, plus narration; players invented their own stories and cared about the characters more than about the gameplay, which Bithell had not expected. - [PocketGamer.biz, the making of Thomas Was Alone](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/feature/50410/a-tale-of-friendship-the-making-of-thomas-was-alone/)
- Florence: "there's intentionally not much animation", leaving room for the player, who "has to give them meaning in their own mind"; each lead has an instrument as a voice and speech-bubble shapes stand in for dialogue. - [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/designing-i-florence-i-to-convey-the-ineffable-feeling-of-being-in-love)
- Brothers and Ico: near-silent or non-shared-language characters, with body language and touch carrying the relationship. - [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2013/09/27/afterwords-brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons); [Game Developer, Ueda](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/-i-ico-i-creator-ueda-on-his-design-ethos-and-development-style)
- Portal's Companion Cube (a box with hearts) and Darling's HEXBUGs (insect-like robots) show attachment to objects with no face at all once they have a role, a name or a story. - [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/best-of-gdc-the-secrets-of-i-portal-i-s-huge-success); [MIT record](https://libmast.utm.my/Record/mit-1721.1%2F109059)
- Infantile facial features drive cuteness ratings and gaze in children across humans, dogs and cats (so a faceless puppy loses this channel). - [Borgi et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00411/pdf)

### Inferences
- The faceless, one-colour-for-life design is well supported, provided motion does the work a face would do. Each person needs a signature movement (Grandpa's slow lean, Mom's quick turn, the dog's tail-wag bounce) kept consistent across ages, the way Thomas Was Alone tied personality to each shape's ability.
- Sadness is the hardest emotion to read from body motion and is not helped by exaggeration (Atkinson et al.). The sad beats should therefore be carried by context, stillness, absence and text (the chat that is not answered, the empty gate) rather than by animated grief.
- Colour as identity is the game's equivalent of Florence's instruments: when a person dies, their colour stopping (the life line ending, the colour disappearing from later scenes, then returning once in an object they left, such as a jam jar's lid) is a clear, faceless way to show loss.
- Low detail invites projection (Florence, Journey, Thomas Was Alone); specificity should go into words, objects and habits, not into rendering.
- For the puppy, replace facial cuteness with body-level cues: smaller and rounder shape, larger head relative to body, clumsy bouncy motion, a high short sound. This is inference; the body-proportion part of Lorenz's baby schema was not verified in a fetched source here.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly compares attachment to faceless vs faced versions of the same game character; the evidence is perception research plus designer accounts.
- Exact Heider and Simmel tallies were not verified from the original paper; secondary sources disagree on group numbers.
- No source was found on whether a consistent single colour per character aids recognition or attachment over a life span; this is a design hypothesis to playtest.
