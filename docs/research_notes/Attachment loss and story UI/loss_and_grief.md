# How a character's death or loss lands in short narrative games and fiction

Scope note: this adds to `docs/reports/What makes a story hit.md`, which already covers transportation, nostalgia, Gračanin's powerlessness review, the 19-nation kama muta study, Ersner-Hershfield's "last time" poignancy, peak-end, affect labelling, spoilers, Fendt's agency result, the enactment effect, the content-warning meta-analysis and Florence, Before Your Eyes and Spiritfarer's hug. Those are cited again below only where a new finding builds on them. Where a source was read only as an abstract, press release or search summary, it says so.

## 1. The psychology of being moved by loss and of enjoying sad media

### Takeaway
People do not enjoy the sadness of a sad story; they enjoy being moved, and sadness is the fuel. Being moved needs a bond made vivid and an act of closeness or care at the moment of loss, so a death "hurts more" when the story shows the love at the point of loss, not when it shows more of the dying.

### Cited Findings
- In a cinema study with 76 viewers and 38 clips of variations on one sad scenario, sadness and enjoyment were strongly correlated, but the correlation was almost fully mediated by feeling moved; the authors call sadness a contributor to and intensifier of being moved (Hanich et al. 2014, *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts*; abstract and citing papers only) ([openHSU](https://openhsu.ub.hsu-hh.de/handle/10.24405/10791)).
- The same mediation held for sad music: the pleasure of sad music ran through being moved (Vuoskoski and Eerola 2017). That paper also summarises Menninghaus et al. 2015: being moved is usually triggered by life events such as birth, death and marriage, and its two main ingredients are sadness and joy ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00439/full)).
- Positive tears, not negative tears, went with being moved, and appraisals of closeness and moral acts explained being moved better than they explained sadness, anxiety or happiness ([Seibt et al. 2017, abstract](https://biomed.reviewer.ly/app/publication/051d490e-e7e9-4d7d-9941-6c703e2c9aa6)).
- Physiologically, kama muta is not sadness: in 144 Portuguese and Norwegian viewers comparing kama muta, sadness and awe videos, strong kama muta went with more phasic skin conductance, skin temperature, goosebumps and smiling-muscle activity, and with lower heart rate and breathing (Zickfeld et al. 2020, *Psychophysiology*, abstract) ([Zendy](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/psyp.13662)).
- Fiske's kama muta theory treats memorials as triggers because they link the living with the dead, and states that keepsakes, heirlooms, mementos and photographs often evoke nostalgic kama muta (Fiske, book chapter "Disasters, memorials, mementos"; chapter page and search summary only) ([Taylor and Francis](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/mono/10.4324/9780367220952-10/disasters-memorials-mementos-alan-page-fiske)).
- Telling viewers to regulate cuts being moved: instructions to suppress reduced both felt being moved and tears compared with no instructions, and instructions to reappraise reduced them more (Portuguese and Norwegian samples, three videos; repository record) ([ISCTE repository](https://repositorio.iscte-iul.pt/handle/10071/11974?locale=en)).
- Tears and goosebumps arriving together marked a "maximal climax within peak moments", and intensity is graded even within peak moments. Prosocial behaviour played "a crucial role" in eliciting both. Compared with equal-length control clips from the same films, the tear clips were built to foreground social interaction and emotional expression (Wassiliwizky et al. 2017, viewers' self-chosen film scenes; abstract only, sample size not seen) ([Frontiers](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00041/text)).
- The paradox of tragedy, current theory: the Distancing-Embracing model says art first gives distance (the frame "this is art", and control over how long one stays with it) and then lets people embrace negative feeling through three things: mixing positive and negative emotion in the composition, the aesthetic qualities of the medium, and meaning-making. Mixed emotions help fold negative feeling into an overall pleasurable experience (Menninghaus, Wagner, Hanich, Wassiliwizky, Jacobsen and Koelsch 2017, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*) ([Cambridge PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/FAAF19734BAB559CD439BB46F8BDC571/S0140525X17000309a.pdf/div-class-title-the-distancing-embracing-model-of-the-enjoyment-of-negative-emotions-in-art-reception-div.pdf)). Zickfeld and colleagues' commentary called parts of it too loosely specified to test (same source page, search summary).
- Sadness from a tragedy turns people toward their own close relationships. 361 students watched an abridged *Atonement*; the more sadness they felt, the more they wrote about real people they were close to, and that writing raised life happiness. Viewers whose thoughts turned to themselves rather than their relationships gained no happiness, which ruled out "at least my life is better" comparison (Knobloch-Westerwick et al. 2013, *Communication Research*; press release) ([Ohio State News](https://news.osu.edu/smiling-through-the-tears-study-shows-how-tearjerkers-make-people-happier---ohio-state-research-and-innovation-communications/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/03/120326132533.htm)).
- An emotionally arousing drama (*Stuart: A Life Backwards*) raised pain threshold, a proxy for endorphin release, and raised felt bonding with the group watching; film viewers held a wall-sit 13.1% longer while documentary viewers held 4.6% less, and the strongest emotional responders gained most in both. Viewers who did not respond emotionally showed neither effect (Dunbar et al. 2016, *Royal Society Open Science*; university release and secondary summaries) ([University of Oxford](https://ox.ac.uk/node/511820); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/fd99f0f4843848cabd8abf22537424bb)).
- Laughing in grief is not a failure of grief: in bereaved adults interviewed months after a spouse's death (39 people per one summary), genuine Duchenne laughter while talking about the loss went with less anger, more enjoyment, better social relations and warmer responses from strangers, and the authors read laughter as a way of getting distance from distress (Keltner and Bonanno 1997, *JPSP*) ([Greater Good PDF](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/dacherkeltner/docs/keltner.laughter.jpsp.1997.pdf); [UC Berkeley release](https://newsarchive.berkeley.edu/news/media/releases/97legacy/death.html)).
- Knowing an ending is near adds sadness without removing happiness (the "last visit" and graduation studies), already summarised in the earlier report ([PMC, Ersner-Hershfield et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2807633)). Memory of an episode follows its peak and end, length barely counting ([Fredrickson and Kahneman 1993](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/fredr-kahneman-jpsp1993.pdf)).
- In a recall study with 512 players, games remembered as meaningful scored higher on appreciation than games remembered as fun, story was the feature most tied to meaningfulness, and insight and relatedness carried the effect (Oliver and Bowman 2015, *Psychology of Popular Media Culture*; university release). A later critique notes appreciation was a single item and eudaimonia was not measured directly (search summary) ([Penn State News](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/video-games-can-power-merely-fun-meaningful-experiences)).

### Inferences
- The author's diagnosis "deaths should hurt more" is better restated as "deaths should move more". The evidence says sadness only becomes the good kind of hurt when it is attached to closeness or a caring act, so the design lever is what sits next to the death (an act of love, a keepsake, a ritual), not how hard the death itself is shown.
- Fiske's keepsake finding fits the draft's jar of jam and Mom's signed text exactly; the deaths that lack an object (Grandpa) or whose object is only shown once will feel thinner. Each loss can carry one thing left behind: the gate (dog), the jar (Grandma), the chat (Dad), the jacket and the signed weather text (Mom).
- The regulation result suggests that narrator lines which reframe a death ("she had a good life", "it was time") work like reappraisal and may lower being moved. Keep the narrator silent or factual at the moment of loss. This applies a viewer-regulation study to narration, so it is inference.
- Knobloch-Westerwick supports the earlier report's "no call to action": sadness already turns people toward their own close relationships, and it worked when thoughts went to other people, not to the self. An ending that leaves room (a blank beat after "home" is sent) lets that turn happen on its own.
- Wassiliwizky's graded climax implies five deaths should not all aim at the top of the scale. A ladder (Grandpa low, dog and Grandma middle, Dad and Mom high, the final "home" highest) keeps the goosebump-and-tears peak for the end, which is what peak-end memory will keep.
- Distancing-Embracing gives a direct rule for a phone game: the player should control how long they stay in a loss (tap to move on, never a forced timer), and the scene should mix a positive element into the negative one.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests anticipatory grief in fiction directly (knowing a character will die versus not). The nearest evidence is Ersner-Hershfield's "last time" work and the mixed spoiler studies in the earlier report.
- No study found applying the peak-end rule to individual sad scenes inside a longer work; the rule is established for whole episodes and clips.
- Wassiliwizky's sample size and the shot-level film measures (shot length, close-ups, music) were not visible in the abstract.
- No study found directly testing whether a character who holds back tears moves an audience more. The closest is that sadness shown with "passionate restraint" was judged more competent and more authentic than open or suppressed displays, which is about judging the character, not feeling moved ([Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/sadness-is-believed-to-signal-competence-when-displayed-with-pass/)).

## 2. Craft: setup and payoff, the unknowing last time, absence, ritual, humour, understatement

### Takeaway
Named practitioners converge on three moves: the coldest possible telling of the death itself, the setup paid off in an object or routine afterwards, and cutting until less carries more. The cheap version is the one that tells the audience what to feel or forces a gesture of mourning.

### Cited Findings
- Chekhov to Lydia Avilova, 19 March 1892: when depicting sad or unlucky people and wanting to touch the reader's heart, be colder, because restraint gives their grief a background against which it stands out; he criticised a story where the characters weep and the narrator sighs (Constance Garnett translation, reproduced) ([ArtsJournal](https://www.artsjournal.com/aboutlastnight/2018/04/almanac-chekhov-on-writers-of-fiction-and-their-characters.html)).
- Pixar's *Up* "Married Life" sequence (a whole marriage to Ellie's death): head of story Ronnie Del Carmen proposed making it silent; Pete Docter says "the less we had the more emotional it felt"; the final cut is only music and pictures; "every shot is a setup for elements we use later in the film"; the animators had "30 to 40 minutes of material that we slowly whittled down" ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/movies/2022/6/14/23166006/up-first-10-minutes-making-of); [SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/924676/30-minutes-had-to-be-cut-from-the-opening-sequence-of-up)). Docter asked Giacchino for a theme that plays like a grandmother's music box, and the camera stays still until the wedding, then moves in gentle lateral tracks (same sources, quoted via search summary).
- What Remains of Edith Finch: each dead Finch's room is sealed as a memorial and the house grows a floor per generation (GameSpot via Giant Bomb's wiki) ([Giant Bomb](https://www.giantbomb.com/games/3030-48329/)). Ian Dallas: the family tree screen after each story was there to give the player a moment to pause and give Edith "room to breathe" ([God is a Geek](https://www.godisageek.com/2017/04/interview-with-ian-dallas-creative-director-on-what-remains-of-edith-finch/)).
- The Spiritfarer loop asks the player to give up the very characters they have cared for, and each departing spirit leaves a Spirit Flower behind (critical analysis, not a developer statement) ([Fandom](https://www.fandom.com/articles/spiritfarer-and-the-gamification-of-grief)).
- The cheap version, documented: Call of Duty: Advanced Warfare's funeral prompt "Press F to pay respects" was mocked at release; Kill Screen's critique is that it uses the same flat command form as "Press X to reload" for grief ([Kill Screen](https://www.killscreen.com/call-duty-doesnt-understand-grief-then-who-does/)). A Game Developer blog contrasts it with Batman: Arkham City, where the player may walk past the alley of the parents' death, while Advanced Warfare cannot be continued without the press ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/all-due-respect-press-f-for-farce)). The screenwriter said he did not know the prompt was in the game until a journalist asked him (secondary) ([Wikiwand](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Press_F_to_pay_respects)).
- Humour next to grief is supported in life (Keltner and Bonanno above) and by the Distancing-Embracing claim that mixed positive and negative emotion makes negative feeling embraceable ([Cambridge PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/FAAF19734BAB559CD439BB46F8BDC571/S0140525X17000309a.pdf/div-class-title-the-distancing-embracing-model-of-the-enjoyment-of-negative-emotions-in-art-reception-div.pdf)).
- Rituals and continued contact with the dead are now read as healthy: the continuing bonds model (Klass, Silverman and Nickman 1996) treats grief as building a new relationship with the dead rather than cutting ties, and later research largely treats continuing bonds as healthy (as cited in a 2022 Springer paper) ([Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12124-022-09679-3)). Mourners use the dead person's social profile to keep the bond and address them directly ([Getty et al., CHI 2011, "I said your name in an empty room"](https://sml.stanford.edu/publications/2011/i-said-your-name-empty-room-grieving-and-continuing-bonds-facebook)), and a 2025 scoping review lists instant messaging as one of the tools people use to communicate with the dead ([Utrecht scoping review PDF](https://ddd17.sites.uu.nl/wp-content/uploads/sites/1084/2025/09/Digital-DeathTEch-Communication-Scoping-Review.pdf)).

### Inferences
- Answer to "too fast and too abrupt": the evidence does not say a death must be slow. Nomura (section 3) argues for sudden deaths and Chekhov for cold ones. What the draft lacks is the before and the after. A working three-beat shape per loss, all of it inference:
  1. Before: an ordinary or funny beat with the person, ideally one the player has seen repeated (the routine that will break). The repetition is the setup; Docter's "every shot is a setup" is the rule.
  2. The moment: short, cold, factual, no feeling named. One line or one image. The draft's "Grandpa is the first one." and "We let him go this morning" are already this.
  3. After: the routine with the hole in it, plus the thing left behind, plus a pause the player ends. This is where the extra weight should go, not into the moment.
- The unknowing last time: Mom's "Rain tomorrow. Take a jacket. Mom" is already the right kind of last line (mundane, signed, cold). It gains force if the jacket comes back as an object afterwards (a rainy scene, the jacket on), which converts the text from a caption into a keepsake.
- The delayed realisation: Mom's "Call me when you can. It's Grandma." works because the player infers the death before anyone says it. Keep it as the only words; adding "Grandma died" after it would explain the inference away.
- Ritual: the draft's strongest ritual is the "home" and "ok." exchange. Continuing-bonds research makes the ending's message to Mom's number a recognised real behaviour, not a contrivance, so it can be played straight.
- Humour: a small funny beat can sit inside the aftermath as well as before it (a capital-letters habit, a family joke repeated at the wrong time). The bereavement laughter data say this reads as real grief, not as relief from it.
- Avoid: a prompt that orders the player to mourn ("tap to say goodbye") with no option to pass; narration that names the feeling; music or visuals that announce the death before the words (covered in the earlier report); stacking several deaths so close that each dilutes the next.

### Gaps
- No primary source found for the common line "sentimentality is unearned emotion"; it was not used.
- The final running time of Up's "Married Life" sequence was not verified in the sources read.
- No empirical study found comparing understated and melodramatic tellings of the same death for audience emotion.

## 3. Death and loss in games: what designers did and said

### Takeaway
The most cited game deaths either make the loss something the player's own hands feel (Brothers), make inevitability a gift instead of a failure (Edith Finch, That Dragon Cancer), or build the goodbye out of repeated care (Spiritfarer). Designers who made sudden deaths work had many hours of bond behind them and warned against diluting one death with others.

### Cited Findings
- Brothers: A Tale of Two Sons. Josef Fares maps the left hand to the older brother and the right hand to the younger, so that after the older brother dies "you actually lose one physical hand and stop using it", giving "that feeling that you're missing something". He calls the final button press "the essence of the whole game itself" and "such a hard moment, and it felt so good". The burial scene is drawn from his own childhood, when at about seven his mother lost a baby and "gave him to me to bury him". He rejected co-op: "I would rather cut my hands than do that" ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2013/09/27/afterwords-brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons)).
- Final Fantasy VII. Tetsuya Nomura (Electronic Gaming Monthly, 2005, as quoted): "death should be something sudden and unexpected, and Aerith's death seemed more natural and realistic"; he also tied her death to the game's theme of life and argued that killing more of the party afterward would dilute its meaning ([Fanlore](https://fanlore.org/wiki/Aerith), secondary). Director Yoshinori Kitase originally planned to kill most of the party, and the team cut it back to Aerith ([Critical Hits](https://criticalhits.com.br/games/squaresoft-quase-matou-um-monte-de-gente-em-final-fantasy-vii-e-nao-apenas-aerith/), secondary, Portuguese).
- What Remains of Edith Finch. Ian Dallas: "There's an inevitability to each of these stories, since you know this family member is going to die by the end of it, but that inevitability is oddly liberating"; "Knowing that you can't stop it also means knowing that you can't fail. You're given permission to enjoy the ride." He frames the game as about facing what you cannot control, with death as one example ([GamingTrend](https://gamingtrend.com/feature/interviews/interview-ian-dallas-talks-about-ambiguity-cut-features-and-magic-in-what-remains-of-edith-finch)).
- That Dragon, Cancer. Ryan Green: "Decisions you make don't change how the story ends", because the story is true and Joel does not survive; the aim was to mourn "in the way we knew how, and hopefully by doing so, invite other people to mourn with us" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/03/22/the-creator-of-that-dragon-cancer-talks-about-loss-love-and-indie-art); [Today](https://www.today.com/health/new-video-game-mirrors-dad-s-grief-over-his-dying-t17506)).
- Spiritfarer. Nicolas Guérin: the hug "allowed us to reinforce mechanical ways to take care of others"; the team wanted to treat "the grave subject matter of death and dying, but in a cozy and wholesome way", because "we spend too little time thinking about it or preparing for it"; he met patients and caregivers in end-of-life care before the project; the Everdoor took "quite a few iterations" and became "a half circle bridge reflecting on water" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/inside-the-thoughtful-design-of-thunder-lotus-i-spiritfarer-i-)). GDC 2021 talk: "Spiritfarer's Journey: Crafting Tenderness" ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1027409/-Spiritfarer-s-Journey-Crafting), abstract only).
- Sabine Harrer's open-access book *Games and Bereavement* (2018) analyses character losses in Final Fantasy VII, Ico, Passage and Brothers, runs a participatory design study with grievers, argues that game design can make difficult personal feelings tangible, and proposes a model for including grievers in development (catalogue description) ([OAPEN](https://library.oapen.org/handle/20.500.12657/25213)).
- Attachment to game characters takes seven forms in a survey of 213 players, from excitement about a character's skill, through admiring them as role models, to deep concern for their well-being (Bopp et al., CHI PLAY 2019, abstract) ([Aalto](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/6cef35fe-1653-4627-912d-79a8947070fe)).

### Inferences
- Brothers is the closest model for this story's Dad thread. The draft already has a repeated input pair, "home" and "ok.". If the player sends "home" with their own tap at several adult visits and Dad's "ok." answers each time, then after his death the same tap sends "home" and nothing comes back. The loss is felt in the thumb, exactly Fares' "missing hand", and it needs no narration. It also sets up the final scene, where the old parent waits for the same word.
- The dog's last walk, where the player holds, is the right kind of interactive mourning because the act is care (walking him), not a ritual on command. Avoid any hold or tap that performs the euthanasia or the farewell on order (the Press F failure). The gate afterwards is the Edith Finch sealed room: the place stays, the occupant does not. If the gate scene recurs once more with the player's hold doing nothing (no dog comes), the absence becomes something the player does.
- Nomura's dilution warning matters more here than his "sudden" rule: five deaths in 20 to 30 minutes is far denser than FFVII's one. The deaths that should get the full three-beat treatment are the ones the ending pays off (Dad's unanswered "home", Mom's last text); Grandpa and Grandma can stay brief on purpose, with Grandpa teaching the visual grammar.
- The life-lines ring is a memorial device like Edith Finch's family tree, which Dallas used as a pause after each death. The first use (Grandpa, low weight) teaches what the ring means; later rings then land on sight. A held beat on each ring, ended by the player's tap, gives the "room to breathe" Dallas described.
- Dallas and Green both make inevitability a relief for the player: nothing could have been done, so the player is not blamed. In this story that keeps guilt with the character's omissions (the deleted "home") rather than with the player's skill.

### Gaps
- The Telltale design interview on Lee's death (Giant Bomb, "Faces of Death, Part 5") returned HTTP 403 and was not read; no primary designer account of Lee's final scene was obtained.
- No primary developer sources were read for Red Dead Redemption 2 (Arthur), To the Moon, Gris, Florence's breakup or Sea of Solitude in this pass; Florence is covered in the earlier report.
- No research found that measures how many minutes of play between a character's introduction and death are needed for attachment. Game examples range from the opening minutes (Up's montage in film) to tens of hours (Aerith, Lee, Arthur), and none is a controlled comparison.

## 4. Grieving players, care and content notes

### Takeaway
Bereaved players can find meaning in loss games, but sudden or unexpected deaths that resemble their own push them away, and low-pressure pacing with no fail states is what lets them reflect. The draft's deaths (old age and illness, off screen, told through messages) are the gentle kind.

### Cited Findings
- Eum and Doh 2023 (*Frontiers in Psychology*): 12 bereaved adults (mean age 25.4) played Bear's Restaurant and Spiritfarer for seven days with diaries and screenshots, then were interviewed for about 1.5 hours. Themes by share of coded instances: reviewing the meaning of loss 24%, acknowledging the deceased's perspective 21%, recognising positive emotions 18%, planning a better future 16%, recalling memories 10%, avoiding engagement with the pain 5%, fulfilling a wish 5%. The two participants who avoided engagement both had sudden, unexpected deaths and high closeness. Death scenes resembling participants' own losses, especially unexpected ones, led to shock and avoidance. Games with no time limits, fail states or penalties supported reflective play; scenes of caring and being supported prompted gratitude; "planning a better future" meant committing to closer relationships. Wish fulfilment happened only when a character closely matched the deceased ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10426378/)).
- Limits of that study: small student sample, participants knew it was about bereavement, and the authors claim no clinical benefit (same source).
- Content warnings: a meta-analysis of 12 studies found warnings raised anxiety beforehand without reducing distress afterward (already in the earlier report) ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/2023-october-content-warnings-distress.html)).
- The Distancing-Embracing model names control over how long one stays with a work as part of what makes negative art bearable ([Cambridge PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/FAAF19734BAB559CD439BB46F8BDC571/S0140525X17000309a.pdf/div-class-title-the-distancing-embracing-model-of-the-enjoyment-of-negative-emotions-in-art-reception-div.pdf)).

### Inferences
- Keep every death off screen, non-violent and already happened by the time the player learns it (the draft does this). The one place a grieving player could be hit by "sudden and unexpected" is the final wait if it reads as the kid not coming home; the earlier report already flags keeping that wait short and the reply certain.
- Every loss beat should wait for the player's tap, with no auto-advance timer, so pace stays in the player's hand. This is the Eum and Doh condition and the Distancing-Embracing "distancing" in one rule.
- The "planning a better future" theme (16%) is the outcome this story wants (players reaching out). It came from scenes of care, not scenes of dying, which again says the weight belongs to the love around the loss.

### Gaps
- No study found on loss-themed games with older bereaved players or with parents who lost a parent; the one study is of people around 25.
- No study found on short (under an hour) loss games or on mobile play specifically.

## 5. Pacing, silence and music around a loss

### Takeaway
Music that matches a sad scene deepens immersion and colours how the character's feeling is read, while taking music away tends to unsettle and make viewers re-evaluate. That suggests silence for a loss that should feel wrong (an unanswered message) and a returning theme for a loss that should feel loved. No research gives a number of seconds to linger.

### Cited Findings
- Viewers were more emotionally and cognitively immersed when a film had its soundtrack than when the music was removed, but only when the music matched the story's emotion; music that conflicted with the film erased or reversed the effect (Costabile and Terman 2013; research summary) ([OnFiction](https://www.onfiction.ca/2015/08/research-bulletin-how-music-affects.html)).
- 177 undergraduates read characters' emotions in line with the emotion of the accompanying music (Tan, Spackman and Bezdek 2007; abstract record) ([Sonicfield library](https://labs.sonicfield.org/library/viewers-interpretations-of-film-characters-emotions-effects-of-presenting-film-m)).
- In a qualitative study of 30 viewers of *Joker*, recurring cello motifs strengthened alignment with the protagonist, and scenes without music disrupted that alignment and brought discomfort and re-evaluation of earlier sympathy (Pirhan and Özad 2025, *SAGE Open*; abstract record) ([CiteDrive](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/film-music-silence-and-moral-alignment-a-joker-case-study-in-northern-cyprus/)).
- Sloboda's study of passages that brought listeners to tears is reported to have found appoggiaturas in 18 of 20 such passages (journalistic summary, original not read) ([repost of Michaeleen Doucleff's article](https://garydavidstratton.com/2012/02/17/anatomy-of-a-tear-jerker-why-does-adeles-someone-like-you-make-everyone-cry-by-michaeleen-doucleff/)).
- Up's lifetime-and-death sequence uses music and pictures only, with a recurring music-box theme ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/movies/2022/6/14/23166006/up-first-10-minutes-making-of)); Edith Finch places a pause screen after each death ([God is a Geek](https://www.godisageek.com/2017/04/interview-with-ian-dallas-creative-director-on-what-remains-of-edith-finch/)).

### Inferences
- Give each parent a short musical motif heard while they are alive (in an ordinary scene), and let it return in the aftermath of their loss. The returning theme is a keepsake in sound (Fiske) and a setup and payoff (Docter).
- Use silence once, where unease is the goal: the moment Dad's "ok." does not come. The Joker result says dropping music unsettles and makes viewers re-evaluate, which is the right feeling for an unanswered message and the wrong one for Mom's gentle text.
- Lingering, as a starting point to tune in playtests (no study supports specific numbers): the moment itself on screen for about as long as it takes to read twice, then the after-beat held until the player taps, then a ring on the life lines that also waits for a tap. Total weight per major loss comes from three tap-gated beats rather than from a longer timer.
- Keep the final "home" in the ending clear of any death beat just before it, so the peak at the end is not shared with a loss peak (peak-end; graded climax).

### Gaps
- No controlled study found that compares the same sad scene with music, with silence, and with music cutting out at the key moment.
- Grewe's work on chills at musical entrances could not be verified in this pass; only a weak flashcard source mentioned Huron's list of chill triggers (sudden changes in loudness, tempo or harmony), so it is not cited as evidence.
- No research found giving durations for how long to hold on a loss in film or games; Cutting's shot-length data and Walter Murch's editing priorities were not checked in this pass.
