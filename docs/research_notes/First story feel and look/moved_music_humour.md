# Being moved, music, humour and recurring threads (for a whole-life second-person web story)

Scope: what makes people feel moved (lump in the throat, tears, chills), how music, humour and recurring motifs produce it, plus the regret, friendship and ageing-parent research the story's beats lean on. Each section ends with inferences written as design rules. Anything not verified in this session is flagged.

Research note: PMC, MDPI, Springer and GameSpot pages blocked the fetcher, so some papers are cited from abstracts, repository records or reviews that summarise them. Where a claim comes only from a search-engine summary or a secondary write-up, it says so.

## 1. Being moved / kama muta: triggers, signs, physiology, and what it means for story beats

### Takeaway
Being moved ("kama muta") is a mostly positive emotion set off by a *sudden intensification* of a close, communal bond: love shown, a reunion, a sacrifice, kindness repaid, belonging. Its signs are moist eyes or tears, chills or goosebumps, warmth in the chest and a lump in the throat. The pattern that works best is longing or the threat of loss, then a sudden fulfilment. The story's peaks should be moments when a bond suddenly becomes visible, not just sad events.

### Cited Findings
- **The cross-cultural core study.** Zickfeld, Schubert, Seibt, Fiske and colleagues (*Emotion*, 2019) ran studies in 19 countries, in 15 languages, with 3,542 participants. Results support kama muta as a distinct positive social-relational emotion. It arises when people witness or experience a sudden increase in communal sharing. Reported signs: warm chest, moist eyes, chills, a lump in the throat, buoyancy, exhilaration. It motivates commitment to communal sharing. Its five facets correlated highly in every sample. ([ISCTE repository record](https://repositorio-iul.iscte.pt/handle/10071/16718); [UiO record](https://duo.uio.no/handle/10852/73382?show=full); [OpenU PDF, KAVIAR project](https://www.openu.ac.il/Lists/MediaServer_Documents/PersonalSites/DrRavitNussinson/Zickfeld%20et%20al.%20(2018)%20KAVIAR%20Project.pdf)) Read from the abstract via search summaries, not the full text.
- **Touching videos across five countries.** Seibt, Schubert, Zickfeld, Zhu, Arriaga, Simão, Nussinson and Fiske (*Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology* 49(3), 2018) used seven samples from the US, Norway, China, Israel and Portugal. In each sample, indicators of increased communal sharing predicted kama muta. Tears, goosebumps or chills, and warmth in the chest were the associated sensations. The emotion was experienced as predominantly positive and led viewers to feel communal with the characters who evoked it. ([eScholarship, full text PDF read pp. 1-2](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4zv550pt))
- **Prototype elicitors named in that paper's opening:** a soldier reunited with his daughter; men welcomed back by a lion they had raised; a doctor cancelling a huge bill out of gratitude for a kindness years before; a singer commemorating a massacre. ([Seibt et al. 2018](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4zv550pt))
- **The "longing, then sudden fulfilment" sequence.** Seibt et al.'s literature review cites Frijda (1988) on sentimentality as a precise sequence: attachment concerns are awakened, an expectation that they will *not* be fulfilled is evoked, and then they are abruptly fulfilled. Kuehnast et al. (2014) and Tan (2009) are cited as similar. ([Seibt et al. 2018](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4zv550pt))
- **Related constructs** in the same review. Elevation (Haidt, 2000) comes from seeing acts of high moral virtue. Menninghaus et al. (2015) hold that being moved comes from significant relationship or critical life events (deaths, births, weddings, reunions) that fit prosocial norms. Being moved is mostly positive (Hanich et al., 2014), though some argue it mixes positive and negative affect (Deonna, 2011; Menninghaus et al., 2015). Its motivation includes approach, prosocial behaviour and strengthened bonds. ([Seibt et al. 2018](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4zv550pt))
- **Physiology.** A psychophysiology study ("Tears of joy, aesthetic chills and heartwarming feelings: physiological correlates of kama muta", *Psychophysiology*, 2020) found that strong self-reported kama muta went with increased phasic skin conductance, skin temperature, piloerection (goosebumps) and zygomaticus (smiling-muscle) activity. There was no clear link to tearing or heart-rate variability. A search summary also mentions lower heart and breathing rates, which I could not confirm from the abstract text. ([ISCTE record](https://ciencia.iscte-iul.pt/publications/tears-of-joy-aesthetic-chills-and-heartwarming-feelings-physiological-correlates-of-kama-muta/73544)) The smiling-muscle result matters: being moved often looks like smiling through tears, not like grief.
- **The lump in the throat itself (globus sensation).** Popular-science sources explain it as tension in the pharynx and laryngeal muscles when emotional arousal holds the glottis open while swallowing or holding back a sob tries to close it. The sensation fades on calming. ([BBC Science Focus](https://www.sciencefocus.com/the-human-body/lump-throat-cry); [ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/why-do-we-get-a-lump-in-our-throats-when-we-re-sad)) **Unverified:** I found no peer-reviewed study of this mechanism. Treat it as a plausible model.
- **Kama muta and music.** Fiske's group assumes the same appraisal (a bond suddenly intensifying) applies to being moved by music, but a 2025 source notes this had not been tested directly. ([Frontiers in Cognition 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcogn.2025.1705976/full)) Beate Seibt (Oslo) lists being moved by events, narratives, videos and music among her research areas. ([UiO profile](https://sv.uio.no/psi/english/people/aca/beatesei))
- **Being there matters.** A 2024 Erasmus University study had 130 volunteers attend a classical or pop concert live, or watch the same concert as a livestream. The live group reported feeling more emotionally moved, more positive emotion and higher heart rates. ([EUR news](https://www.eur.nl/en/news/difference-between-live-music-and-streaming-concerts-being-there-person-better-your-well-being))

### Inferences (design rules)
- **Rule 1: Every big peak is a bond becoming visible, suddenly.** Use moments like the parent waiting up, the online friend answering at 3 a.m. without judging, the old friend's door turning out to be open, and you sending "text me when you get home". Sadness alone (death, loss) produces grief. It produces being moved only when the loss *reveals* the love.
- **Rule 2: Use the Frijda sequence for every peak.** (1) Make the reader care about a bond. (2) Make them expect it will not be honoured: the friend has drifted, the parent forgets, nobody is waiting. (3) Fulfil it abruptly and simply. The night of "nobody needs to know if you got home safe" is step 2. Sending the text yourself, years later, is step 3.
- **Rule 3: Second person is an advantage.** The research covers both *experiencing* and *witnessing* a bond intensifying. "You" lets the reader be both the one loved and, later, the one who loves.
- **Rule 4: Being moved smiles.** Peaks can be warm and funny at the same moment (zygomaticus activity). The final beat can end on a small smile rather than devastation.
- **Rule 5: The first minute should contain a mini kama muta beat.** One small, sudden act of communal love, like a parent's hand around a newborn's, or a grandparent's silly name for you. Put it next to a joke, so the reader is hooked on warmth, not only on curiosity.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed physiology of the "lump in the throat" (globus) during being moved was found. Only popular-science explanations.
- I could not read the full Zickfeld 2019 paper (blocked), so the facet names and scale items are not listed here.
- Whether kama muta is stronger for experienced versus witnessed bonds was not checked.

## 2. Music and chills; leitmotif return as payoff (Up, To the Moon, Journey, Gris)

### Takeaway
Chills and tears in music come from *expectation*: a build-up that predicts something, then the moment it arrives or is subverted. Tears are linked to appoggiaturas and melodic sequences, and chills to new or unexpected harmony, a new voice or instrument entering, crescendos, and a solo set against the full texture. Chill-inducing music is, on average, sadder, slower and more instrumental, and local context decides whether a trigger works. A theme introduced simply and early, then returned transformed at the peak, uses all of this.

### Cited Findings
- **Sloboda (1991)**, *Psychology of Music* 19(2), 110-120. 83 listeners. Over 80% reported shivers down the spine, laughter, tears and a lump in the throat to music. Structural analysis of the passages they named showed tears most reliably linked to melodic **sequences** and **appoggiaturas**, and shivers linked to **new or unexpected harmonies**. This supports expectancy-based accounts of musical emotion. ([Scilit record](https://scilit.com/publications/28754222606c17abaae30191e67bc59d); [SciSpace](https://www.typeset.io/papers/music-structure-and-emotional-response-some-empirical-366hteuxxx)) **Unverified detail:** a repost of a 2012 Wall Street Journal article says 18 of 20 tear-evoking passages contained appoggiaturas. I could not check this against the paper. ([repost](https://garydavidstratton.com/2012/02/17/anatomy-of-a-tear-jerker-why-does-adeles-someone-like-you-make-everyone-cry-by-michaeleen-doucleff/))
- **Grewe et al. (2007) and Guhn et al. (2007)**, as reviewed by Bannister and Eerola (2018). Grewe tied chills to dynamic changes, the **entrance of new voices or instruments**, and rises in loudness and sharpness. Guhn tied them to **interactions between a solo instrument and the accompaniment** and to expansions in frequency range. Reviewers caution that these features co-occur, for example a new instrument entering also makes the music louder. ([Bannister and Eerola 2018, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02046/full))
- **Panksepp (1995)**, via the same review. Chills clustered at a crescendo in a Pink Floyd track. For one piece, the chills depended on the sad, nostalgic mood the rest of the piece had set up. ([Bannister and Eerola 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02046/full))
- **Bannister and Eerola's own experiment** (N = 24 frequent chills-getters). Removing the chills sections from three pieces cut chills reports (10 vs 7, 10 vs 8 and 10 vs 6 people), but no single piece reached significance and the combined effect was marginal (p = 0.049). **Local context mattered.** In one piece a guitar solo remained the strongest trigger. Control passages with similar features (a new voice, a cello entrance, a dynamic change) did not produce the same response. Chills sections were rated more intense and, in some pieces, more "moving". ([Frontiers 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02046/full))
- **Expectation, fear and pleasure.** The same review cites Huron (2006): a violated expectation triggers a fast, fear-like appraisal, followed by a slower aesthetic appraisal that can turn into pleasure. Slow crescendos may also evoke "auditory looming". ([Frontiers 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02046/full))
- **Systematic review.** de Fleurian and Pearce (2021), *Psychological Bulletin* 147(9), 890-920, synthesised 167 research reports (1980-2020). They propose a preliminary model with three mechanisms for music-evoked chills: **arousal, expectation, emotion**. ([QMUL repository](https://qmro.qmul.ac.uk/xmlui/handle/123456789/75159))
- **Chills music is sad.** de Fleurian and Pearce (2021, *i-Perception*) compared 988 tracks reported to give chills with control tracks matched by artist, duration and popularity. The chills tracks were **sadder** (confirmatory), and in exploratory results **slower, less intense and more instrumental**. ([CAS catalogue record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsdoj,edsdoj.7c30c521a2214a22a9e6c8d3dd069773))
- **Expectation predicts chill onsets.** A 2024 preprint from the same group ("Expectation elicits music-evoked chills", over 1,000 pieces) reports that expectation predicts *when* chills start better than acoustic features do. ([OUCI record](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4VjBMQ54)) This is a preprint, not peer-reviewed.
- **Dopamine: anticipation, then peak.** Salimpoor, Benovoy, Larcher, Dagher and Zatorre (2011, *Nature Neuroscience* 14, 257-262) found dopamine released in the striatum at peak emotional arousal to music. The **caudate** was more involved during **anticipation** and the **nucleus accumbens** during the **peak** itself. ([Zatorre lab PDF](https://www.zlab.mcgill.ca/publications/docs/salimpoor_2011_nn.pdf); [Medical News Today summary](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/213412)) A blog repost puts the anticipation window at about 15 seconds before the chill. Not verified against the paper. ([Scientific American blog](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/scicurious-brain/repost-this-is-your-brain-on-music))
- **Up (Giacchino).** The waltz theme tied to Ellie is first heard **slow and spare** in "We're in the Club Now". It is fleshed out in "Married Life" with a striking violin statement, developed in "Carl Goes Up", and given its fullest treatment in the end credits. ([Apple Music album notes](https://music.apple.com/album/1440617705); [Filmtracks review](https://www.filmtracks.com/titles/up.html?mobile=yes)) Giacchino said the theme was designed to capture Ellie's spirit. ([All About Jazz](https://www.allaboutjazz.com/news/michael-giacchino-on-the-emotional-time-bomb-of-up))
- **Up's montage was stripped to music.** The "Married Life" sequence was first written with dialogue. Story artist Ronnie del Carmen proposed making it silent. Sound effects (jars of money smashing, a tyre popping) stayed in for a long time, then were cut too, leaving only the score. Docter, a silent-film fan, found that the less it had, the more emotional it felt. ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2022/06/14/movies/up-first-10-minutes-making-of); [Slashfilm](https://slashfilm.com/1582050/up-opening-emotionally-devastating-pixar-multiple-last-minute-changes-exclusive); [KPBS interview with Docter](https://www.kpbs.org/news/2009/may/29/interview-director-peter-docter/))
- **Journey (Wintory).** Wintory has said the game has only one theme and it evolves through the whole game. A solo cello represents the player. He described the score as a big cello concerto in which the player is the soloist and the other instruments are the world. He wrote the theme ("Nascence") on the day he was hired, about three years before release. ([SixthAxis interview](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2012/03/15/interview-journey-composer-austin-wintory/); [RNZ](https://www.rnz.co.nz/concert/programmes/musicalive/20200717); [Destructoid](https://destructoid.com/?p=126775); [CinemaBlend](https://cinemablend.com/games/Austin-Wintory-Interview-Exploring-Leisure-Suit-Larry-Reloaded-Banner-Saga-Journey-55833.html)) He also said he never wrote a cue without detailed implementation instructions from thatgamecompany. ([8dio interview](https://8dio.com/blogs/news/interview-with-austin-win))
- **To the Moon (Kan Gao).** "For River" is a piano piece Gao composed, and it appears in several versions on the soundtrack. ([Moegirl fan wiki](https://zh.moegirl.org.cn/For_River), a weak source) In one interview, Gao said the hard part was the timing of the music cues, not the music itself. ([Analog Addiction, part 1](https://analogaddiction.org/2014/09/05/to-the-moon-director-talks-about-a-bird-story-part-1)) The story began when his grandfather fell ill and he started thinking about mortality and regret. ([GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/?p=55683); [profile](https://st2.moderno.dk/kan-gao-to-the-moon-and-back.html))
- **Gris (Berlinist).** Berlinist is Marco Albano, Luigi Gervasi and Gemma Gamarra. The official piano arrangement notes say the pieces follow the protagonist's emotions as she heals, and colour is added the further the journey goes. ([Materia Collective](https://materiacollective.com/music/gris-piano-collections)) A popular reading maps the colour chapters to Kübler-Ross grief stages (grey, red, green, blue, yellow). That is a critic's interpretation, not a developer statement. ([Nicolle Lamerichs essay](https://nicollelamerichs.com/2021/02/22/game-theory-gris-and-grief-in-games/))

### Inferences (design rules)
- **Rule 6: One theme, many lives.** Like Journey, write **one main melody** and make every act a variation of it. The reader should not consciously notice it until the last act. Give it a clear contour, 6 to 8 notes, singable, with one **appoggiatura** (a leaning note that resolves down a step) at a phrase end. That leaning note is the "tear" device.
- **Rule 7: Introduce light, return transformed.** As in Up, the theme is first heard in childhood as something small and playful: a music-box timbre, faster, major key, staccato. At the peak it returns slower, lower and sparser (one voice, legato), possibly in another key or with one reharmonised chord (the "new or unexpected harmony" that gives shivers). In the very last beat, a second voice enters under it. That is the "new voice entering" chill trigger, and it can stand for you joining your parent's melody.
- **Rule 8: Build anticipation before every peak.** Dopamine is split between anticipation and arrival. Give the reader 10 to 20 seconds of build: a held drone, a rising filter, the melody's first half repeated. Then deliver the resolution exactly as the text lands. Do not land the chord before the words.
- **Rule 9: Sad context, then a warm trigger.** Chills come more often in sadder, slower, more instrumental music, and they depend on the mood set beforehand. So the peak should come *out of* a sparse, sad passage, not out of a happy one.
- **Rule 10: Stripping down is itself the climax tool.** Up got stronger every time something was removed. At the biggest moment, remove the sound effects and ambience and let one instrument carry the scene.
- **Rule 11: Chill triggers that a simple Web Audio synth can make:** (a) a new oscillator voice fading in; (b) a sudden widening of range (add a sub-octave and a high octave at once); (c) a crescendo over 4 to 8 bars; (d) a solo line after the full texture drops out; (e) one unexpected chord, for example the flat VI or a major IV in a minor context; (f) the return of the childhood melody. Use each at most once or twice in the whole story.

### Gaps
- No primary interview found in which Kan Gao explains how "For River" recurs or why. Track versions were not verified from an official source.
- No direct Giacchino quote found on *why* the waltz returns or how it was transformed (only album notes and reviews).
- No Berlinist interview found on matching music to colour or chapter.
- I did not read the full Sloboda (1991) or de Fleurian and Pearce (2021) texts, so the exact frequencies of each trigger are missing.

## 3. Adaptive music with simple generative audio: techniques and per-life-stage parameters

### Takeaway
The two standard game-music techniques both work with live Web Audio synthesis. *Vertical layering* adds or removes synchronised parts to raise or lower intensity. *Horizontal resequencing* jumps between loopable sections when the story moves on. Mode, tempo and register are the strongest emotional levers, and they combine roughly additively. Silence and stripping down are the strongest contrast tools.

### Cited Findings
- **Vertical layering** means several independent tracks playing in sync. The interactivity comes from changing which layers are active. ([Winifred Phillips, GDC 2021 series, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/blogs/pure-vertical-layering-for-game-music-composers-from-spyder-to-sackboy-gdc-2021-)) Layers are added or removed to vary the music, shift mood, and build or release tension. ([Audiokinetic on Joe Thwaites' Sackboy talk](https://www.audiokinetic.com/en/blog/how-to-use-interactive-music-to-score-gameplay/))
- **Horizontal resequencing** means building the music dynamically from loopable chunks, so it can jump to a new section or repeat one in response to the player. Sackboy split tracks into loopable chunks for this. Stingers and embellishments are listed as extra techniques. ([Audiokinetic](https://www.audiokinetic.com/en/blog/how-to-use-interactive-music-to-score-gameplay/)) Phillips' GDC 2021 lecture "From Spyder to Sackboy" also covers **hybrid** horizontal and vertical structures. ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/hybrid-horizontal-vertical-structure-for-game-music-composers-from-spyder-to-sackboy-gdc-2021-))
- **Which musical cues carry emotion.** Eerola, Friberg and Bresin (2013, *Frontiers in Psychology*): listeners rated 200 systematically varied examples for happy, sad, peaceful and scary. **Mode** was the most important cue, then tempo, register, dynamics, articulation and timbre, though the ranking varied by emotion. Cue effects were mostly linear and **additive** (no interactions) and explained 77-89% of the variance in ratings. ([PMC3726864](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3726864/); [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00487/pdf))
- **Mode and arousal.** Major mode goes with positive emotion (especially happiness) and minor mode with negative (especially sadness), citing Juslin and Laukka (2004). Loudness and high pitch go with high arousal. Slow tempo, legato and soft timbre go with low arousal (sadness, calm). Hevner's 1930s studies already found mode, tempo and rhythm to be the main determinants. (Same search set; [Eerola et al. 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3726864/))
- **Silence as a tool.** The best-documented case found was Up's montage, where cutting dialogue and then effects made it more emotional. ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2022/06/14/movies/up-first-10-minutes-making-of))

### Inferences (design rules and parameter sketch)
These are proposals built from the findings above. They have not been tested.

- **Rule 12: Build the score as stems the code can mix live.** Use 4 or 5 Web Audio layers on one shared clock: (1) pad or drone; (2) bass; (3) the melody (the theme); (4) rhythm, such as soft noise clicks or a pulse; (5) "sparkle", such as a high bell or arpeggio. Each layer gets its own GainNode, faded with `setTargetAtTime` over 1 to 4 seconds. Acts change *which* layers play (vertical). Story beats switch phrases or sections at bar lines (horizontal).
- **Rule 13: Change three levers per act, mode, tempo and register,** because they are the strongest and they add up. Then use layer count and timbre (filter cutoff, waveform) for finer shading.
- **Rule 14: Reserve one "music drops out" moment** for the loneliest scene (the night nobody needs to know if you got home). Fade everything to silence, or to one quiet room tone (low-passed noise), over several seconds. Keep it silent for a whole screen or two. Bring the theme back as a single line only when the next bond appears.
- **Proposed per-stage settings** (tempo in BPM; mode; register; layers; timbre; colour cue):
  - *Birth and early childhood:* about 96-108 BPM; major or Lydian (the raised 4th sounds wondrous); high register; pad and melody only, then add sparkle; sine or triangle with a short "music box" decay; warm pastels, soft light. The theme is heard for the first time here, playful and staccato.
  - *School, the best friend:* about 108-120 BPM; major or Mixolydian; mid-high register; add the rhythm layer and a second melodic voice for the friend that answers the theme (call and response); brighter, saturated colours. For the friend drifting away, take out the answering voice *one bar at a time across several scenes*, without comment, so the reader notices only later that it is gone.
  - *Teenage years, the online friend:* about 112-124 BPM; Dorian (minor but hopeful); add syncopation; a distinct "chiptune" square-wave timbre for the online friend, so it can come back decades later as a motif; night palette with screen-glow accents.
  - *Leaving home:* tempo eases to about 92-100 BPM; the theme in open fifths, a wider stereo image, one new instrument entering (a chill trigger); dawn or transit colours.
  - *Loneliness:* about 60-72 BPM or no pulse at all; Aeolian minor; low register; drone only, then silence (Rule 14); desaturated blue-grey, cold streetlight.
  - *Finding yourself, choosing your people:* layers rebuilt one at a time as each person enters (vertical); move from minor to major through a borrowed chord; the online friend's square-wave motif comes back; colour returns gradually (the Gris approach).
  - *Parents ageing:* about 66-76 BPM; the childhood theme returns slower, lower and legato, as a solo line with the appoggiatura held longer; amber and late-afternoon light. The parent's music-box timbre can now play under *your* melody, roles swapped.
  - *Old age and the ending:* the theme stated once in full (all layers, a slow crescendo, a new voice entering), then reduced to one line, then silence. Then the final message "text me when you get home", with a single held note or a resolved appoggiatura.
- **Practical note (general browser knowledge, not researched here):** mobile browsers start an AudioContext only after a user gesture, so the opening tap (for example "be born") should also start the audio.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study on silence or a sudden "music drop" as an emotional intensifier in games or film. The claim rests on practitioner accounts (Up).
- The GDC talks themselves (video or slides) were not reviewed. Only write-ups were.
- No research was found mapping specific BPM ranges to life stages. The numbers above are design guesses built from the cue research.

## 4. Humour and sadness together; setups, payoffs and recurring threads

### Takeaway
People really can feel happy and sad at the same moment, and bittersweet material reliably produces that mixed state. Arousal from one emotion can carry over and amplify the next one. Both support putting a light, funny beat right next to a sad one. Direct experimental proof that comic relief makes later sadness hit harder is thin. The craft evidence (Up, To the Moon) is that cute, funny details planted early become the very things that hurt when they come back recontextualised.

### Cited Findings
- **Mixed emotions are real.** Larsen, McGraw and Cacioppo (2001, *JPSP*) had viewers watch *Life Is Beautiful* with separate "happy" and "sad" buttons. About half pressed both at once, if only briefly. ([search summary of the literature](https://www.today.com/health/it-possible-feel-happy-sad-same-time-t128850); [SciSpace PDF, "Can people feel happy and sad at the same time?"](https://typeset.io/pdf/can-people-feel-happy-and-sad-at-the-same-time-5dlzxfsaku.pdf))
- Larsen and McGraw (2011, *JPSP*, "Further evidence for mixed emotions") found that people reported more *simultaneous* mixed emotions during a bittersweet film clip than during a control clip. The effect held even when they were told mixed emotions were not expected. ([SciSpace PDF](https://typeset.io/pdf/further-evidence-for-mixed-emotions-1bav3o3u2c.pdf)) A 2021 *Emotion* paper by Larsen, Hershfield and colleagues extends this work to reminiscence and meaningful endings. ([UCLA Anderson PDF](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/Larsen-Hershfield-et-al_2021_Emotion.pdf)) Only its framing was read, not its results.
- **Arousal carries over.** Zillmann's excitation-transfer theory: leftover arousal from one stimulus amplifies the emotional reaction to a later one, *whatever the valence of either*, especially when the person does not attribute the arousal to its real source. ([Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excitation-transfer_theory); [Encyclopedia.com, Arousal Processes and Media Effects](https://encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/arousal-processes-and-media-effects)) Secondary sources only. The claim that residual arousal fades within minutes comes from a weak source.
- **Comic relief, classic view.** Literary glossaries use Hamlet's gravediggers as the model case: we laugh, and the humour's seriousness intensifies the graver feelings. ([Macmillan glossary](https://www.macmillanlearning.com/studentresources/college/english/meyerlit9e/lit_gloss/comic_relief.html)) A popular piece argues that humour resets an emotional baseline numbed by continuous misery. ([Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20230512-comic-relief)) This is not peer-reviewed. Its "emotional blunting" claim is not sourced.
- **Up's montage plants and pays off.** It opens like a fairy tale: zoo jobs, painting the house in Ellie's colours, picnics, clouds shaped like animals and babies, a nursery being decorated. Then a slow pan to a darkened doctor's office. The team once considered cutting the miscarriage, but the film felt less affecting without it. Near the end, the same lateral camera moves return when Carl looks through the adventure book. ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2022/06/14/movies/up-first-10-minutes-making-of)) Docter said every shot in the montage is a setup for something used later, and that the aim was to tie the house to Ellie and to the adventure they never took. ([Flickering Myth](https://www.flickeringmyth.com/up-at-15-the-story-behind-pixars-poignant-animated-adventure); [Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/70909/15-adventurous-facts-about)) Docter attributes the universal response to the wish for a lifelong partner and the fear of losing one. A 16-year-old with no such loss still cried. ([The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2022/06/14/movies/up-first-10-minutes-making-of))
- **To the Moon's woven threads.** Reviewers note that the paper rabbits and the toy platypus look odd at first and later turn out to carry deliberate meaning. The rabbit ties back to a childhood stargazing scene. The "meet on the moon" line is planted early and returns at the end. ([Gnome Stew review](https://gnomestew.com/steampowered-to-the-moon-review/); [UFV Cascade review](https://ufvcascade.ca/cascade-rewind-platypuses-promises-and-pixelated-devastation/)) High Def Digest describes the game's constant, often juvenile humour as a coping mechanism for its sentimentality. ([High Def Digest](https://www.highdefdigest.com/blog/?p=44069)) Players in a Steam thread name the platypus origin and the final hand-holding scene as the saddest moments. One says the long action sequences before the end weakened it. ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/206440/discussions/0/846959520866767232)) This is a reminder that *pacing*, not only humour, controls the landing.
- **Kama muta link.** The "longing, then sudden fulfilment" sequence (Section 1) is itself a setup and payoff: the attachment is planted first, then honoured. ([Seibt et al. 2018](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4zv550pt))

### Inferences (design rules)
- **Rule 15: Plant everything funny so it can come back sad, and vice versa.** Every running gag should have a last appearance with a new meaning:
  - *"Text me when you get home."* Mum says it when you are 15; you roll your eyes (a gag, 2 or 3 times). At 23, nobody says it (the silence of Rule 14). At the end, you say it to your own child or your ageing parent (the payoff).
  - *The online friend's tell.* For example, always typing "..." for too long, or a silly sign-off. It is funny every time, and in a late act the same tell appears when they are there for you.
  - *The best-friend handshake or inside joke.* Shown complete in childhood, half-forgotten in the teens, then remembered by one of you decades later (the door was open; Section 5).
  - *A grandparent's or parent's silly name for you.* It later becomes the name you use for them when they need care (Section 6).
- **Rule 16: Put the joke one beat before the turn, not on top of it.** Up's fairy-tale run of clouds and the nursery comes right before the darkened office. Laughter raises arousal, and that arousal carries into the sad beat (excitation transfer). Never put a joke *inside* the peak line itself.
- **Rule 17: Make mixed feelings the target, not pure sadness.** The best moments are bittersweet: the funny line is the sad line, as in the eye-roll that becomes the text you send.
- **Rule 18: The structure of "everything connects".** Give each act at least one object, phrase or melody that returns at least twice in a later act, changed. Keep a planted-items table in the code (`motif id → acts where it appears → meaning each time`) so the payoffs cannot be forgotten.
- **Rule 19: Keep the stretch before a peak short and quiet.** The To the Moon player feedback suggests long, busy sequences before the ending dull it. The last act should be lean.

### Gaps
- I found **no experimental study** directly showing that a comic beat before a sad beat increases the sad beat's intensity. Rule 16 rests on excitation-transfer theory, mixed-emotion research and practitioner craft.
- No Kan Gao interview was found explaining why he uses Watts and Rosalene's banter or how he balances humour and sadness. GameSpot's postmortem Q&A was blocked (HTTP 403). Nothing was found on the humour in *Finding Paradise* or *Impostor Factory*.
- No sourced Pixar statement on humour placement was found (e.g. Docter or Andrew Stanton on laughs before tears).
- **Not fetched, verify before use:** Ersner-Hershfield, Mikels, Sullivan and Carstensen (2008, *JPSP*) on "poignancy", mixed emotion at meaningful endings, which is highly relevant to "the last time" scenes. It is cited from memory only.

## 5. Regret and choice; friendship drift; how long a friend takes; The Tail End

### Takeaway
In the short term people regret what they did. Over a lifetime the inactions, the things they never did, loom larger, though this pattern only partly replicates. Pink's crowd-sourced survey puts "connection" regrets (drifted relationships never repaired) among the most common, and his message is that the door is usually still open. Friendships decay without contact far faster than family ties. Close friendships take 200+ hours of shared leisure to form. Tim Urban's arithmetic is sound under his stated assumptions: by 18, about 93% of in-person time with parents is used up.

### Cited Findings
- **Gilovich and Medvec (1995, *Psychological Review*, a review).** Actions (errors of commission) cause more regret in the short term, and inactions (omissions) more in the long run. Psychological processes soften the pain of regretted actions over time, strengthen the pain of regretted inactions, and keep the inactions more available in memory. ([search summaries of the review](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/995800952)) **Kahneman's objection:** long-term regrets of inaction are mostly wistful rather than painful. The joint resolution (Gilovich, Medvec and Kahneman, 1998, "Varieties of regret") concluded that some inaction regrets are wistful and others are troublesome. ([Kellogg research page](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/1998/varieties-of-regret-a-debate-and-partial-resolution))
- **Replication status (mixed).** A 2022 preregistered replication by Yeung and Feldman supported three of four paradigms from Gilovich and Medvec (1994), but Study 5 (the lifetime action and inaction pattern) did not replicate. ([FORRT replication atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.357/)) Richardson and Gilovich (2023, *Royal Society Open Science*, "A very public replication of the temporal pattern to people's regrets") used museum visitors. It replicated the significant interaction between action and inaction and time perspective, but the exact pattern differed from the original. ([PMC10282588](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10282588/)) A secondary source puts the sample at about 2,600 usable responses. Not verified.
- **Daniel Pink, *The Power of Regret* (2022), World Regret Survey.** The survey began online and gathered about 15,000 regrets from 100+ countries, later 23,000+ from 109 countries. It is *not a random sample*, so Pink avoids claims about national differences. In a separate quantitative survey, 83% said they look back and wish they had done something differently, at least occasionally. Young people report about equal numbers of action and inaction regrets. With age, inaction regrets dominate, overall about twice as common. ([Behavioral Scientist interview](https://behavioralscientist.org/what-is-the-power-of-regret-a-conversation-with-daniel-pink/)) Another interview gives 24,000+ regrets from 109 countries. ([LEADERS magazine](https://leadersmag.com/issues/2023.2_Apr/Resilience/LEADERS-Daniel-Pink.html))
- **Pink's four core regrets:** **foundation** (early small choices that compound, such as saving, health or study), **boldness** (playing it safe: not travelling, not asking someone out, not starting something), **moral** (doing wrong), **connection** (relationships that drifted or were not repaired). ([LEADERS](https://leadersmag.com/issues/2023.2_Apr/Resilience/LEADERS-Daniel-Pink.html); [Behavioral Scientist](https://behavioralscientist.org/what-is-the-power-of-regret-a-conversation-with-daniel-pink/)) The Behavioral Scientist summary describes connection regrets as the largest category. I did not verify this against the book.
- **Connection regrets mechanism and the door.** Relationships usually fade slowly and quietly. People want to reach out but fear it will be awkward or unwelcome. The longer they wait, the more awkward it feels, so they wait longer. Pink separates **open-door** regrets (the person can still be reached: act now) from **closed-door** ones (feel it, accept it, do better next time). His advice when unsure is to reach out. He admits he was frustrated with interviewees who did not call, then realised he did not follow his own advice. ([Behavioral Scientist](https://behavioralscientist.org/what-is-the-power-of-regret-a-conversation-with-daniel-pink/))
- **Bronnie Ware, *The Top Five Regrets of the Dying* (2011).** A former palliative-care nurse's reflections, first on a blog. The list, as widely reported: wishing you had lived true to yourself rather than to others' expectations, worked less, expressed your feelings, stayed in touch with friends, and let yourself be happier. **Evidence strength: anecdotal**, from conversations with patients, with no systematic sample, coding or count. ([Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/us/blog/mindful-relationships/202312/how-to-live-what-the-dying-tell-us); [Jumpseat News repost](https://jumpseatnews.com/2012/6/top-five-regrets-of-the-dying/)) A Psychology Today piece says quantitative research supports many of the items but does not name the studies, so that is unverified.
- **Friendship decay vs kin.** Roberts and Dunbar (2011, *Evolution and Human Behavior*) followed 25 students over 18 months as they moved from school to university. The emotional intensity of *friendships* was more sensitive than that of kin ties to drops in contact frequency and in shared activities. Friendships cost much more to maintain. ([CORE PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/131226437.pdf); [Oxford record](https://www.neuroscience.ox.ac.uk/publications/572905))
- Roberts and Dunbar (2015, *Human Nature* 26(4), 426-450): the decline in friendship quality during a life transition was offset by more effort, with a gender difference. Women's friendships held up when they **talked** more, men's when they **did more activities together**. ([Bib-BVB record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_887497992))
- **How long a friend takes.** Jeffrey Hall (2018, *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships*) studied 355 adults who had recently moved and 112 first-year students over nine weeks. It takes about 40-60 hours to go from acquaintance to casual friend, about 80-100 hours to become a "friend", and more than 200 hours to become a close or best friend. Time spent hanging out, joking around and gaming counts. Time spent working together counts much less. ([University of Kansas news](https://news.ku.edu/2018/03/06/study-reveals-number-hours-it-takes-make-friend))
- **Tim Urban, "The Tail End" (Wait But Why, 2015), checked.** Assumptions: Urban is 34, his parents are in their mid-60s, he lives to 90, and his parents live while he is in his 60s, about 30 more years. He spent time with his parents on at least 90% of days for his first 18 years. Since college he sees them about 5 times a year for about 2 days, about 10 days a year. That leaves about **300 days**, fewer than in any single childhood year. By the end of high school he had used about **93%** of his lifetime in-person time with his parents, with about 5% left. Siblings: about 15% of the hangout time left. His high-school friend group (about 700 hangouts in 4 years, now about 10 days a decade): the final 7%. Takeaways: living near people matters (about 10 times more remaining time with people in the same city), priorities should be set on purpose, and quality time matters. ([Wait But Why](https://waitbutwhy.com/2015/12/the-tail-end.html))
  - *Arithmetic check (mine):* 18 years × 365 × 0.9 ≈ 5,913 days. Plus 16 years × 10 ≈ 160 (ages 18-34). Plus 30 years × 10 = 300 still to come. Total ≈ 6,373. 5,913 / 6,373 ≈ 92.8% used by 18, and 300 / 6,373 ≈ 4.7% left. **The numbers hold under his assumptions.** They are one person's illustration, not population data, and they swing with distance, visit frequency and lifespan.

### Inferences (design rules)
- **Rule 20: The regret choice should be inaction vs action, with time shown.** Let "you" choose between two regrets, for example the bold move or the safe one. Show the short-term sting of the action right away, and let the inaction come back quietly in a later act. Mark this as a *tendency*, not a law. The replications are mixed, and the game should not preach.
- **Rule 21: The friend who drifts is a connection regret with an open door.** Make the drift silent and two-sided (Roberts and Dunbar: friendships fade without contact; Pink: slow and quiet). Later, offer one tap to reach out. If you do, the old friend answers warmly (the open door). If you don't, the story can close that door near the end (a closed-door regret, felt and accepted, not punished).
- **Rule 22: The online friend is the counter-example.** They are "there" through talk alone, which fits the finding that talking keeps some friendships alive. Show their hours adding up in small, light scenes (Hall: leisure time builds closeness).
- **Rule 23: A Tail End beat, shown rather than told.** At "leaving home", one quiet visual: a grid of dots for days with your parents, mostly already filled. Present it as *your* life's count, not a lecture, and do not cite numbers in the story text. Later, in the ageing-parents act, the few remaining dots return.
- **Rule 24: Don't present Bronnie Ware's list as data.** If the story echoes her themes (staying in touch, saying what you feel), it should come through scenes, not as a statistic.

### Gaps
- I found **no verified primary study** for the popular claim that most friendships end by fading rather than conflict. Blogs cite a "2023 *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships* study" on drifting, but I could not find or verify it. Roberts and Dunbar support decay without contact, not the "most friendships" claim.
- The full texts of Hall (2018), Richardson and Gilovich (2023) and Pink's book were not read. The split between categories in Pink's survey is unverified.
- No sourced critique of Bronnie Ware's method was found beyond its anecdotal nature.

## 6. Parents ageing and role reversal (filial maturity)

### Takeaway
Gerontology frames the change not as "your parents become your children" but as **filial maturity** (Blenkner, 1965). After a mid-life "filial crisis", the adult child sees the parent as a person with their own needs, becomes someone the parent can depend on, and the relationship becomes more equal and sideways rather than flipped. Being depended on by a parent is different in meaning from being depended on by a child. A true, gentle scene keeps the parent's dignity and history visible.

### Cited Findings
- **Blenkner (1965):** filial maturity is a developmental stage reached by resolving a "filial crisis", usually in the middle years (often put around 40-50). The adult child realises the parent is becoming dependent on them and can be depended on in turn. ([Springer, J Adult Development 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10804-015-9207-4); [Springer, earlier article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02285778)) Search summaries only; the full texts were blocked.
- A Portuguese review describes overcoming the crisis as recognising that the parent also needs support and comfort, and relating to the parent more **horizontally**, beyond the strictly parental role. ([OasisBR record, "Maturidade filial e cuidados a pais envelhecidos"](https://oasisbr.ibict.br/vufind/Record/RCAP_779e00c713e99bd8a1ead4e449ca4ded))
- **Later models.** Marcoen treats filial maturity as ongoing, successful coping with the normative task of parent care, with seven components: four about commitment to support, the rest about **filial autonomy**. Nydegger (1991) keeps Blenkner's relational emphasis. Cicirelli noted that Blenkner's concept was hard to research. **Brody** argued that being depended on by a parent differs in meaning from being depended on by a child. ([search summary of MDPI *Geriatrics* 9(1):17, "Filial Maturity and Caregiving to Aging Parents"](https://Mdpi.Com/2308-3417/9/1/17); [MDPI IJERPH 20(1):761](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/20/1/761))
- **Empirical work.** In a study of 304 adult children aged 35-64 with a living parent aged 65 or older, attachment and mental representations of caregiving explained 24.5% of the variation in the "Comprehending" dimension of filial maturity and 11.1% in "Distance". (Same MDPI summary) A 1995 *Journal of Psychology* study reported a late-life period of heightened filial anxiety prompted by an ageing parent's care needs. ([Scilit](https://scilit.com/publications/6456ec76ca95faa1ed7056e911554a4e)) Work on dementia care describes becoming filially mature as grieving, mourning and letting go of old rules about the relationship with parents. ([Scilit, "Negotiating Family Relationships: Dementia Care as a Midlife Developmental Task"](https://scilit.com/publications/bfb2ebfd7d6c4f419d4b194143f2c363))
- **"Role reversal" is a different, mostly negative construct** in the literature: children taking on parental, spousal or peer roles toward a parent, which blurs generational boundaries. It is linked in students to "pseudo-maturity". It is not the same as filial maturity. ([Springer, J Child Fam Stud 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10826-019-01490-w); [Adelphi, "Parent-child role confusion: a critical review"](https://scholarlyworks.adelphi.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Parent-child-role-confusion-A-critical-review-of/991004311846206266))

### Inferences (design rules)
- **Rule 25: Mirror the gestures, keep the person.** The parent becoming "like a child" can be shown by *mirroring* earlier scenes. You tie their shoe as they once tied yours. You wait up for them. You say "text me when you get home". But the parent stays an adult with a history: a joke only they would make, a memory they correct you on. That is filial maturity, which is more equal and sideways, not infantilising.
- **Rule 26: The music mirrors the roles.** The childhood music-box theme (the parent's lullaby to you) returns with *your* hands playing it, slower and lower. The parent's old silly name for you becomes the name you use, gently, for them (Rule 15).
- **Rule 27: Let grief arrive early and quietly.** The dementia-care research names mourning and letting go of old rules. A small scene works: the parent forgets the running gag, and you finish it for them. That is the Frijda sequence (bond threatened, then honoured) and a likely lump-in-the-throat beat.
- **Rule 28: The final payoff completes the circle.** Sending "text me when you get home" is the moment you become the one who waits up. It is the cleanest kama muta trigger in the story: a bond made visible, suddenly, in one line. Pair it with the theme's last return and a single new voice entering (Rule 7).

### Gaps
- Blenkner's 1965 chapter and the full filial-maturity papers could not be read (Springer and MDPI blocked). Definitions come from abstracts and search summaries.
- No source was found on how *fiction* handles ageing-parent scenes well, or on audience responses to them.
