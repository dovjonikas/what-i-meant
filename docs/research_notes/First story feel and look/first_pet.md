# First pet: the dog's life and death in "Text me when you get home"

Scope: how to tell a first pet's arc (a dog arrives when "you" are about five, waits at the gate after school, grows old in your teens, is put to sleep while you are not there, a parent says "we had to let him go") so it lands with warmth and a lump in the throat without being manipulative or cheesy; what is actually known about dogs greeting and waiting; what films and games did well or badly; which dog behaviours make good, short humour; how to draw and animate the dog in a flat, faceless-adjacent style.

Source quality note: pages fetched and read in full this session: ScienceDaily on Crawford et al. 2020 and on Russell 2016, KidsHealth (Nemours) on pet death, the Psychology Today summary of Adrian and Stitt 2019, the NPR story on Donut the dog (LAist mirror), Wendy Packman's Open to Hope essay, the SYFY Wire interview with Eric Kaplan, and the ludo.guide Firewatch prologue walkthrough. Every other finding below comes from search-result snippets and is marked (snippet). Several primary papers (Kaufman and Kaufman 2006, Gomez et al. 2018, Rehn and Keeling 2011, Wiseman et al. 1998, Sommese et al. 2021, Leonetti et al. 2024) were seen only as abstracts or summaries, not full text. No AVMA or Association for Pet Loss and Bereavement page was read (see Gaps). All "design rules" are inferences for the author, not findings.

## 1. Children and a first pet's death: grief, wording, not being there, keeping something

### Takeaway
A pet's death is very often a child's first real loss, and it is not small: the largest study links it to measurable distress lasting years, and teens' pet grief is routinely brushed off by others. What helps is honest, plain wording, a chance to say goodbye, rituals, and a kept bond (an object, talking about the pet); what hurts is euphemism a child takes literally ("put to sleep" for small children), comforting lies ("ran away", "went on a trip"), suddenness, and being shut out of the goodbye. A death after a full life is accepted far more easily than an early or unexpected one.

### Cited Findings
**How common, how serious**
- Crawford et al. 2020 (European Child and Adolescent Psychiatry, Massachusetts General Hospital), 6,260 children in the ALSPAC cohort, Bristol: the press release calls a pet's death likely one of the first major losses a child meets; about 63% of children with pets experienced a pet's death in their first seven years. Pet death was associated with raised mental health symptoms, a possible sign of distress lasting three years or more; the link held regardless of socioeconomic status and earlier hardship, was stronger in boys than girls (which surprised the authors), and did not depend on when in childhood the death happened or how many pets died. The authors urge adults to take children's pet grief seriously. Source: [ScienceDaily, MGH release](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/09/200910130412.htm); also [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2020/09/losing-a-pet-can-affect-childrens-mental-health-study-finds/) (snippet).
- Russell 2016 (Canisius College), one-to-one interviews with children aged 6 to 13 (sample size not given in the release): children described pets as siblings or best friends and saw themselves as central to the pet's affection. They judged a death fair or unfair by whether the animal reached an appropriate age: short lives were normal for hamsters and fish, unexpected for dogs, cats and rabbits. Pets that lived a full lifespan or longer were accepted at death; sudden deaths were described as emotionally and morally unfair and were harder to cope with (one 13-year-old said his life was over, two years after his cat died). Children generally saw euthanasia as the moral choice when a pet is suffering. Family discussion and family rituals helped. On a new pet, children split: some felt it would dishonour the dead pet, others saw it as a new relationship that does not erase the old one. Source: [ScienceDaily, 2016](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/10/161019122554.htm).
- Kaufman and Kaufman 2006, "And Then the Dog Died", Death Studies 30: 61 to 76: a case analysis (one child with several prior losses) plus literature review, not a sample study. Grief was intensified by the child's attachment to the pet and by the suddenness of the death; emotional expression of grief and a cognitive approach to it helped prevent complicated grief. Source: [Death Studies record](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/07481180500348811); [scite summary](https://scite.ai/reports/and-then-the-dog-died-OEONR8) (snippet, abstract only).

**Teenagers**
- Brown et al. 1996, Journal of Counseling and Development 74(5): 505 to 509: 55 adolescents (27 boys, 28 girls) who had lost a pet in the previous 12 months with no other major life change; a stronger bond predicted more intense grief. Source: [journal index record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/20568065) (snippet).
- Gomez, Rappoport, Moses, Cohen, Packman, Naylor and Schmidt 2018, "Pet loss and continuing bonds in children and adolescents", Death Studies 44(5): 32 children aged 5 to 18 and their parents, mixed methods. All children kept a continuing bond with the dead pet; how they expressed it varied with age, level of grief and strength of attachment. Source: [library record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi.dedup.....8765bd2fda7c9dfab2da03d4060d8499) (snippet; the page itself was blocked; one later citation gives the year as 2020, likely an error).
- A German study of 226 mostly 14 to 16 year olds found mid-adolescents use grieving rules similar to adults, but are more willing than adults to count losses such as pets as real grief. Source: [GESIS record, Siebel 2024](https://data.gesis.org/gesiskg/resource/bibsonomy-siebel2024disenfranchised) (snippet).
- A small qualitative study of adolescents reported that their pet loss was often not validated by others and that they coped by keeping a continuing bond. Source: [IJARBSS, "When Best Friends Leave"](https://hrmars.com/ijarbss/article/view/28657/When-Best-Friends-Leave-Adolescents-Grief-Experiences-and-their-Coping-Mechanisms-in-Facing-Pet-Loss) (snippet; attribution of this summary to this paper is probable, not certain).
- An expert quoted in a news piece says the literature shows pet death is often trivialised, and that children are the biggest group of disenfranchised grievers. Source: [Arkansas Democrat-Gazette (evangelist.org mirror)](https://evangelist.org/news/2018/apr/06/parents-can-help-kids-mourn-loss-of-pet) (snippet).

**Wording: what helps, what hurts**
- KidsHealth (Nemours, medically reviewed June 2020): use clear words, "death" and "dying", as age allows; avoid saying a pet "got put to sleep", because young children take it literally and may fear sleep, surgery or anaesthesia; avoid comforting untruths such as "went on a trip", which do not ease sadness and can make the child angry when the truth comes out; match detail to age and maturity. Source: [KidsHealth, When a Pet Dies](https://kidshealth.org/AetnaBetterHealthVirginia/en/parents/pet-death.html?view=ptr).
- Baltimore Humane Society handout: avoid "ran away" or "went on a trip" (children can feel abandoned); a concrete alternative is to explain that the vet gives a shot that first makes the pet sleep and then stops its heart. Source: [BARCS/Baltimore Humane PDF](https://bmorehumane.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/Help-kids-with-pet-loss.pdf) (snippet).
- Some guidance is less absolute: a family that already uses "put down" may find it understandable. Source: [funeral.com, age-by-age scripts](https://funeral.com/blogs/the-journal/how-to-tell-kids-a-pet-died-age-by-age-scripts-3-5-6-9-10-13-teens-what-not-to-say) (snippet; a commercial blog, low authority).
- No source found evaluates "we had to let him go" specifically (see Gaps).

**Being there, or not**
- KidsHealth: many kids want a chance to say goodbye before the pet dies; some older or emotionally mature children choose to be present to comfort the pet; children commonly feel guilt (past unkindness, not caring for the pet as promised) and should be told these feelings are natural. Source: [KidsHealth](https://kidshealth.org/AetnaBetterHealthVirginia/en/parents/pet-death.html?view=ptr).
- A veterinarian's guidance (Lap of Love): tell the child before the euthanasia, not after, so they can say goodbye; a missed goodbye can leave lasting regret; younger children are usually better not present, school-age children can be asked. Source: [Lap of Love PDF](https://training.lapoflove.com/pet-loss-support/children-and-pet-grief-resources/a-veterinarians-perspective-children-and-euthanasia.pdf) (snippet).
- Adrian and Stitt 2019, Anthrozoös (adult guardians): 34% chose to be present at the euthanasia, 50% did not, 16% did not answer. Being present was not linked to complicated grief or PTSD, and was linked to greater acceptance of the death; guardians who were not present reported more regret. Source: [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/all-dogs-go-to-heaven/201910/should-you-be-present-for-the-euthanasia-of-your-pet) (secondhand; original paper not read).
- Conflicting adult data: a survey of 236 Hawaii pet owners found attending was not correlated with complicated grief or PTSD; another study lists presence among predictors of complicated grief (direction not clear from the abstract); an Italian study found more guilt in owners who felt the euthanasia came too early. Sources: [PMC7558086 PDF](https://rcastoragev2.blob.core.windows.net/2407dce7f0e34c3ac07d4f2448c4155f/PMC7558086.pdf); [Padua repository](https://www.research.unipd.it/handle/11577/2980302) (both snippet). No study found on children who were absent.

**Keeping something**
- Wendy Packman (psychologist, Palo Alto University; leads cross-cultural pet-loss research): some bereaved owners keep belongings to stay close (her examples: dog tags, a paw-print necklace), talk to the pet as if present, recall memories, make memorials or rituals, and reflect on what the pet taught them; for some this ongoing connection comforts and helps coping. Source: [Open to Hope essay, 2012](https://www.opentohope.com/continuing-connection-with-a-pet-that-has-died). (Note: one search summary called Packman "he"; she is a woman.)
- Habarth et al. 2017 (co-authored by Packman), as cited in a 2024 paper: continuing bonds show up as reminiscing, memorialising, keeping special possessions, and experiences such as hearing or feeling the presence of the dead animal. Source: [Lykins, McGreevy and Paul 2024, UNE repository](https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/58281) (snippet; Habarth not read).
- KidsHealth suggests a ceremony, sharing favourite memories and funny moments, a scrapbook, and talking about the pet often; it notes children may grieve a pet as deeply as a relative, and that a new pet should come not as a replacement. Source: [KidsHealth](https://kidshealth.org/AetnaBetterHealthVirginia/en/parents/pet-death.html?view=ptr).

### Inferences
Design rules for the death scene and its aftermath (all inference):
- **Old age, not an accident.** Russell's children accept a full-life death and call a sudden one unfair. The story wants a lump in the throat, not outrage, so the dog dies of age, after a visible decline the player has watched. No car, no sudden illness twist.
- **"We had to let him go" works for a teen, with one plain word near it.** The research against euphemism is about young children taking "sleep" literally; a 15 to 17 year old understands "let him go". The phrase also carries something true and useful: "had to" and "we" say a decision was made, by them, without you. That is the sting, and it is honest. Keep one plain word in the scene (the parent or the narration says he died) so it does not go vague. Never use "put to sleep" in any scene with the young child; never "ran away" or "went to a farm".
- **Not being there is realistic and research-backed as a source of regret, so use it quietly, not as punishment.** Adults who were absent report more regret; vets advise telling children before. A parent who tells after, to spare you, is common and forgivable. Two options:
  - (a) The parent told you the night before or that morning; you sat with him and did not know what to do. Lower regret, softer.
  - (b) The morning is completely ordinary and short, nothing flagged; the meaning arrives only at the empty gate. Stronger. It stays honest only if the morning is not telegraphed (no lingering camera, no "last look" line). If the morning is milked, it turns manipulative.
- **Answer the unasked question.** The deepest fear in pet death stories is that the animal was alone or scared (see Jurassic Bark in section 3). A parent's short, plain answer that someone held him turns devastation into warmth. Do not show the vet table.
- **Let the teen's grief be slightly unrecognised by someone, and recognised by someone else.** Teens' pet grief is often dismissed; one small beat (a friend who shrugs, or simply no one at school knowing) and one person who takes it seriously (the parent who knew him as long as you did) is true to the research without speeches.
- **One kept object, in one place.** Continuing bonds are normal at every age. The collar or leash stays on its hook by the door; the hook can outlive the dog by years and quietly reappear (moving out, coming home as an adult). One object, not a shrine. Talking to the dog once after he is gone is also documented behaviour and can be a single line.
- **No quick replacement.** Children are split on new pets; a new dog in the next scene would cheapen it. If another animal appears, much later, and as its own relationship.

### Gaps
- No source evaluates the phrase "we had to let him go" (or "let go") with children or teens; the advice found targets "put to sleep" and lies. AVMA and the Association for Pet Loss and Bereavement pages were not read.
- No study found on children or teens who were absent at a pet's euthanasia and told afterwards; the regret findings are from adults (Adrian and Stitt, secondhand).
- Packman et al. 2011 and Habarth et al. 2017 (continuing bonds, sensing presence, kept possessions) were not read in full; no percentages for collar or object keeping were found.
- Russell 2016's journal and sample size were not stated in the release.
- A 2022 systematic review reportedly found that continuing bonds can intensify grief when the grief is not socially recognised; the exact citation could not be pinned down.

## 2. Dogs and coming home: greeting, waiting, routine, ageing

### Takeaway
Dogs greet more intensely after longer absences (two hours and up versus half an hour) and the reunion is physical: contact matters to them. Dogs clearly learn daily routines and can be at the window before the school bus every day; how they time it is unknown (a scent-fading idea is untested), and the telepathic-dog claim (Sheldrake's Jaytee) was not supported by the independent test. In old age the greeting fades for ordinary reasons: deafness, deeper sleep, stiffness, and in some dogs cognitive decline, which lists "less interest in greeting" as a sign. A crossbreed lives about 12 years, so a puppy who comes when you are five dies when you are about seventeen.

### Cited Findings
- Rehn and Keeling 2011, Applied Animal Behaviour Science: 12 privately owned dogs without separation problems, filmed alone at home for 30 minutes, 2 hours and 4 hours. Behaviour during the separation did not change with duration, but at the owner's return, after 2 and 4 hours, dogs greeted more intensely, were more physically active and attentive, wagged their tails more, interacted more with the owner, and showed more lip licking and body shaking than after 30 minutes. 2 hours and 4 hours did not differ significantly. The authors could not tell whether dogs perceive the duration or only register it at the reunion. Sources: [SLU publication page](https://research.slu.se/en/publications/the-effect-of-time-left-alone-at-home-on-dog-welfare/); [PDF](https://slunik.slu.se/kursfiler/DO0031/40007.1213/The_effect_of_time_left_alone_at_home_on_dog_welfare.pdf) (snippet).
- Rehn et al. 2014 (12 female beagles, the familiar person returned and either touched and talked, only talked, or ignored the dog): the person's return alone raised oxytocin and contact seeking, but physical contact was needed for a sustained oxytocin rise and a cortisol drop; touched dogs initiated more contact. With talk only, early reactions were tail wagging and vocalising. Source: [SLU publication page](https://research.slu.se/en/publications/dogs-endocrine-and-behavioural-responses-at-reunion-are-affected-/) (snippet).
- A real "dog at the window before the bus" case, NPR 2023: Donut, a stray hound mix, came to a family when the boy was about 4 and stayed through his high school years. She slept on the living room rug, and every weekday, shortly before the bus dropped the brothers off, she got up, sat by the back-porch window, then greeted them at the door, always on time. This is family memory, not timed observation. Dog cognition researcher Alexandra Horowitz suggests the dog may read the boys' scent fading through the day and learn which faint level means the bus is near (the absences were the same length each school day). NPR states that nobody has tested this. Source: [NPR via LAist](https://laist.com/news/npr-news/can-dogs-smell-time-just-ask-donut-the-dog).
- Sheldrake's "dogs that know when their owners are coming home": Wiseman, Smith and Milton 1998 (British Journal of Psychology) tested Jaytee, the dog used in Sheldrake's work, and the data did not support the hypothesis that he psychically detected his owner's return. Sources: [University of Hertfordshire archive](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/2336/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9734300) (snippet). Sheldrake and supporters dispute this (Jaytee was at the window 65% of the time in the return period by Sheldrake's count), per the parapsychology-friendly [Psi Encyclopedia](https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/?p=1810) (snippet; partisan source). Not established science.
- Ageing and hearing: the AKC says hearing loss in senior dogs creeps up slowly; signs include not responding to the name and no longer running to the door at a knock; deaf dogs sleep more deeply, startle easily, and as mobility drops are more reluctant to get up to investigate sounds. Sources: [AKC](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/hearing-loss-senior-dogs-symptoms-management/); [Vetnique](https://vetnique.com/blogs/vets-corner/is-your-senior-dog-going-deaf) (snippet; Vetnique is a retailer blog).
- Canine cognitive dysfunction (DISHAA screening: disorientation, interactions, sleep-wake, house soiling, learning and memory, activity, anxiety) lists decreased interest in approaching, greeting or seeking affection under "interactions"; a single sign is not diagnostic. Source: [Morris Animal Foundation DISHAA tool](https://www.morrisanimalfoundation.org/sites/default/files/filesync/DISHAA-Assessment-Tool.pdf) (snippet).
- Lifespan: McMillan et al. 2024 (Scientific Reports, Dogs Trust, UK dogs only): crossbreeds' median life expectancy 12.0 years, pure breeds 12.7. Source: [Anglia Ruskin University news](https://www.anglia.ac.uk/news/new-research-calculates-a-dogs-life-expectancy) (snippet).
- Hachiko, the cultural reference: an Akita whose owner, Professor Hidesaburo Ueno, died suddenly at work on 21 May 1925; Hachiko kept going to Shibuya Station at the time the train came, for about nine years and nine months by the most cited count (accounts vary from seven to ten years); a 1932 newspaper article made him famous, a statue was unveiled in April 1934, and he died on 8 March 1935, about 11 years old (a 2011 study attributed death to cancer and heartworm). Source: [Nippon.com](https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-glances/jg00137/hachiko-the-faithful-dog.html) (snippet).

### Inferences
- **The gate ritual is factual, so the story can show it without explaining it.** Learned routine plus a fixed school day is enough; Donut is almost exactly the planned dog (arrives at four, waits for the bus until high school). If a character says "he knows before we do", it is a character's belief, never the narrator's claim. No telepathy, no "sixth sense" line.
- **Scale the greeting by absence.** A school day earns the full greeting (wag, wiggle, lip licks, a shake-off, jumping); a quick trip out earns a smaller one. This is both true and funny.
- **The greeting is physical.** Contact is what matters to the dog (Rehn 2014): the child kneels, the dog leans in. Later this becomes the reversal (below).
- **Age it through the greeting, in three steps the player can feel:**
  1. He still comes, but slower; meets you halfway down the path instead of at the gate.
  2. He hears you late; gets up after you are already at the door.
  3. He sleeps through your arrival (deafness, deep sleep); you have to touch him to wake him; he startles, then wags.
  Step 3 is the quiet hinge of the whole arc: the first day nobody is at the gate happens before he dies. And it flips the theme: now you are the one who checks, every day after school, that he is all right. The waited-for becomes the one who waits.
- **Timeline math:** puppy at your age 5, crossbreed median about 12 years, death at about your 17. Fits "teens" without stretching.
- **Hachiko: use sparingly or not at all.** Everyone knows it and it is a famous tearjerker; naming it in the story would point at the device. If used, as background texture (a picture in a school book) rather than a comparison.

### Gaps
- The often-repeated finding that dogs move an eyebrow or ears differently for their owner (attributed to Miho Nagasawa's group) was found only secondhand and is unverified.
- No peer-reviewed study found on how greeting intensity changes across a dog's lifespan (the ageing pattern above is assembled from veterinary sources on deafness and cognitive decline).
- No study tests the scent-fading timing hypothesis.

## 3. Pets in stories and games: what lands, what feels manipulative

### Takeaway
What lands: a pet built from real observed behaviour (Dug, Trico, Dogmeat), a small chosen detail that makes it yours (Firewatch's dog choice and the turtle), and a death whose force comes from revealed waiting or an empty place rather than on-screen suffering. What reads as manipulative: drawn-out death and funeral sequences, a dog killed as a lever for someone else's plot, and stories that punish the viewer with the animal's loneliness. Audiences actively dread dog deaths, so the story has to earn it with a full, funny life and keep the death short.

### Cited Findings
**Firewatch (Campo Santo, 2016)**
- In the text-only prologue, while you date Julia, she wants a dog and you pick a Beagle (Julia names it Bucket) or a German Shepherd (Henry names it Mayhem). Source: [ludo.guide prologue walkthrough](https://www.ludo.guide/guide/firewatch/prologue). The choice does not change where the story goes (Julia's early-onset dementia, Henry's flight to the tower). Source: [Calvin Pitt blog](https://calvinpitt.blogspot.com/2025/12/bury-ashes-of-your-past.html) (snippet); [GamesRadar on small choices](https://gamesradar.com/firewatch-oxenfree-mgs5-new-wave-of-moral-choices) (snippet).
- In the main game you can adopt a turtle found on a rock near a supply cache; if you tell Delilah, she offers names (fan wiki lists Turt Reynolds and Shelly Duvall); anything left in the lookout, the turtle included, is lost in the final fire unless you carry it out. Sources: [Firewatch wiki, The Turtle](https://firewatch.gamepedia.com/The_Turtle?oldid=3235); [TV Tropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Videogame/Firewatch) (snippet; fan sources).

**Up (Pixar, 2009), Dug**
- Bob Peterson (co-writer, Dug's voice) wrote Dug as a combination of all the dogs he had owned; the "squirrel" distraction came from a game he played with his dogs (stop suddenly as if he had seen something, and they would freeze to attention too); he built the voice on how he talks to his own dogs. Sources: [Movie Mom, Glenn Close interviews Bob Peterson](https://moviemom.com/glenn-close-interviews-pixars/); [HuffPost](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/pixars-bob-peterson-anima_b_460293) (snippet).

**Futurama, "Jurassic Bark" (2002)**
- Writer Eric Kaplan: the episode is about the stories we tell ourselves; Fry decides not to revive his dog Seymour because he believes the dog moved on and had a happy life; the ending reveals Seymour waited outside the pizzeria where Fry told him to wait, exposing that comforting story as false. Kaplan argues stories let us experience an ending we cannot report back from in life. Source: [SYFY Wire](https://www.syfy.com/syfy-wire/futurama-writer-most-devastating-moment-jurassic-bark). Kaplan reportedly insisted the dog's ending stay tragic; viewers split between a powerful statement on loyalty and a cruel manipulation; a later film softened it (Seymour was not alone). Sources: [Looper](https://www.looper.com/383184/how-futuramas-jurassic-bark-episode-could-have-been-sadder); [/Film](https://www.slashfilm.com/979689/futuramas-infamous-jurassic-bark-episode-could-have-been-even-more-grim) (snippet).

**Marley and Me (2008), Old Yeller (1957)**
- Slant Magazine called the death sequences exploitative and the closing backyard funeral, with the children reading letters to the dead dog, melodrama. Source: [Slant](https://www.slantmagazine.com/film/marley-and-me/) (snippet). Deep Focus Review called the film openly manipulative but successful sentimentalism, and still worth watching as a dose of reality, comparing the ending to Old Yeller. Source: [Deep Focus Review](https://www.deepfocusreview.com/reviews/marley-me/) (snippet). JoBlo found the ending hard to resist for anyone who has lost a pet. Source: [JoBlo](https://www.joblo.com/review-marley-me/) (snippet).
- Old Yeller is remembered as a boy bonding with a stray who is put down because of sickness. Source: [Culture Trip](https://theculturetrip.com/europe/united-kingdom/articles/childhood-tearjerkers-that-still-make-us-cry) (snippet). (The plot detail that the boy himself has to shoot the rabid dog is general knowledge, not verified this session.)

**Audience dread**
- Viewers' avoidance of dog deaths is widely discussed: dogs read as pure innocence with no role but being loved; filmmakers use a dog's death as a shortcut to make a villain (John Wick, House of Cards); a film's account reassured viewers that the dog in The Mountain Between Us survives; some viewers skip the scene on rewatch; the "does the dog die" habit is framed as emotional preparation. Sources: [AV Club](https://avclub.com/read-this-why-are-we-obsessed-with-dogs-dying-in-movie-1819283627); [Looper](https://www.looper.com/1220232/why-dog-death-scenes-in-movies-are-the-one-line-many-viewers-wont-cross); [Pajiba](https://www.pajiba.com/think_pieces/why-its-so-hard-to-watch-the-dog-die-in-movies.php); [MEL Magazine](https://melmagazine.com/en-us/story/why-are-we-more-willing-to-watch-a-movie-if-we-know-the-dog-wont-die) (all snippet).

**Games**
- Spiritfarer (Thunder Lotus, 2020): the core idea was to treat death and dying in a cozy, wholesome way; you care for spirits on your boat, they tell you when they are ready, and you escort them to the Everdoor (a half-circle bridge over water, designed by art director Jo Gauthier); you cannot progress without letting them go. Sources: [Game Developer interview with Nicolas Guerin](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/inside-the-thoughtful-design-of-thunder-lotus-i-spiritfarer-i-); [PC Gamer via Magzter](https://magzter.com/stories/Technology/PC-Gamer-US-Edition/LIFEBOAT); [Vice](https://creators.vice.com/en_us/article/n7wywg/spiritfarer-a-game-of-hugging-and-letting-go-is-perfect-for-right-now) (all snippet).
- The Last Guardian (2016): Fumito Ueda's team drove Trico with procedural animation and AI (for example it avoids stepping on things it would not want to step on), and called movement one of the hardest parts of development; Empire credited small behaviours, ear waggles and sniffing the wind, with making Trico feel like a real pet; Trico is timid and nervous, may refuse water, and becomes more responsive as the bond grows. Sources: [TheSixthAxis, Ueda on animation](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2010/09/02/fumito-ueda-talks-about-animation-in-the-last-guardian/); [Empire review](https://www.empireonline.com/gaming/reviews/last-guardian-review); [ActuGaming, from Edge](https://www.actugaming.net/the-last-guardian-sortie-confirmee-2016-informations-supplementaires-31139) (snippet).
- Fallout 4 (2015), Dogmeat: motion reference came from River, a German Shepherd owned by Bethesda staff; rather than a marker suit, they reportedly let River play and filmed what happened. River died in June 2021. Sources: [Wikipedia, Dogmeat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogmeat_(Fallout)); [Thumbsticks](https://www.thumbsticks.com/meet-the-real-life-dogmeat-from-fallout-4/) (snippet; ownership details conflict between sources).
- A Short Hike and Old Man's Journey: no dog character was found in either. Sources: [Start Menu review, A Short Hike](https://www.startmenu.co.uk/home/review-a-short-hike); [iMore, Old Man's Journey](https://www.imore.com/old-mans-journey) (snippet).

### Inferences
Craft lessons (inference):
- **Build the dog from real dogs.** Dug's best gag, Trico's ears and Dogmeat's motion all came from watching real animals. The author's own (or a friend's) dog habits will be more specific and less cheesy than invented ones.
- **Let the player make one small choice that makes the dog theirs**, as Firewatch does with breed and name, and say nothing about it. A name chosen by the five-year-old (or a colour) costs one tap and makes the later ring on the life lines view personal. The choice should not change the outcome.
- **Use the reveal of waiting, but reverse its cruelty.** Jurassic Bark's force is the discovery that the dog waited; its cruelty is that he waited alone, forever, and the viewer is made to watch. Here the waiting is shown as joy every day, the story's own theme ("who waits for you to get home"), and the end is an empty gate, not a lonely dog. The player is never shown the dog suffering or alone.
- **Keep the death to one short scene, and no funeral set piece.** The Marley and Me criticism lands on length and staging (extended death, letters at a funeral). One scene for the telling, one for the empty gate, and one later moment with the kept object is enough.
- **Never use the dog's death as a lever for something else** (a lesson, another character's arc, a plot turn). It is the dog's own ending, after a full life.
- **Spiritfarer's lesson: a goodbye can be a ritual the player performs, not a surprise done to them.** If the story offers interaction at the end, it should be small and voluntary (touching the sleeping old dog, sitting with him), never a "press to say goodbye" prompt.
- **Expect dread, and pay for it with joy.** Players brace when a dog appears in a birth-to-old-age story. The answer is not to hide the death but to make the dog's years funny and full enough that the player is glad they met him.

Proposed arc, scene by scene (inference, placeholders only; the author writes the real lines):
1. **Arrival (you about 5):** a puppy (or a stray, as Donut was) at the gate. One tap: name him. First gag (he steals your shoe, or falls asleep mid-play).
2. **The gate (you 6 to 10):** the after-school beat, repeated a few times with small variations: he is at the gate before the bus; full greeting; zoomies around the yard. This is the first "who waits for you to get home". Repetition is the point; each repeat is short.
3. **Mischief montage (you 8 to 12):** two or three running gags (shoe or sock theft, the bed he is not allowed on, the head tilt at one word). Each one introduced once and echoed later.
4. **Grey muzzle (you 13 to 15):** same scenes, lower energy: the zoomie becomes one slow lap; he meets you halfway; he naps on your shoe instead of stealing it.
5. **The day he does not come (you about 15 to 16):** you find him asleep, deaf to the gate; you wake him with a touch; he wags. You start checking on him after school.
6. **"We had to let him go" (you about 17):** ordinary morning, or the night-before version (see section 1). The empty gate on the way home. The parent's short, honest telling, including that he was held.
7. **After:** the collar on the hook. Life lines view: his short line ends in the first ring the player has seen.
8. **Years later (optional, once):** coming home as an adult, a glance at a gate or a hook. No flashback, no narration.
- **The life lines view does real work here.** The dog's line will likely be the first line to end, so his ring teaches the player what a ring means, gently, before any person's ring appears. His line's shortness against the human lines is itself the point about dog lifespans; no text needed.
- **Tie to the title without saying it.** The dog is pre-phone: he is the first one who needed to know you got home, and he knew it by being at the gate. When "text me when you get home" appears later in life, the player can feel the lineage without a line pointing at it.

## 4. Humour and liveliness: dog behaviours that read in a few lines

### Takeaway
The safest, most universal dog comedy is physical and true: zoomies (they really do happen at transitions such as someone coming home, and after baths), stealing socks and shoes (mostly attention and the owner's smell), the play bow, the head tilt (real, but strongest when a dog hears words it knows), and the whole-body wiggle greeting. They work in one line of text or a two-second animation, and each can age with the dog.

### Cited Findings
- Zoomies, or frenetic random activity periods (FRAPs): no single proven cause; vets link them to releasing pent-up energy and to transitions in the day, such as the owner getting home or a guest arriving, and to being made to stay still (a bath, a vet visit). Usually a few minutes; over ten is rare. A loose, wiggly, carefree zoomie is a happy one; pinned-back ears, very wide eyes or a tense body warrant a closer look. Sources: [Discover](https://www.discovermagazine.com/planet-earth/why-do-dogs-suddenly-get-the-zoomies); [WLOX, CNN/KSDK syndication](https://www.wlox.com/2024/06/14/heres-why-zoomies-cause-your-dog-burst-with-energy/); [Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.in/insider-picks/news/vets-explain-why-dogs-get-the-zoomies-and-how-to-keep-them-safe-when-theyre-charging-around-the-house/articleshow/97290783.cms) (all snippet).
- Head tilt: Sommese et al. 2021, Animal Cognition (Eotvos Lorand University): of 40 dogs, 7 "gifted word learners" that knew object names tilted their heads in about 43% of trials when the owner asked for a toy by name, versus about 2% for typical dogs; each dog kept tilting to the same side over time; the authors suggest the tilt may signal attention to meaningful words. Sources: [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2021-10-talk-to-tilt-tilting-dogs.html); [Discover](https://www.discovermagazine.com/planet-earth/why-do-dogs-tilt-their-heads-to-one-side) (snippet).
- Sock and shoe theft: pet-care sources agree on attention (even being scolded counts, and a chase turns it into a rewarding keep-away game), the strong smell of the owner as comfort when alone, boredom, and teething in puppies; swallowed socks can cause blockages needing surgery. Sources: [Kinship](https://www.kinship.com/dog-behavior/why-does-my-dog-steal-my-socks); [HuffPost UK](https://huffingtonpost.co.uk/entry/why-dogs-steal-shoes_uk_66c5b95ae4b0e284ae55506d); [Dogster](https://www.dogster.com/lifestyle/why-does-my-dog-steal-my-socks/) (snippet; pet sites, not peer-reviewed).
- Play bow (front legs down, rear up) is an invitation to play; a relaxed dog has a loose body, natural ears, open relaxed mouth and a wagging tail. Source: [RSPCA Australia](https://kb.rspca.org.au/knowledge-base/what-does-my-dogs-body-language-mean/) (snippet).
- Tail wagging: dogs wag far more than other canids; Leonetti, Cimarelli, Hersh and Ravignani 2024 (Biology Letters) propose it may be a by-product of selection for tameness, or that humans favoured it because people are drawn to rhythm; they note wagging has rarely been measured quantitatively. Sources: [PMC10792393](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10792393); [Science News](https://sciencenews.org/?p=3135083) (snippet).
- Dug's "squirrel" came from a real dog habit (sudden freeze to attention). Source: [Movie Mom](https://moviemom.com/glenn-close-interviews-pixars/) (snippet).

### Inferences
- **Pick three running gags, not ten,** and give each a young, old and after version:
  - Shoe theft: puppy steals your shoe and runs (keep-away); old dog sleeps on your shoe while you are at school (the smell of you, which is the true reason); after, the shoe is just a shoe.
  - Zoomies on your arrival: full laps of the yard when young; one slow lap when old; the yard still after.
  - Head tilt at one word (his name, or "walk"): stays to the end; it can be the last thing he does in the story, a small tilt from his bed.
- **The bed rule** (he is not allowed on your bed; he is on your bed every morning) is a classic two-line gag, but it assumes an indoor dog. A yard dog who waits at the gate fits the plan better and is common in many countries; choose one and keep it consistent.
- **Funny, not talking.** Dug translates the dog's mind into words; this story's quieter tone works better if the dog is never given lines or thought bubbles. The humour comes from what he does and from family members' reactions.
- **Universality:** zoomies, wagging, the play bow, stealing objects and the head tilt are dog behaviours found wherever dogs are, so they read across audiences. What varies between cultures is where the dog lives (indoors, on the bed, versus in the yard or on a chain) and whether dogs are welcome indoors at all. The gate works for both.

### Gaps
- No cross-cultural study of how audiences read dog behaviours was found; the universality point above is inference.
- No peer-reviewed source was read on sock or shoe stealing, or on dogs sleeping on beds.

## 5. Drawing and animating the dog in a flat, faceless-adjacent style

### Takeaway
Simple flat dogs stay expressive by moving the emotion off the face and into ears, tail, head angle and body height, exactly the signals real dogs use. Rounded shapes read friendly (Bluey's art direction is the clearest statement of this); Gromit proves a dog with no mouth carries everything with a brow and posture. For a faceless human cast, a nose dot and maybe no eyes is enough: ears are the dog's eyebrows, the tail is its voice. Animate the walk in the real lateral footfall order and let ears and tail follow through behind the body.

### Cited Findings
- Bluey (Ludo Studio): art director Catriona Drummond's shape language: circles are round, friendly and soft; squares sturdy and planted; horizontals calm, verticals upright, diagonals off-balance; rounded rectangles became a hallmark, and the character shape language was extended to the world. Source: [It's Nice That](https://www.itsnicethat.com/features/how-we-built-bluey-s-world-cartoon-background-scenery-art-director-catriona-drummond-animation-090725) (snippet). Being a cutout-animation show pushed the characters to be geometric and simple; Joe Brumm says he is not a character designer, and Mark Paterson refined the style. Source: [Laughing Place, Annecy 2023](https://www.laughingplace.com/w/articles/2023/06/15/annecy-2023-recap-the-story-behind-bluey-with-joe-brumm-richard-jeffery-and-mark-paterson/) (snippet). The 2D Heelers have no necks; the show hid this by cutting between a few fixed angles, which became a problem when moving to 3D. Source: [Laughing Place, Bluey movie](https://www.laughingplace.com/disney-entertainment/bluey-movie-cg-animation-d23/) (snippet).
- Gromit: Nick Park first designed him with a mouth to talk in A Grand Day Out; he could not reach or manipulate the mouth on set, lowered the eyebrow instead, and says that is when he found the character, more introverted and more expressive with the eyes; animator Merlin Crossingham says his body language and brows communicate more powerfully than speech would. Sources: [Bullseye transcript, Nick Park](https://maximumfun.org/transcripts/bullseye-with-jesse-thorn/transcript-bullseye-with-jesse-thorn-wallace-gromit-creator); [Wikipedia, Wallace and Gromit characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Wallace_and_Gromit_characters); [Kottke](https://kottke.org/16/05/how-gromit-from-wallace-and-gromit-is-built) (snippet).
- Reading a real dog (veterinary sources): a loose tail with the whole rear end wiggling reads happy; a stiff wag is a caution sign; a slow, sweeping wag suggests comfort, a quick, narrow wag arousal or conflict; tail low signals appeasement, fear or stress; tucked means intense anxiety. Ears forward means alert; held out to the side, worry or uncertainty; pinned back, appeasement. Ear carriage varies by breed, and no single signal is conclusive. Source: [dvm360, Canine body language basics](https://www.dvm360.com/view/canine-body-language-basics) (snippet). Fearful or stressed: lowered body, tail tucked, ears back, yawning, lip licking, head turned away. Source: [RSPCA Australia](https://kb.rspca.org.au/knowledge-base/what-does-my-dogs-body-language-mean/) (snippet).
- Wag direction: Quaranta, Siniscalchi and Vallortigara 2007 found wagging biased to the dog's right with positive stimuli (such as the owner) and to the left with negative ones; Siniscalchi et al. 2013 found dogs watching videos of left-biased wagging, including dog silhouettes, had higher heart rates and more anxious behaviour. Source: [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/blog/scicurious/wag-dog-when-left-vs-right-matters) (snippet).
- Walk gait: Muybridge wrote that dogs follow the same walking footfall sequence as the horse; the lateral sequence is hind left, fore left, hind right, fore right, keeping at least three feet on the ground for stability. Sources: [Royal Academy, Muybridge](https://www.royalacademy.org.uk/art-artists/work-of-art/goat-walking); [Live Science](https://livescience.com/5304-museum-animal-displays-dont-walk-walk.html) (snippet; one lecture deck gave a different dog order, treat with caution).
- Animation process: quadruped tutorials block the main poses first, then refine with overlapping action and offsets, treating the head, ears and tail as a separate overlap and polish pass; video reference of a real animal is stressed. Sources: [Animation Mentor, quadruped walk tutorial](https://www.animationmentor.com/?p=45178); [Skillshare, Opi Chaggar](https://www.skillshare.com/en/classes/animating-a-quadruped-walk/1863315195) (snippet).
- Small ear and nose behaviours (ear waggles, sniffing the wind) were singled out as what made Trico feel like a real pet. Source: [Empire](https://www.empireonline.com/gaming/reviews/last-guardian-review) (snippet).

### Inferences
Design rules for the dog's look (inference):
- **Shape:** a mid-size mixed-breed built from soft shapes: a bean or rounded-rectangle body, a round head with a short rounded muzzle, soft drop or half-drop ears (they can lift and flatten, which upright triangle ears do less clearly), a thick curved tail stroke. Mostly horizontal body line for calm; diagonals only in action (zoomies, the jump at the gate).
- **Face:** match the faceless people: a nose dot at the tip of the muzzle and no mouth. Eyes are an open choice: none keeps the cast consistent; a single small dot in profile would make the dog the only being who "looks at" you, which is a deliberate statement, not a default. Gromit shows the face can carry a lot with almost nothing, but here the ears do the brow's job.
- **Colour:** one coat colour that becomes his line colour in the life lines view; a lighter muzzle patch that grows with age is the single visual marker of time (the "grey muzzle"), paired with behaviour changes, never alone.
- **Few angles:** like Bluey, use a small set of fixed views (side, three-quarter, curled asleep) rather than a turnable 3D-like rig.

Animation rules (inference; the numbers are starting values to tune by eye, not from any source):

| State | Tail | Ears | Body | Starting values |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Content, idle | slow, wide sweeping wag | relaxed, natural | level, slight breathing | wag about 1.5 to 2 cycles per second, 25 to 35 degrees each side |
| Alert (bus coming) | tail up, still or slow | forward, lifted | head up, weight forward | ear lift over about 0.2 s, hold |
| Greeting at the gate | fast wag that drives the whole rear (wiggle) | pulled back and flat (joyful appeasement) | low front, rump wiggle, a jump, a full-body shake after | wag about 4 to 6 cycles per second, hips rotating with it |
| Play bow, zoomies | high, loose | flapping on each stride | front down, rear up; then fast arcs | zoomie a few laps, ears and tail lagging 2 to 4 frames |
| Worried | low, slow or still | out to the side or back | lowered, head turned away | slow everything, small amplitude |
| Old | low carriage, slow small wag | lower resting position | head lower, slower rise, longer pauses | wag about 1 cycle per second, half amplitude; sit and rise with a hitch |
| Asleep | still, curled around body | soft, flat | curled; breathing | body scale 1 to 2 percent, about one breath every 2 seconds |

- **Follow-through:** ears and tail always lag the body by a few frames and settle after it stops; this single rule does more to make a flat dog feel alive than any detail.
- **Walk:** hind left, fore left, hind right, fore right, with the hip and shoulder lifting in turn and a small head bob; for a phone, legs as simple rounded strokes driven by offset cycles are enough.
- **Wag bias as a hidden truth:** a slight right bias in happy wagging, left in worry, is a true detail nobody needs to notice.
- **Micro-life over detail:** an occasional ear flick, a sniff (nose dot nudging up twice), and a head tilt carry more life than fur texture, as Trico's ears and sniffing did.

- No authoritative source was found on the ears-back joyful greeting specifically (as opposed to ears back as appeasement in general); the greeting row combines the appeasement reading with the reunion findings in section 2.
