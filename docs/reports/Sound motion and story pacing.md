# Land each cue on its beat

"Text me when you get home" now has the shape the evidence asks for, and what still holds it back is mostly timing: the score's key cues land late, blurred or under the next line, and the bodies cannot yet show waiting or old age. Read on 2026-10-30, the code already carries about twenty of the changes in this round's notes, among them the reordered dawn act, a music box cue for the send to Mom, a held chord from "Read" to the reply, Mom's typing dots on the city night and the partner asleep upstairs at the end. The text fixes that went in landed as the notes wrote them; the sound and motion fixes landed half way. Computed from `score.js` and `script.js`, **the held chord starts on the next chord of whatever is playing, 1.5 to 10.5 seconds after "Read", while the kid's "home." arrives about 9.5 seconds after it**, so the hold usually begins around the typing dots and can miss the reply altogether. When "home." lands, the chord that was sounding rings on under the resolution, the kid's theme then runs about 25 seconds on a timer and closes over the reveal, and the music box phrase for Mom needs about 9 seconds while one quick tap brings the light switch click into its second bar. The research says why this matters: chills come when a voice enters and loudness rises, tears come with slow, calm music and last about twice as long, and a silence after an unfinished phrase feels tense while one after a closed phrase feels calm, so each effect depends on where the note falls against the story event. Sadness is the emotion people read best from a faceless walk and the one exaggeration does not help, so the rig needs a head that can drop and its own tempo for each state. The twist is now planted on the player's own screen, which makes the three lines that explain it at the end worth testing without. None of the studies measured music or motion inside a game like this one, so every open change comes with a playtest.

## The score's key cues land late, blurred or under the next line

The evidence separates two peaks. In Mori and Iwanaga's study, listeners who got chills showed rising skin conductance, listeners who cried breathed more slowly and rated their songs sad and calm, and **tears lasted 6.60 seconds on average against 3.47 for chills** ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5384201/)). Chills followed the entry of a new voice and changes in loudness, confirmed on retest ([Grewe et al.](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_222216288)), clustered at crescendos, solo entries and a widening frequency range together with rises in heart rate ([Guhn et al.](https://labs.sonicfield.org/library/physiological-and-musico-acoustic-correlates-of-the-chill-response)), and came with loudness rising mostly between 920 and 4,400 Hz ([Nagel et al.](https://sonicfield.org/library/psychoacoustical-correlates-of-musically-induced-chills)). In Sloboda's survey of 83 listeners, tears went most reliably with appoggiaturas, a leaning note that resolves a step down ([Scilit](https://scilit.com/publications/28754222606c17abaae30191e67bc59d)), though a critique notes the study cannot count the appoggiaturas that moved nobody ([New Music USA](https://newmusicusa.org/?p=275769)). A review of 167 reports names arousal, expectation and emotion as the routes to chills ([QMUL](https://qmro.qmul.ac.uk/xmlui/handle/123456789/75159)), and listeners heard the same silence as less tense after a closed phrase than after an open one ([Princeton](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/silences-in-music-are-musical-not-silent-an-exploratory-study-of-/)). The enjoyment of sad music runs through feeling moved, in 308 participants ([Frontiers](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00439/text)), and across 19 nations being moved followed a sudden rise in closeness ([ISCTE](https://ciencia.iscte-iul.pt/publications/kama-muta-conceptualizing-and-measuring-the-experience-of-being-moved-across-19-nations-and-15/47393?lang=en)). Inference: in this story closeness rises at status labels and arriving bubbles, so the musical event has to land on that story event to borrow its force.

The held chord is the clearest case. In `score.js` a cue for a new act waits in `pending` until the scheduler reaches a chord it has not yet queued, and `urgent` only removes the rule that the switch falls on an even chord. The scheduler queues 1.5 seconds ahead and the porch music changes chord every 9 seconds (the old-age act every 8), so the `wait` chord begins between 1.5 and 10.5 seconds after "Read", about 6 seconds on average. The steps from "Read" to "home." add up to about 9.5 seconds: 3.2 seconds of silence, 2.2 of dots, a 1.5 second stop, 1.8 more of dots and 0.8 before the bubble. Computed from the code, the hold usually starts around the first dots, and when the chord before it has only just begun, `home()` clears the pending act before it sounds at all. Under "Read" the porch music keeps cycling D, G, B minor and A, so the label that should feel most uncertain can sit on the home chord itself. The `wait` chord is also no suspension: `As` holds A, E and C sharp, a plain dominant, and it is struck again every 7 seconds with its filter opening and closing each time. The notes' design answers all three. Each act's chords run through their own gain, created in `play()`, so a cue can fade the old act over 0.3 to 2 seconds; urgent cues start on the next beat; and the wait is one chord held with no new attack, bass A under D, E, A and B so the theme's last note hangs unresolved, its filter breathing at 0.05 to 0.1 Hz and thinning to the high D over the bass at the first dots. Once the hold sounds from "Read" every time, the silence after "Read" can grow from 3.2 seconds toward 5 or 6, where an earlier round put it on the evidence that **negative feelings arrive sooner after a message shows Read than while it is unread** ([IRMA](https://irma-international.org/article/reply-speed-as-nonverbal-cue-in-text-messaging-with-a-read-receipt-display-function/239530/)). Today the silence after "Read" is only 0.6 seconds longer than the one after "Delivered". A longer wait also survives replay: readers still felt suspense about an outcome they already knew ([Stony Brook](https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/suspense-in-the-absence-of-uncertainty/)), and Dad's unanswered "Delivered" has already taught the player what a missing reply can mean.

The resolution then blurs. `home()` stops new chords, but the chord already sounding keeps its full length and a 4.2 second release, and a chord queued inside the 1.5 second lookahead still starts, so the A chord can sound for up to about 11 seconds under the D that answers it. The bubble also lands first: a phone step runs its `music` after the step, so the generic `pop()` plays with the bubble and the theme follows 0.3 seconds later. The notes' fix is to fade the old act's gain in about 0.3 seconds when `home()` fires, to drop the pop for this one message so the theme's first piano note is the arrival sound, and to schedule it from the bubble's moment with an offset of 0.1 seconds at most. That figure is an engineering guess; no source on how tightly a musical resolution must meet a visual event was found.

The two closing cues run on timers of their own. The kid's theme plays four bars of 5 seconds, so its closing D lands about 15 seconds after the reply, a last D chord holds to about 28 seconds, and a timer fades it out by about 31. The kid's "love you", the player's reply, the kid's answer and the chat's close take about 5 to 12 seconds on a typical read, so on most plays the closing bar falls on the hinge line or the fade into Mom's kitchen, and the tail sounds under Mom's typing. The notes ask for the reverse order: the cadence finishes, a calm silence follows, and the reveal plays with no music and only the kitchen's own sound. The music box has the opposite problem. `remember()` plays bars of 2.48, 2.84, 3.20 and 3.56 seconds, so its last D sounds about 9 seconds after the send, while the chat waits 2.8 seconds and then for any tap, and the next beat plays the light switch click at once. A player who taps right away hears the switch in bar two and the dark porch in bar three, which cuts the one cue built for tears, the response that needs the most time. Inference: the fix is the same in both places. The score reports when its last note ends, and the next line waits for it.

Smaller cue problems cluster in Noon and after Dad. Between the old dog and the friend's drift the cues run evening, noon, frame, noon, grief, noon, grief and noon within 26 beats; ordinary changes wait up to two chords, so several never sound, and each grief cue restarts the grief chords from the top. A cue for the act already playing also restarts it, which happens with grief at Grandma and with the afternoon act after Dad. On porch returns `frame` is not urgent, so on a short return the present's music can still be waiting when the next chapter's cue replaces it, and only the clock tick marks the present in sound every time. After Dad's cut the warm afternoon act comes back at age 58.3, two captions later, under "Your thumb still does it after every visit." Firewatch's composer describes a constant instinct to tamp the sound down ([Designing Sound](https://designingsound.org/2017/07/19/firewatch-solitude-and-companionship-through-minimalism-an-interview-with-chris-remo/)), Gustavo Santaolalla treats silence as part of the score of The Last of Us ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/latino/last-us-composer-gustavo-santaolalla-silence-key-drawing-audience-rcna102364)), and in 30 viewers of Joker the scenes without music disrupted alignment with the protagonist ([CiteDrive](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/film-music-silence-and-moral-alignment-a-joker-case-study-in-northern-cyprus/)). Inference: one grief span from the last walk through the empty gate, a `play()` that ignores the act already playing, an urgent `frame` with a short crossfade, and rain with no music from Dad's "Delivered" until Mom's door.

## Two closings of one theme need two kinds of sound

The design follows the strongest precedents. Up's married-life cue began as a request for a tune like a grandmother's music box, and as Ellie dies the instruments drop out until a piano is left, the music ending with the fade to black ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Up_opening_sequence)). Journey's theme was written on the first day and underlies nearly every note in the game ([Destructoid](https://destructoid.com/?p=126775)). Florence gives Florence the piano and Krish the cello, which joins as the romance begins ([WSHU](https://www.wshu.org/culture/2018-06-21/kevin-penkins-intimate-conversational-soundtrack-for-florence)). In 772 listeners, nostalgia was the emotion sad music evoked most often, with memory the main route ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4203803)), and songs tied to a listener's own life and familiar to them evoked more nostalgia ([Southampton](https://www.southampton.ac.uk/%7Ecrsi/Barrett,%20grimm,%20Robins,%20Wildschut,%20Sedikides,%20&%20Janata,%202010,%20Emotion.pdf)). Inference: one "home" theme, a music box in childhood and a piano later, is the right tool, and the open work is in placing it, where it goes quiet and where it closes.

Read from the chord orders, the theme stays open through the life: bar four lands on A in Morning and Afternoon and on B minor in Noon and now in Dawn, so **the first full close on D is the kid's "home."**, and the music box closes it a second time for Mom. The comment at the top of `score.js` says the box finishes the theme "for the first time", so the comment and the code describe two different endings. The notes argue for two closes by two routes: the kid's reply as a chill (piano alone for bar one, strings entering at bar two or three, the pad's filter opening toward 2 to 3 kHz on top of the swell) and the send to Mom as tears (the box alone, slow and quiet, with an appoggiatura on the last bar). The case for one close is real too. In film scenes viewers chose themselves, tears and goosebumps arriving together marked the top of a graded scale of peak moments ([Frontiers](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00041/text)), and an earlier round asked that the peak not be split. The alternative stops the kid's theme at bar three over the held A and closes the phrase only at Mom. The two versions differ by a few lines in `home()`, and a playtest that asks where players got goosebumps and where their eyes went wet would choose between them.

Two things spend the chill before the reply. The dawn act already plays strings at half level, so by "home." the strings are a voice the player heard in the partner's chapter, and the most reliable chill trigger, a voice entering, has been used. The finale is also barely louder than daily life: the acts run their feeds at 0.85 with pad levels of 0.5 to 0.75, while `home()` raises the feeds to 0.95 with a pad of 0.75, a few decibels at most. The new limiter leaves swells alone, and there is little swell for it to leave. Inference: keep strings for the finale and give Dawn another colour, bring them in at bar two or three with a slow attack, and set everyday acts 6 to 10 dB under the finale's peak. Sony's guidance for handheld consoles put integrated loudness near -18 LUFS, a 2012 to 2018 figure and the closest one found for phones ([ITU-R](https://www.itu.int/dms_pub/itu-r/opb/rep/R-REP-BS.2434-2018-PDF-E.pdf)); measuring one full playthrough against it would also show whether the quietest scenes still carry.

Mom's bar needs its leaning note. `remember()` slows by bar, from 0.62 to 0.89 seconds a beat, and its E falls to the final D as a passing note at the end of bar three. Starting bar four on E over the D chord and letting it fall after one or two beats turns that step into an appoggiatura, and slowing each note by 2 to 4 percent, with a click of mechanism 1 to 2 ms long before each, makes the box sound like it is running down.

Inference, building on the notes: the player's thumb can play the opening. "home" is typed by hand eight times on one path (the city night, the partner, Dad four times counting the last and the one after him, Mom, and Mom at the end), and each tap types two letters, so each "home" takes two taps. If those taps sounded the theme's first two notes, D and E, the motif would be learned through the hand. A meta-analysis of 145 studies found **performed actions remembered far better than watched ones (g = 1.23)** ([Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/memory-attention-cognition-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/enact22.pdf)), and music let viewers recognise scenes they could not recall ([Haverford](https://scholarship.haverford.edu/psychology_facpubs/260)). On the city night the two notes are typed and erased. At Dad's last "home" they rise and the cut on "Delivered" leaves them hanging, a tense silence by Margulis's result. At Mom the player plays D and E and the music box picks up at F sharp and finishes the phrase. This would replace the notes' plan to sync the lonely act's first bar to the typing. No study tests it, and the risk is that players hear the notes as button sounds; a question about the tune after play would show which.

The small sounds have three gaps. The cold open and the reveal both use the porch's night ambience of crickets, so Mom's kitchen sounds like the present. An identical bed in both, a lower and slower wall clock for the clock the reveal draws at 1:06 and a fridge hum at 100 or 120 Hz with harmonics up to 400 Hz, would let the reveal sound familiar before the text says why, the recognition effect above. The kettle is a 7 second rising hiss on "You put the kettle on"; the notes want it heating under "Delivered" and "Read" and clicking off by itself, never whistling. The light switch is a click and a short tone, and the room does not change, since the dark porch keeps the same ambience; a hum that stops at the switch would be the room going to sleep. The room and car beds are low-passed at 110 and 160 Hz and the buzz sits at 75 and 150 Hz, which by inference (no measurement was found) a phone speaker mostly drops, so a quiet layer at 250 to 700 Hz and a 2 to 4 kHz table rattle under the buzz would carry. The typing dots are silent, as in life, and should stay so. Vibration cannot carry meaning on iPhone, where WebKit has never shipped the Vibration API ([W3C](https://w3c.github.io/vibration/reports/implementation.html)).

## Sadness reads at 94 percent from a walk, so the rig needs a head and a tempo

Bodies without faces carry sadness better than any other emotion. Observers named sadness from walkers whose heads were hidden **94 percent of the time, against 74 for happiness and 56 for pride**, and sad gaits had less arm swing ([Xu et al. review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.11461)). Some mannequin postures for sadness drew over 90 percent agreement ([UEA](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/73537/)). Sad or depressed walkers moved slower, with less arm swing, less up and down head movement, more side to side sway of the upper body and a slumped posture ([Michalak et al.](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/900434655)); sad gaits lowered the head and shrank joint amplitudes ([Xu et al. review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.11461)), and neck and chest flexion in sadness held at any walking speed ([Gross et al.](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_921144378)). Exaggerating a movement made every emotion easier to recognise except sadness ([Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/p5096)). Speed carries arousal, while pleasantness sits in the timing between body segments, since it vanished when the segments were phase-scrambled ([Glasgow](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/25001)). Classic animation teaches the same through timing alone: one head turn reads as a twitch, a dodge, an inviting glance or a thought depending only on the number of drawings between its poses ([SIGGRAPH HyperGraph](https://education.siggraph.org/static/HyperGraph/animation/character_animation/principles/timing.htm)). No lab has measured waiting or longing as body categories, so those have to be built from orientation toward something absent, repetition and tempo.

The rig has the start of this. `mood: 'sad'` slows breathing to 0.7 of its rate, leans the trunk 0.09 radians (about 5 degrees) and makes the standing sway slower and wider, at the dog's last walk, the empty gate and Grandma's snow door. Breathing rates differ per person, and the dog lies at the gate with an almost still tail until the child comes round the corner, then wags. Four gaps remain. The head is drawn in the torso's frame, so sadness can only tip the whole body; a head pitch about the neck, 8 to 15 degrees down for grief, is the cue every gait study above names. The sad walk is unchanged where it matters: sway switches off while moving and bob and arm swing keep their full size, so on the last walk, the one sad scene that walks, the figure bounces like a happy one. The notes' preset is bob and arm swing at 0.4, stride at 0.7, and the sway kept on. Mom in the reveal is drawn close, filling the kitchen window, and hers is the body the twist rests on, yet she stands at the calm default. An anxious preset, with a breath every 2.8 to 3 seconds, weight shifts every 6 to 7, the head down to the phone and, at random intervals, a turn to the window over 0.7 to 0.9 seconds (the thinking band of the timing table), would show her waiting without a word. Age has no tempo either: the wave is the same 1.26 second cycle for a child, a father, Grandma at her door and Mom at hers, while the notes give an old wave 1.8 to 2.2 seconds, a smaller arc from the forearm, and the raised hand held 1.5 to 2 seconds at the end. Grandma's ghost fades on a straight line while waving at full speed; letting the wave slow as she fades, with the raised hand the last thing to go, is craft inference. Seated people still all breathe at one rate.

The camera is mostly right. Pushes ease in and out symmetrically, run 14 seconds to a scale of 1.03 to 1.05, sit on 24 of the script's 65 scene beats, and leave the grief scenes alone, which matches the notes' advice to hold still on the hardest beats. The parallax drift still runs on every scene, so even the empty gate slides by up to 22 pixels. Inference, with no source on grief and a still frame: stopping the drift on the last walk, the empty gate, the snow door and Mom's dark door would make the stillness a choice. A target that starts to move is found faster than one already moving ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03193623.pdf)), so in the still kitchen of the reveal the phone's light is the onset the eye should get, and a slow push toward Mom's phone during her typing would bring the camera closest as "are you home?" appears.

## The twist is on screen now, so the ending can stop explaining it

Counted on one path through the current script (the teen "ok", goose "kind of", three picks, Grandma's weekend, the friend written to, the last "ok."), the story has **232 beats, 172 caption taps and 1,444 caption words plus 151 in chats**, with six meaningful choices at beats 73, 114, 125, 128, 169 and 217, porch returns at 49, 111, 159 and 210, and deaths at 65, 89, 146, 199 and 207. By the notes' method that is still roughly 16 to 22 minutes for a reader who does not linger. Community heuristics ask for a choice every 100 to 400 words ([Choice of Games forum](https://forum.choiceofgames.com/t/how-long-is-too-long-to-go-without-a-choice/58610)); at about eight words a caption the reading load is light, and the slow part is the first meaningful choice, which waits until beat 73. Memory of an episode follows its peak and its end, with length barely counting ([Fredrickson and Kahneman](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/fredr-kahneman-jpsp1993.pdf)), and a life that ended at a high point was rated better than the same life with mildly pleasant years added ([Scilit](https://scilit.com/publications/a9d0541822a5d3517ea84a7c627ec097)). That is why the timing of the last two minutes, above, matters more than any cut for length. The life's two falls and two rises sit close to the "two sequential man in a hole" arc, one of the most downloaded in a study of Project Gutenberg fiction ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.07772v1.pdf)).

The twist is now planted fairly. The cold open stamps Thursday 01:06 against the porch's Sunday; Mom's "I couldn't sleep" is followed by "She says that a lot. You believe her for years."; the city night stamps the same minute and shows her typing dots after the erased "home"; the narrator says "Nobody is lying awake about it."; and the cold open's window comes back when the kid at sixteen stays out. Vera Tobin places the pleasure of a twist mainly in its setup and in the feeling that the evidence was there all along ([Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/544879/why-our-brains-love-plot-twists)). Readers enjoyed ironic-twist stories more when spoiled in advance ([Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/gb/blog/the-psychology-of-fiction/201110/spoilers-dont-spoil-stories)), a result later studies contradict ([CORE](https://core.ac.uk/download/72840983.pdf)). The ending still explains itself in words: the hinge line and the "years ago" label say the same thing, then one line names the night, one says she typed and deleted, and one points to the weather text, while the phone already shows the chat header, the bonfire night, "here" on September 1, the stamp and her erased question. The comment over the reveal says every step is said plainly so nobody is lost, and the concern is fair. Transportation is attention converging on the story ([Green and Brock](https://gwern.net/docs/www/pdfs.semanticscholar.org/39691cee32e52fbfc33f1a0316aa0d008cb699a4.pdf)), and confusion at the peak would cost more than anywhere else. Inference: with the dots on the city night, three lines repeat what is on screen, and the earlier finding that naming a feeling damps it plausibly extends to naming a reveal. Keep one of the hinge line and the label, keep "She was lying awake about it.", which inverts the city night's line, and test the other three in an A/B.

Second person suits the form. Sentences with "you" put readers in the actor's view and made them feel more present in the scene ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/02/090218135124.htm)), a review found consistent embodiment only in second-person present narration ([Diegesis](https://www.diegesis.uni-wuppertal.de/index.php/diegesis/article/download/214/305)), and Robert Kurvitz finds second person gimmicky in prose and natural when a game speaks to its player ([PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/australia/edge/20191107/282961041930482)). Its cost is complicity, since whatever "you" does, the player did, and the script handles that well: the small, common neglects are imposed and the loaded acts are choices. What still moves the player from actor to reader is the thesis line, and five remain: "Some people are there for one chapter. It still counts.", "The door was open the whole time.", "Most lines cross yours once and keep going." with the line after it, "So you get to choose a bit." with the line before it, and "Either way you'll miss something. You get to pick what." Ken Wong's practice of leaving things unsaid for the audience to fill ([GameDaily](https://gamedaily.biz/?p=2307)) argues each scene can carry its meaning alone. Which lines go is the author's call.

The rest is local. The strangers aside still sits between Dad's death and Mom's, nine captions and two chats apart on screen, and in 241 bereaved adults a humour-focused eulogy story raised **enjoyment from 2.82 to 4.35 with appreciation unchanged** ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2022.973239/pdf)), which argues for a light beat with Mom in that slot in place of a statement about strangers. The pick of three and the weekend choice come three beats apart, each introduced by a line telling the player they choose, and the weekend's setup packs two plans into one sentence; two chats for the two plans, the pick moved to the end of Evening and a hold on Grandpa's apple curl in Morning would spread the choices and bring the first one forward. The six people kept close stay labels, and an emotional bond with a character holds less well when the character is not defined ([Choice of Games](https://www.choiceofgames.com/2023/07/author-interview-emily-short-elite-status-platinum-concierge/)), so one concrete text from a picked person in midlife would give their later lines someone to land on. Time shifts drop earlier information from working memory ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2667942)), and Grandpa is still "him" at age 10 and "He" at 11.8, right after lines about the dog. Dad still moves across the life lines at 6.1 with no caption, which can read as a separation. "Neither of you jumps" appears twice word for word. Eight lines use "smells", the last on the city night; an earlier round asked for a smell in every childhood scene, so the open question is whether the stop at the city is meant, which one line noticing it would settle. ## Conclusion

Inference, from the code and the evidence together: the story now runs on two clocks. Its own clock is the status labels, the bubbles and the player's taps, and every beat that matters in the last minutes falls on one of them; the score runs on a chord clock of 5 to 9 seconds that knows nothing about them. The effects at stake depend on where the sound falls against the event: a chill needs the voice to enter on the bubble, and a tear needs the seconds after it. So the largest gain left is to put the score on the story's clock, with cues fired by the labels and the taps, and closes that report their end so the next line can wait for them. The rig has the same gap, since it has the poses and lacks the tempos, and the ending has the reverse one, since the evidence is now on screen and the words have not stepped back.

What stays unknown is specific. No study measured tears or chills to music inside an interactive story, none gave a tolerance for how closely a musical resolution must meet a visual event, and none counted how many hearings a motif needs before people recognise it, so the timings above are craft and engineering guesses. The question of one close or two is the cheapest to settle: five to ten players, one version each, and two questions after play about goosebumps and wet eyes would say more about this ending than the literature can.
