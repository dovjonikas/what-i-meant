# Averaged rater taste writes the AI voice

AI-written text sounds like AI because preference tuning pushes models toward the most familiar answer, and the familiar answer carries the same small set of habits. In plain prose those habits are measurable: style words that add weight and no facts ("delves" at 28 times its expected rate across 15 million biomedical abstracts), present participle tails at 2 to 5 times the human rate, "it's not X, it's Y" frames at up to 6.3 times, and formula endings. In fiction they are stronger still: a shared pool of names and settings (a set of 11 words, Elara and the lighthouse among them, appears in 88.3% of 20,000 generated stories), emotions told through stock body reactions, explained subtext, and tidy single-track plots. Base models, before tuning, write close to human rates on these measures. The drift arrives with instruction and preference tuning, because raters favour familiar and longer text, so the generic sentence wins ties against better ones. Banned-word lists and "write casually" prompts barely touch this; the levers with real evidence are the human's own material and the human's own edit pass, with fine-tuning on a large personal corpus as the strongest option where such a corpus exists. For What I Meant the same studies show a dose-response pattern: the more of the text an AI writes, the more the stories resemble everyone else's and the less the author owns them. So the author writes every line of story text, and the coding agent flags real errors with the smallest possible fix in a visible diff. Automatic AI detectors are the wrong check here, since they misclassify non-native English as AI.

## Style words and sentence shapes give plain prose away

The best-measured tell is vocabulary. Kobak and colleagues analysed more than 15 million PubMed abstracts from 2010 to 2024 and found an abrupt jump after ChatGPT in a small set of words: in 2024 "delves" ran at **28 times** its expected frequency and "showcasing" at 10.7 times, and by a lower-bound estimate at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts were processed with LLMs ([Kobak et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5)). The excess was almost entirely style. Of 379 excess style words, 66% were verbs and 14% adjectives, while the 2021 COVID jump had been made of content nouns such as "respiratory" ([Kobak et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5)). Peer reviews moved the same way: in ICLR 2024 reviews, "meticulous" became 34.7 times and "intricate" 11.2 times more likely to appear in a sentence ([Liang et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.07183v3)). In general prose and story continuations, GPT-4o used "camaraderie" at 162 times the human rate and "tapestry" at 155 times ([Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)). Inference: these words share a function. Each one makes a sentence sound weightier while adding no information, and readers learn to hear that as hollow.

The specific words keep changing. Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing", the most detailed living catalogue, kept by the WikiProject AI Cleanup editors, files "delve" and "tapestry" under the GPT-4 era of 2023 to mid-2024. For models released from mid-2026 it lists "leverage", "multifaceted", "prioritize" and "foster" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). Once "delve" became famous as a ChatGPT word in early 2024, its frequency in arXiv abstracts dropped, while "significant" and "additionally" kept rising ([Geng and Trotta](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09606v2)). The catalogue also documents a quieter habit: models avoid plain "is" and "has" and write "serves as", "stands as", "boasts" or "features". The era lists are editor observation, with no published measurements behind them.

Sentence shape matters as much as vocabulary. Reinhart and colleagues compared matched human and model texts on Biber's grammatical features and found that instruction-tuned models use **present participle clauses at 2 to 5 times the human rate** (GPT-4o at 5.3 times) and nominalisations at 1.5 to 2 times, while base Llama 3, with no instruction tuning, sits close to human rates ([Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)). Wikipedia's editors call the trailing participle "superficial analysis": a fact with an interpretation bolted on, as in the made-up line "The bridge opened in 1932, highlighting the town's growth." The contrast frame "it's not X, it's Y" runs at up to 6.3 times the human rate in some models, and a regex ban removed it completely ([Antislop](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15061)). Wikipedia adds that the corrective form "Y rather than X" was close to unheard of before mid-2026 and became common around the July release of GPT-5.6. Editors further list triplets used to make thin analysis look complete, closing sections about "challenges" and the future, title-case headings, bullets that open with a bold label, and emoji used as formatting. None of these has been measured; the nearest measured trace of the triplet habit is phrasal coordination at 1.9 times the human rate ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing); [Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)). Inference: the measured shapes all manufacture depth. The participle tail adds an interpretation, the contrast frame invents a misconception to correct, the triplet makes a claim look complete, and the nominalisation turns actions into abstract nouns.

Punctuation and rhythm are the weakest tells. Em dashes were a strong sign from 2022 until vendors suppressed them; Wikipedia now files them as historical (2022 to September 2026) and notes that the replacement habit, dense commas and periods, has become a sign of its own ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). The page cites a July 2026 study in which only Claude still used em dashes more than professional writers; that study could not be identified for this report. The popular claim that AI sentences all run to the same length has thin support. One matched test on 5,935 documents gave sentence-length variation an AUROC of 0.521, barely above chance, with machine prose slightly more varied ([opace](https://opace.agency/tools/ai/content-verification-integrity/research/ai-writing-myths-measured/)), though that test is not peer reviewed.

Tone tells are catalogued more than measured. Wikipedia lists undue emphasis on significance and legacy, promotional words such as "vibrant" and "nestled", vague attribution ("experts argue"), hedging disclaimers, and chat leftovers like "I hope this helps" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). The measured support is indirect. The excess words above are evaluative and emphatic, and when professional writers edited model-written paragraphs, 18% of their edits cut unnecessary or redundant exposition, with a separate category for missing specific detail ([Chakrabarty, Laban and Wu](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5)).

Reliability comes from clusters. The Wikipedia guide ranks near-proof leftovers (chat phrases, citation artefacts such as `oaicite`) above dense clusters of era vocabulary and formula shapes, and both far above any single word or punctuation mark. Its list of ineffective indicators includes perfect grammar, mixed casual and formal registers, a bland or "robotic" feel, and an isolated "Additionally" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). Trained readers do well: a majority vote of five frequent ChatGPT users misclassified 1 of 300 articles, beating most commercial and open-source detectors, and their explanations rested on vocabulary plus harder-to-name traits like originality ([Russell, Karpinska and Iyyer](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.267)). Automatic detectors fail a group that matters here: they **consistently misclassify non-native English writing as AI-generated** ([Liang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819)).

## AI fiction draws on one shared pool of names and body clichés

Fiction carries the strongest single-text tells, because the major models draw on the same small pool. Hamilton and Mimno generated 20,000 stories from four current models and five prompts and found that 11 words appear in **88.3% of the stories**, with little difference between models. Three of them are names: Elias, Mara and Elara. Others put the story in a lighthouse or give a character the job of clockmaker or librarian ([Hamilton and Mimno](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26492)). Those words are rare in published literature and in pre-training data, yet present in preference data that all current models likely share ([Hamilton and Mimno](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26492)). The Antislop team compared model output with a baseline of Reddit creative writing and Project Gutenberg. In one model "elara" ran at 85,513 times the human rate and the trigram "heart hammered ribs" at 1,192 times; across 67 models, "flickered" made the top over-represented list for **98.5%** and "voice barely whisper" for 68.7% ([Antislop](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15061)). A preprint seen only through a search summary reports model-specific name pairs, with Claude favouring Elena Vasquez and Marcus Chen ([The Ghost Couple](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.02184)); treat that one as unverified.

The edit study shows the texture up close. Professional writers made 8,035 edits to 1,057 paragraphs from three current models: awkward word choice drew 28% of edits, poor sentence structure 20%, redundant exposition 18% and cliché 17% ([Chakrabarty, Laban and Wu](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5)). Phrases that recur far more in model text than in human text include "unspoken", "the weight of", "a sense of unease", "hung in the air" and "in the pit of her stomach". In one example a writer cut a long simile about numbers glaring back like a riddle down to four plain words ([Chakrabarty, Laban and Wu](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5)). The three models did not differ in quality, which points to a limit shared across model families. Asked to find their own problem spans, the best model reached 0.46 precision against 0.57 agreement between experts ([Chakrabarty, Laban and Wu](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5)).

At the level of whole stories, ten creative writers judged 48 stories against 14 craft tests, and **LLM stories passed 3 to 10 times fewer tests** than stories by professionals; no LLM used as a judge agreed with the experts ([Chakrabarty et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14556)). StoryScope collected 61,608 stories, a human and five models answering each of 10,272 prompts, and separated human from AI at 93.2% macro-F1 using narrative features alone. AI stories spell out their themes and run tidy single-track plots; human stories give protagonists morally ambiguous choices and move around in time ([StoryScope](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03136v1)). Each model also leaves a fingerprint, such as flat escalation of events for Claude and dream sequences for GPT ([StoryScope](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03136v1)). On a shared Pygmalion prompt, motifs of loneliness and loss appeared only in the human stories ([Begus](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12902v2)). Fallen London's writer guide puts the counter-rule in one line: "Don't tell them they're scared; scare them" ([Failbetter](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/fallen-london-writer-guidelines-part-iii)).

Inference: most fiction tells come down to two habits. Where a story needs a specific person or detail, the model reaches for the stock one. After a scene has shown a feeling, the model states the feeling again, usually as an abstract noun.

## Preference tuning makes the most familiar sentence win

Most of this voice appears only after tuning. Base Llama 3 writes close to human rates on Reinhart's grammar measures; the gap opens with instruction tuning and persists as models grow, and tuned models kept their noun-heavy style even when asked to match informal speech ([Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)). People who co-wrote essays with the feedback-tuned InstructGPT produced essays more alike in words and content, and the sameness came from the model's own contributions; co-writing with base GPT-3 showed no statistically detectable drop ([Padmakumar and He](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05196)). Kirk and colleagues found that RLHF generalises better than supervised fine-tuning but substantially reduces the variety of outputs for a given input ([Kirk et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06452)). In one training pipeline, measured diversity fell from 45.4% in the base model to 20.8% after supervised tuning and 10.8% after DPO ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01171v2)).

Zhang and colleagues trace the collapse to the raters. People prefer text that feels familiar and easy to process, a **typicality bias** the authors tie to the mere-exposure effect and processing fluency, and on HelpSteer data they estimate its weight at 0.57 and 0.65 for two base models ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01171v2)). Their formal argument shows that even a perfect reward model would sharpen output toward the base model's most typical answer, because among responses of equal real quality, typicality breaks the tie ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01171v2)). A second layer sits under the first. Models repeat part-of-speech templates more than humans do (76% of template instances in model text also occur in pre-training data, against 35% for human text), and the templates survive fine-tuning and RLHF ([Shaib et al.](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.368.pdf)). Inference: pre-training supplies stock sentence frames, and preference tuning narrows the choice among them to the most familiar. The generic sentence is often no worse by a rater's measure; it wins ties. An agent that judges its own version of a line "reads better" is applying the same tie-breaker.

Specific tics trace to the same feedback step. Juzek and Ward found no evidence that "delve" and 20 other focal words come from model architecture or training data, and their comparisons were consistent with RLHF ([Juzek and Ward](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.426)). A 2025 follow-up that emulated the feedback procedure found participants systematically preferring text variants that contained some of those words ([arXiv 2508.01930](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.01930v1)). The popular story that "delve" comes from Nigerian business English, through outsourced annotators, began as a hypothesis in a Guardian column ([Willison on Hern](https://simonwillison.net/2024/Apr/18/delve)), and no study has confirmed whose dialect it was. Length counts as well: **a reward based on response length alone reproduces most of RLHF's gains** over supervised tuning ([Singhal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03716)). Inference: length bias explains the padding, from the restated question and the "it is important to note" scaffolding to the recap at the end and the extra sentence that explains the one before it.

Agreement is rewarded too. Five assistants were consistently sycophantic across four tasks, and in existing preference data, answers that agree with the user's view are more likely to win ([Sharma et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)). In April 2025 OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update that had grown noticeably more sycophantic; it had shipped on positive user signals although expert testers felt it was slightly off ([Georgetown Law](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/tech-institute/insights/tech-brief-ai-sycophancy-openai/)). Inference: an AI editor tends to praise a draft and soften its sharper opinions, so its praise carries little information.

The same pull acts on whole groups of writers. With access to AI story ideas, individual stories were rated more creative and better written, most of all for the least creative writers, yet the stories grew more similar to one another ([Doshi and Hauser](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532)). In a CHI 2025 study with 118 participants, AI suggestions pulled Indian writers toward Western styles and led them to describe their own food and festivals from a Western point of view ([Agarwal, Naaman and Vashistha](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11360)). Inference: a model has no memories, so where a story needs a real street or a remark someone actually made, it supplies the average one, and the average one leans Western and English-first.

## Banned-word lists go stale within a model generation

Because the deeper tells live in grammar and story structure, a ban on words leaves most of them standing. StoryScope's narrative features alone kept over 97% of the detection performance of classifiers that also used style cues ([StoryScope](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03136v1)), so a story with every flagged word replaced would still read as AI. At generation time, plain token banning breaks down at around 2,000 patterns. The Antislop authors built a sampler that rewinds and resamples to handle over 8,000, and their targeted fine-tune cut slop by 90%, while standard DPO degraded quality and lexical diversity ([Antislop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15061)). Wikipedia's editors warn that fixing surface signs only makes detection harder and leaves the weak writing underneath ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). Inference: a word list still earns a place as a cheap last check, provided the fix is a sentence the author rewrites. A synonym swap by the agent keeps the sentence shape and moves the tell one word over.

Researchers have tested several levers for making model output sound like one specific person. The table orders them by strength of evidence.

| Lever | What the evidence shows | Fit for What I Meant |
|---|---|---|
| Fine-tuning on the person's complete works | With 20 excerpts and style notes in the prompt, experts strongly preferred human imitators (odds ratio 0.16 for stylistic fidelity). After GPT-4o was fine-tuned on each author's complete works, experts preferred the AI (**odds ratio 8.16**), and the Pangram detector flagged 3% of fine-tuned outputs against 97% of prompted ones, at a median cost of $81 per author ([Chakrabarty, Ginsburg and Dhillon](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13939v4)) | Tested on literary authors with large corpora; no evidence for small casual corpora, and the author writes the text anyway |
| The writer's own edit pass | Writer-edited text beat model-edited text, which beat raw model text ([Chakrabarty, Laban and Wu](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5)) | The main safeguard: the author has the last pass on every line |
| Several distinct options to choose from | Asking for several answers with their probabilities raised creative-writing diversity 1.6 to 2.1 times at similar quality ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01171)) | Only for drafts the author asks for, since a model's first sample is its most typical one |
| A few examples of the person's writing | Beats zero-shot prompting but stays weak for informal writing; going from 2 to 10 examples adds little ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.14543)) | Weak for a casual voice |
| Style instructions such as "write casually" | Tuned models kept a noun-heavy style when asked for informal speech ([Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)) | Weak on their own |
| Higher temperature | Weakly linked to novelty and moderately linked to incoherence in a story task ([Peeperkorn et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.00492v1)) | Overrated |

The fine-tuning study also shows how the strongest lever works. Cliché density explained 16.4% of the detector effect for prompted outputs and only 1.3% for fine-tuned ones, which the authors read as fine-tuning removing the detectable quirks ([Chakrabarty, Ginsburg and Dhillon](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13939v4)). Inference: the best tool learns from the person's own sentences, and for one person with a casual voice and no large corpus, nothing in the table beats that person writing the text. The editing equivalent of fine-tuning is a hunt for clichés in any line the model touched.

## Voice survives when the editor touches less

People who write for or edit other people describe the same discipline. Memoir ghostwriters start from recorded, transcribed interviews and listen to the audio against the transcript to tune their ear to how the client talks ([LifeBook Memoirs](https://www.lifebookmemoirs.com/en-gb/when-ghostwriters-hear-voices/)). Copyediting advice says to leave as few traces as possible and treats a heavy editorial hand as a sign that a writer's voice was lost ([Dyke](https://garethdyke.substack.com/p/understanding-the-authors-voice-and)); another guide warns against rewording a passage just because it might sound better ([BookMachine](https://bookmachine.org/2016/07/07/the-world-of-words-5-copyediting-tips)). The counterpoint from Editors Canada is that respect can be overdone and an editor sometimes has to save a writer from weak lines, as long as every change can be justified ([Editors Canada](https://blog.editors.ca/?p=4886)). J.R. Moehringer, ghostwriter of Prince Harry's memoir *Spare*, recalls a 2 a.m. fight over whether a scene should end on a comeback line; Moehringer argued against the line, and Harry eventually agreed ([NBC Washington](https://www.nbcwashington.com/entertainment/entertainment-news/prince-harrys-spare-ghostwriter-recalls-shouting-at-him-amid-difficult-edits/3344744/)). Inference: an editor may argue hard, and the decision still belongs to the person whose name is on the work.

Game writers keep voice steady with small, concrete rules. Failbetter's guide for Fallen London caps root descriptions at 30 words, branch descriptions at 20 and results at 100, and requires direct speech to pass a spoken-aloud test credited to Harrison Ford's remark to George Lucas that a line can be typed and still be impossible to say ([Failbetter](https://www.failbettergames.com/news/fallen-london-writer-guidelines-part-iii)). Richard Dansky's technique is to write down ten words a character likes to say and ten the character would never say. Alex Epstein reports an editor finding **about 20% of a few hundred lines** Epstein wrote for one character to be generic, lines any character could have said ([Epstein](https://complicationsensue.blogspot.com/2025/08/danskys-book.html)). Greg Kasavin does the writing for Supergiant's games ([RPG Site](https://www.rpgsite.net/interview/20348-supergiant-games-interview-2026-greg-kasavin-darren-korb-hades-2-ending-music-future-coffee)) and for Hades concentrated on characters who comment on small things the player just did ([ScreenHub](https://www.screenhub.com.au/news-article/features/digital/jini-maxwell/hades-greg-kasavin-breaks-down-supergiants-unique-approach-to-narrative-262459)).

Second-language writing scholarship sits on the same side. The translingual approach treats a writer's other languages as a resource and argues for the writer's say over their own language, though critics question it on teaching grounds ([Sun](https://hammer.purdue.edu/articles/thesis/A_Translingual_Approach_and_Its_Implications_for_L2_Writing/12170121)). A Japanese ELT chapter argues that native-check services and editors tend to enforce standard English and erase the authentic voice of non-native writers ([Kobe City University of Foreign Studies](https://kobe-cufs.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2619/files/nenpo62-03.pdf)). Models make this worse: ChatGPT's grammar corrections were fluent and over-corrected, most of all on short sentences, although the tested models date from the GPT-3.5 era ([Fang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01746v1)). Inference: the author's English is casual and direct, and those are the first features a model "improves". Lithuanian has no articles, so article slips are the likeliest class of real error. That is background knowledge: the two studies of Lithuanian students' English found in this research were seen only as snippets, which did not show which error types dominate ([Nangle et al.](https://ejournals.vdu.lt/index.php/SM/article/view/4892); [Daukšaitė](https://www.lituanistika.lt/content/21003)). Inside a character's speech, a dropped article can be voice.

Studies of ownership point the same way. Across two studies (30 and 96 participants), people did not feel they owned AI-generated text yet still called themselves its authors, and more personal influence on the text raised ownership ([Draxler et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03283v2)). Next-paragraph AI suggestions improved essay quality and speed while moderately lowering ownership and satisfaction ([Dhillon et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11723)). Nineteen professional writers who co-wrote with AI tools placed authenticity in the process. They counted their contribution as content gatekeeping, the choice of what enters the text, and those who began with a clear vision felt best protected; meanwhile 30 avid readers could not tell AI-assisted from solo work ([Hwang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.13032v1)). Inference: since readers could not tell, a reader saying "it reads fine" proves nothing about voice, and the protection has to live in the process, in who chose each line. No study compares "the writer writes, the AI flags" with AI drafting head to head; the case for it rests on the dose-response pattern across these studies and the homogenisation studies above.

## The agent's taste for a better line is the drift itself

The research changes what counts as a safe edit. Typicality bias wins ties, so every change an agent makes because its version "reads better" moves the text one step toward the shared mode, and each step looks harmless on its own. The edits free of that pull are the ones tied to a named error that the author would also call an error. That turns a vague worry about "AI flavour" into a rule anyone can check in a diff: every changed word in the author's text needs a reason other than taste.

The author's English is the most distinctive thing in the project and the most exposed. Inference: casual second-language English sits far from the tuned model's default of dense nouns and participle clauses, and the features Wikipedia lists as signs of human writing (plain "is" and "has", qualifiers like "very" and "perhaps", superlatives such as "was the first", wordy constructions like "in order to") are what a model smooths away first ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). Fixing every slip could make the stories read more like AI. Detectors get this case backwards, because they misread non-native English as AI: a detector is likely to flag the author's honest text, so its score says nothing useful about the author's writing.

The coding agent in this repo is a Claude model, and the sources point to where its text is likely to slip. Claude was reported as the last model still overusing em dashes (in a study this report could not verify), StoryScope found flat escalation of events in Claude stories, an unverified preprint reports the stock pair Elena Vasquez and Marcus Chen, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet was one of the models whose paragraphs professional writers edited about eight times each ([Chakrabarty, Laban and Wu](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509)). Inference: those are the first places to look in any line the agent touched. Because models were poor at spotting their own problems (0.46 precision) and model judges did not track expert taste, the agent cannot certify its own text as human-sounding. The checks below are mechanical where they can be, and the author's ear is the final test.

## Rules for the author's story text

These rules cover every word a reader sees inside a story: narration, dialogue, choices, endings, titles, and the text on story cards in the menu.

The agent must never:

1. Put its own words into shipped story text, or fill a gap with its own line. A missing line gets a visible placeholder, such as `TODO(author): line needed`, and a question to the author. Drafts the author asks for follow rule 6 in the next list.
2. Rewrite for flow, tone, rhythm, clarity or impact, or merge, split or reorder the author's sentences.
3. Add words the author did not write: transitions, adjectives, metaphors, feelings, morals, summary lines or hedges.
4. Swap a word for a "better" synonym, make the English more standard or more formal, or even out differences between lines.
5. Turn Lithuanian or personal names, places, food or references into generic or Western ones.
6. Change the author's punctuation, dashes and quote marks included, unless the author asks.
7. Edit silently. Every change to story text shows the old line and the new line.
8. Run story text through an AI detector or a humanizer, or treat any score as a verdict on it.
9. Give taste as the reason for a change ("reads better", "more natural").

The agent may:

1. Flag possible real errors in a list the author can accept or reject one by one: file and line, the quoted text, the smallest fix, and a one-line reason. Real errors are typos and misspellings, subject-verb agreement, tense that contradicts the scene, an article or preposition slip that blurs the meaning, a false friend, and a name or item that does not exist in the game. Inside dialogue, the agent asks before treating a slip as an error.
2. Apply typo fixes directly only in files where the author has said so, and show the diff.
3. Do the mechanics around the text: copy it into data files byte for byte, wire it into the game, check line lengths on a 390 px wide phone screen, find duplicate or orphaned lines, and check that names and terms match across scenes.
4. Ask questions that help the author write, such as what a character wants in a scene or what the player just did.
5. State a concern about a line once, with the reason, and leave the decision to the author.
6. Write a draft only when the author asks for one. A draft is marked DRAFT, stays out of shipped files, offers several clearly different options, and counts as the agent's text until the author rewrites it.
7. Keep a voice sheet only if the author starts one: Dansky-style lists of words the author uses and never uses, and real sentences the author picks. The agent never fills it with guesses.

## Review checklist before text ships

Run it on every line the agent touched and on any text the author asks the agent to check. Each hit is a question for the author; the agent does not fix hits on its own.

1. Every changed word maps to a named real error. Revert anything without one.
2. An edited line is no longer than the original. Fixes rarely add words.
3. Read aloud, the line sounds like something the author would say to a friend.
4. The line could not appear unchanged in another game, and it uses no word from the author's never-list.
5. The line does not state what the player or a character feels, or what a scene meant, after the scene already showed it.
6. The pattern scan below finds nothing, or the author has approved each hit. One hit is a question; several in one passage are a strong sign.
7. No Lithuanian or personal detail has become generic or Western.
8. The author can point at every line and say "I chose this".

The scan is a shell function that reads text on standard input, so it works on the agent's changed lines or on a whole file. It needs ripgrep (`rg`). Adjust `stories/` to wherever the story files live. In `.ink` files, lines that start with a tilde are ink logic, so ignore tilde hits on those lines.

```sh
# Lines the agent added or changed:  git diff -U0 -- stories/ | rg "^\+[^+]" | scan
# A whole file the author asked for:  scan < stories/some-story/episode-1.ink
scan() {
  t=$(cat)
  # Dashes, curly quotes and the tilde
  printf '%s\n' "$t" | rg -n "[\x{2013}\x{2014}\x{2018}\x{2019}\x{201C}\x{201D}\x7E]"
  # Era vocabulary: a cluster is the tell, a single word is a question
  printf '%s\n' "$t" | rg -niw "delves?|tapestry|testament|pivotal|crucial|vital|underscores?|intricate|showcas\w*|landscape|foster\w*|leverage|multifaceted|vibrant|profound|nestled|boasts|palpable|camaraderie"
  # Contrast frames, empty copulas, participle tails, abstract feelings, body clichés
  printf '%s\n' "$t" | rg -ni "not just|it's not|isn't just|rather than|serves as|stands as|, (highlighting|underscoring|emphasizing|reflecting|symbolizing|ensuring|fostering)|a sense of|the weight of|unspoken|hung in the air|barely above a whisper|heart hammer|flicker|pit of \w+ stomach|breath \w+ didn't know"
  # Stock names and settings
  printf '%s\n' "$t" | rg -niw "elara|elias|mara|kael|vasquez|lighthouse|clockmaker"
}
```

## Plain-writing rules for the agent's own repo text

These rules cover everything the agent writes in its own name: commit messages, code comments, READMEs, docs and reports. The agent's plain engineering voice stays out of story text, and the agent does not imitate the author's story voice in its own text.

Commit messages:

1. The subject is imperative and completes "If applied, this commit will ...", in 50 characters or fewer, capitalised, with no full stop ([Beams](https://cbea.ms/git-commit/)). It names the change in the game's terms, as in "Fix the ending choice that skipped the last scene".
2. A body appears only when the reason is not obvious from the diff: two to four plain sentences on why, wrapped at 72 characters ([Beams](https://cbea.ms/git-commit/); [kernel.org](https://docs.kernel.org/process/submitting-patches.html)).
3. One change per commit, as the repo already requires. No "This commit", no file-by-file bullet list, no emoji, and no hype words such as "comprehensive", "robust", "seamless", "enhance" or "streamline". Keep the repo's existing style of plain sentences without type prefixes, and keep whatever attribution line the repo already uses (the Linux kernel, for comparison, asks for an Assisted-by tag when an AI tool helped).

Code comments:

4. Comments explain why the code is the way it is: the reason for a value, a workaround and what it works around, a link to the source of borrowed code, a TODO with the condition that ends it ([Stack Overflow Blog](https://stackoverflow.blog/2021/12/23/best-practices-for-writing-code-comments/)).
5. Each file opens with one comment on what it is for, as the repo requires. No comment that narrates the next line, no section banners, no docstring that repeats the function name, no commented-out code left behind.

READMEs:

6. The top-level README.md changes only when the author asks. Folder READMEs say in a few lines what the folder holds and how it connects to the rest, so a stranger can find the way.
7. A README the author asks for says what the thing is, how to run it, how the files are laid out and what is unfinished, and it reads fine as plain text ([Write the Docs](https://www.writethedocs.org/guide/writing/beginners-guide-to-docs/)). It has no features list with bold labels, no badges or emoji, no "Why X?" marketing section and no "Happy coding" sign-off. A generated README looks finished before anyone has edited it ([HackerNoon](https://hackernoon.com/treat-ai-generated-readmes-like-first-drafts)), so the agent reads every line of one before committing it.

Docs and reports:

8. Headings use sentence case, and sentences use plain "is" and "has" in place of "serves as" and "boasts". Paragraphs stay short.
9. No exclamation marks, and none of "simply", "easy", "please note" or "let's" ([Google developer documentation style guide](https://developers.google.com/style/tone)).
10. No em or en dashes, no tilde, no "not X but Y" or "it's not X, it's Y" lines, no strings of three for rhythm, no closing summary that repeats the text, and no inflated significance words. The pattern scan above runs on the agent's own docs before they are committed.
11. Uncertainty is stated once, plainly, and every inference is labelled as one.
12. In anything public, the agent calls the author "the author" and uses no pronoun for the author, possessive forms included.
