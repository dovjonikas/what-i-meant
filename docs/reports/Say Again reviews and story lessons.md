# Let the player decode his own son

The notes point to one answer. Critics and players remember a sound or an object that made them act, never a speech: the killer's whistle, a frequency that starts to shake, a diploma that fits nowhere but under the bed, a trigger that moves a dead brother. They forgive a story they can guess and they punish three things: friction that stops carrying news (**no skip was the most counted Steam complaint for Killer Frequency, 33 mentions**), reveals that fall apart on a second pass (Firewatch's "Scooby-Doo" ending), and endings that explain (Stories Untold's fourth episode, Night in the Woods' cult). Deduction games earn their click by letting the player lay the last piece and confirming in bundles, not per item: Obra Dinn locks fates in threes, Golden Idol only says "two or fewer slots are incorrect". Fair twists are written to a rules sheet and seeded in things the audience handled; the strongest recognition beats are two words and a gesture, and the quiet scene before the twist outranks the twist. Father-and-son stories land through acts withheld, with the child setting the terms, and die when a catastrophe hands the father cheap forgiveness. Read against the draft of Say Again, this means: keep the SOS a scared boy in a dark house, keep the father wordless at the table, and put the "oh" on the chart, where the player lays the third line and decodes D-A-D with their own taps. The largest changes are to night one (eleven sticky notes, and the son confirmed three times before the first morning), to the music (a "reveal" cue that plays before the reveal), and to the knob (fourteen turns, half of them procedure). Everything below comes from reviews and designer interviews; the big outlets (Eurogamer, Rock Paper Shotgun, IGN, Polygon, the Guardian) blocked fetches and appear second-hand or not at all, and no study measures hold length or text speed for a story game, so the interface numbers are platform floors, not optima.

## Players remember a sound that forces a hand, never a speech

The radio games agree on what sticks. For Killer Frequency it is the whistle, framed by one reviewer as a decision: "The Whistling Man is approaching as you can hear their whistle, what do you do?" ([Finger Guns](https://fingerguns.net/reviews/2023/06/09/killer-frequency-review-ps5-radio-graves/)). The same reviewer felt the town from one room: "Despite only being in one building, it felt so much like I saw all of Gallows Creek." Steam players' most-counted praise is **voice acting and audio (43 mentions), then puzzles (39), impactful choices (22) and characters (22)** ([Vaporlens](https://vaporlens.app/app/1903620/killer_frequency)). The memory that stays from the 2019 prototype is guilt: "I always felt terrible when one was stabbed due to my poor decisions" ([The Escapist](https://www.escapistmagazine.com/killer-frequency-is-a-free-radio-show-simulator-where-callers-lives-are-on-the-line/)). Firewatch's Delilah is remembered as a person who is never seen: "She's this spectral voice on the radio" who "remains something of a phantom" and "always seems to know the right thing to say" ([A.V. Club](https://www.avclub.com/firewatch-review)). Oxenfree's radio is remembered for the dial itself: scroll "until something comes through - or starts to shake, in the case of the island's more ominous secrets" ([A.V. Club](https://www.avclub.com/oxenfree-tells-a-great-horror-story-by-taking-its-teena-1798186455)), and the studio recorded its shortwave sound through a World War II radio set ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxenfree)). Stories Untold's designer names the deepest version of the trick: "Something as seemingly innocent as a frequency generator can take on a whole new meaning", because "each machine was a way to get closer and closer to the truth" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/crafting-the-deliberately-frustrating-interfaces-of-i-stories-untold-i-)). Iron Lung, which shows no window at all, is "a masterclass" in tension because "the unknown is making noise" ([Pixeldie](https://pixeldie.com/2022/03/12/iron-lung-review)).

The short emotional games say the same thing in a different register. Unpacking's one named moment is a diploma: "It doesn't fit on the bedroom walls. It doesn't fit anywhere in the kitchen." "The only possible place for it? Under the bed." ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/unpacking-might-have-the-worst-video-game-boyfriend-of-1848006376)), and the studio's phrase for its method is "Empathy through detective work" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/gdc2022/gdc-2022-unpacking-a-narrative-through-1-000-household-items)). To the Moon plants paper rabbits, a stuffed platypus and a lighthouse named Anya, and the reveal is the moment "when the events of the future begin to make sense" ([Baik, GDC 2021](https://media.gdcvault.com/GDC+2021/EmilyBaik+GDC+Narrative+Review+2021.pdf)). Spiritfarer's "most affecting mechanic however is also its simplest", the hug ([NME](https://www.nme.com/reviews/spiritfarer-review-a-beautiful-management-game-about-dying-too-mired-in-frustrating-busywork-2732038)). A Short Hike withholds a surgery from the player and pays it off with a phone call at the summit after "the music fades out, with the only noise being the strong winds" ([Copas, GDC 2024](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2024/GNR/Papers/Copas+GNR+-+A+Short+Hike.docx.pdf)). Brothers: A Tale of Two Sons is the reference case critics reach for: "There is only a button press that has come to mean something, and a situation in which that meaning is transformed into a rich and moving sentiment" ([GameCritics](https://gamecritics.com/sparky-clarkson/brothers-a-tale-of-two-sons-second-opinion/)). Before Your Eyes makes the player's own blink the loss: "disappointed after I blinked involuntarily and advanced the story sooner than I would have liked" ([Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/review/before-your-eyes/before-your-eyes-review-an-emotional-eye-opening-experience)).

The psychology behind this was covered in the earlier research and holds here. Memory for performed actions beats memory for watched ones by **g = 1.23 across 145 studies**, with most of the benefit coming from planning the action ([University of Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/memory-attention-cognition-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/enact22.pdf)), and the effect shows most clearly when performed and watched actions are mixed ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01907/full)). Being moved follows Frijda's order: a bond is awakened, the viewer is led to expect it will not be honoured, then it is honoured all at once ([eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4zv550pt)), and powerlessness is "probably the most common emotional trigger" of adult tears ([Gračanin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6402489/)).

Inference, read against the draft: Say Again already has its objects seen twice. The mother's first note says he "says 'over' at the table now"; the kitchen pays it with "You say 'over' at dinner." The second note has the box from the hall; the kitchen has "I broke the antenna. I fixed it with tape", which also pays off the night-four "Check the antenna" choice. The third note has the batteries gone from the drawer; night four opens with "No batteries in the drawer at home either" and the boy sends BAT. The bearing **084°** appears on night one as Cape's "That is not the sea, Dune. That is the town", returns on night three as "Only your mast heard him", and is the first of the three lines on night four. That is the To the Moon structure. The strongest beat in the draft is the one the player performs: after laying Cape's and the pilot's lines and seeing the cocked hat zoom to the town, the player decodes D, A, D at the slowest unit in the game from four choices. Nobody tells them. That is Brothers' trigger and BioShock's pinboard, where the player is allowed to "connect the pieces" seconds before the game states it ([PC Gamer, search excerpt](https://pcgamer.com/great-moments-in-pc-gaming-bioshocks-twist)). The two weak spots are that the draft cues `music: 'reveal'` before the lines cross and before DAD, and that none of the objects seen twice is seen; they are all in text.

## Friction earns its keep only while it carries news, and "no skip" is the top complaint

Stories Untold is the clearest statement of why a clunky instrument is worth it. "In any other game, this would just be bad UI/UX. But here, it is essential." "Each device in the game is method-acting." The machines teach by repetition: "There's a lot of deliberate repetition at play, so that when the time comes, you know roughly what to do already." The designer admits the cost in the same interview: "Some of the machines are clunky the point of being straight up difficult to use" and "It can turn people off." ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/crafting-the-deliberately-frustrating-interfaces-of-i-stories-untold-i-)). Reviewers confirm both halves. The radio episode is the favourite, "genuinely challenging, but not to the point of obscurity" ([Dread Central](https://dreadcentral.com/reviews/221189/stories-untold-video-game)), yet "The Morse code puzzle is a bit inaccessible as the beeps aren't explained" ([TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2020/01/30/stories-untold-review/)), and one player was stuck about 30 minutes "because the computer never understood what I was saying" ([Metacritic users](https://www.metacritic.com/game/stories-untold/user-reviews/?platform=pc)).

The line between friction and chore is whether the action still carries information. Oxenfree's tuning is "our literally only playable mechanic" and gameplay is mostly "tuning your radio to the right frequency in the right place" ([Metacritic users](https://www.metacritic.com/game/oxenfree/user-reviews/?platform=pc)); Radio Commander makes you "regularly ping your units for their position" ([GameGrin](https://www.gamegrin.com/reviews/radio-commander-review)) until "the use of the radio for every action and order" becomes "repetitive and frustrating" ([Cultured Vultures](https://culturedvultures.com/?p=118248)); The Signal State "sometimes requires a full 30 seconds of rotating the mouse wheel to change a dial" ([GameCritics](https://gamecritics.com/damiano-gerli/the-signal-state-review/)). Killer Frequency's Steam complaints are a ranked list of the same thing: **no skip or fast-forward (33 mentions), no sprint (23), downtime and waiting for calls (20)** ([Vaporlens](https://vaporlens.app/app/1903620/killer_frequency)), and the deeper fault is passivity: "I wasn't bored, but I wasn't fully engaged either with the gameplay" ([Finger Guns](https://fingerguns.net/reviews/2023/06/09/killer-frequency-review-ps5-radio-graves/)). Adrian Hon's verdict on its script applies to any radio game: "If the script were half as long, it would've been twice as good." He also names what kept it moving: "If you fail a puzzle, someone dies" and "the game keeps rolling" ([Adrian Hon](https://adrianhon.substack.com/p/killer-frequency)).

Timed talk is the one radio mechanic with a documented failure mode. Oxenfree players complain that "the time limit runs out before the other person is even done talking" ([Metacritic users](https://www.metacritic.com/game/oxenfree/user-reviews/?platform=pc)), and a whole Steam thread turns on the fact that the game never signals whether waiting is allowed, so lines get missed ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/388880/discussions/0/350532795330246701)). Silence as a choice, by contrast, is praised everywhere it appears: Firewatch lets you choose "whether to respond at all" ([Anigamers](https://anigamers.com/reviews/firewatch-gone-anywhere-but-home/)), and in Killer Frequency "sometimes the best choice is to not make one at all" ([Horror Obsessive](https://horrorobsessive.com/2023/06/13/anything-you-say-can-and-will-be-broadcast-in-killer-frequency/)). A.V. Club's reviewer refused to replay for a better outcome: "Henry couldn't redo his conversations, and I shouldn't either" ([A.V. Club](https://www.avclub.com/firewatch-review)).

Waiting works when the game keeps its own rule. Lifeline's real-time gaps were "a fantastic way to simulate the time between Taylor's check-ins" ([TouchArcade](https://toucharcade.com/?p=170487)) and "something I could look forward to between appointments" ([GameCritics](https://gamecritics.com/brad-gallaway/lifeline-review/)), until the closing chapter dumped "back to back walls of text" and "it lost me completely when it abandoned the one thing that makes it unique" ([Kritiqal](https://kritiqal.com/articles/2015/12/24/lifeline-review)). Emily Short found that switching to the fast mode brought "a slackening of my investment in Taylor personally" ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2015/05/23/lifeline-3-minute-games/)). Bury Me, My Love's reviewer describes the dread exactly: "A text can be good but that wait provides you a space to think on all the things that can do wrong" ([Ploughshares](https://pshares.org/blog/the-story-of-a-text-conversation-in-bury-me-my-love/)).

Teaching has the same shape. Lucas Pope "wanted the player to be overwhelmed at first and to have to feel their way through the interface and ruleset" ([localization.it](https://localization.it/papers-please-what-the-game-told-us/)). Outer Wilds cut its meteor opening because "players weren't familiar with the village yet" and you cannot make someone curious about a change to something they do not know ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/live-die-repeat-how-i-outer-wilds-i-piques-curiosity-in-an-ambivalent-solar-system)). On the other side, Killer Frequency's "tutorial itself can feel a little long winded" ([Dread Central](https://dreadcentral.com/reviews/492570/killer-frequency-review-long-time-listener-first-time-killer)) and Not for Broadcast's "can become slightly overwhelming" ([Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/not-broadcast-game-review/)). Short runtimes are praised and stretching is punished: Killer Frequency "does not outstay its welcome" at about four hours ([Metacritic critics](https://www.metacritic.com/game/killer-frequency/critic-reviews/?platform=pc)), Iron Lung "stops before you start feeling safe in your metal womb" ([Destructoid](https://www.destructoid.com/reviews/review-iron-lung-pc-horror-game/)), and Stories Untold's rule was "get a feel across, riff on it a little bit, and then move on to the next story" ([Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2017/03/04/the-developers-of-stories-untold-on-alien-isolation-stranger-things-and-how-they-made-their-game-in-six-months)).

Inference, read against the draft: Say Again's knob is turned **fourteen times** across the script, six of them on night two (16 to announce the fog warning, 67 to read it, 16 to wait, 67 for the boy, 16 for the pan-pan, 67 for the boy again). The turns that carry news are the ones the fiction explains: the finder listens on 16, the boy lives on 67. The announce-then-read round trip is procedure, and it happens twice (fog, gale). Stories Untold's rule says repetition is fine when it builds a reflex for night four; Killer Frequency's counts say it is the first thing players name as dead time. The draft's "say again" replay, where letting go of the button early makes the other side ask for the message again, is Papers, Please's "easy to go back and replay the day" in the game's own vocabulary and is the best thing in the interface. Night one carries **eleven sticky notes** (mayday, over and out, DF, one line, figures, clicks, Morse, flare, fix, HI HI, channel 16) against two on night three; that is the long-winded tutorial reviewers flag, and the FIGURES, CHANNEL 16 and HI HI notes can wait for the moment they are used. The one wait that should be long is the one the draft keeps short: night four's "Nothing on 67" at the boy's time runs 3.2 then 3.6 seconds. That is Frijda's second step, the bond expected to fail, and Nielsen's 10-second attention limit says a wait longer than that needs visible feedback ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/)); the wall clock moving from 02:40 to 03:02 is that feedback.

## Deduction clicks when the player lays the last line, and the phone must show what to touch

Every deduction game the notes cover solved the same problem the same way: never confirm one fact at a time. Obra Dinn validates fates "only in sets of three" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_of_the_Obra_Dinn)); "It's the rule of three: as soon as three deaths have been accurately logged, the game pulls me out of the action", which "prevents most brute-force verification" though it still invites "a reasonable amount of blindly fishing for answers" ([Film Stories](https://filmstories.co.uk/?p=83249)). Kotaku's praise is the feeling the rule produces: "the agony and joy of scrutinizing tiny details and making connections between them" and "There is enough information to figure everything out without guessing" ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/return-of-the-obra-dinn-the-kotaku-review-1829797772)). Golden Idol's designer went through the whole arc: early testers "had no idea which of their deductions were correct", the team "could not validate each phrase slot", judging the whole puzzle "frustrated the players", and the fix was the indicator "two or fewer slots are incorrect", which "served the same purpose of rewarding the player for being close". His principle: "Given the opportunity, players will optimize the fun out of a game" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/case-of-the-golden-idol)). Her Story gives no verdict at all; the joy is "making a connection, and then typing it in to see an array of new videos" ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/games/her_story/b/pc/archive/2015/06/26/her-story-review-game-informer)), and Barlow kept it that way because "If there are loads of cool things to do outside of typing in words, then it's going to diminish that" ([Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2015/07/03/inside-her-story-the-indie-mystery-game-that-people-can-t-stop-analyzing.aspx)). Roottrees locks confirmed entries and removes them from the pool, "rewarded with a short fade to black" ([PC Gamer via Yahoo](https://tech.yahoo.com/general/articles/roottrees-dead-review-180121009.html)). The named faults are near-synonyms ("Some potential causes of death are so similar that it can take a little bit of guessing", [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/return-of-the-obra-dinn-the-kotaku-review-1829797772)), late items that can only be eliminated, and repeated input: dragging first and last names separately "doubles the amount of mouse work needed" ([RPGFan](https://www.rpgfan.com/review/the-case-of-the-golden-idol/)). The cheapest way to mark the next step is to withhold: Obra Dinn unblurs a face only when its identity can be deduced ([patientgamers mirror, search excerpt](https://lr.in.psf.lt/r/patientgamers/comments/1e4oqw1/spoilers_return_of_the_obra_dinn_is_a_great_game/ldizoe7/?context=3)), and Outer Wilds' ship log keeps "the marker within if there's a remaining clue" ([DualShockers](https://www.dualshockers.com/outer-wilds-emergent-gameplay-design-opinion/)).

Phone-first craft has floors but no optima. A working visual novel developer polled her players and most chose "fast-but-not-instant" text; she disables tap-to-reveal at important moments to "catch the player off guard" ([In Stars and Time devlog](https://armor-games-studios.itch.io/in-stars-and-time/devlog/386975/devlog-3-musings-about-text-speed)). Ren'Py's documented example sets **40 characters per second** ([Ren'Py](https://www.renpy.org/doc/html/preferences.html)); Say Again's received text runs at 22. The interactive fiction community is near unanimous: "The only way timed text can work is if the player has direct, immediate control over the display rate" (Plotkin), players hate "press twice every time. Once to show all text, and once more to continue", and they want "a scroll back button" ([intfiction.org](https://intfiction.org/t/timed-aesthetics/66900)). Hold-to-talk is the rare case that passes Plotkin's test, because the words go out while the thumb is down. Platform long-press thresholds are **400 ms on Android** ([Android source](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/main/core/java/android/view/ViewConfiguration.java)) and **0.5 s on iOS** ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/documentation/uikit/uilongpressgesturerecognizer/minimumpressduration.json)), and Android ships an accessibility delay because people "touch and hold by accident when you meant to tap" ([Google](https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/6006989?hl=en-GB)). Targets: **1 cm square** as a physical minimum ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/touch-target-size/)), **48 dp with 8 dp gaps** ([Google](https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/7101858)), **44 CSS px** at WCAG AAA, which names frequent controls and "steps in a sequence" as the cases that matter most ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-enhanced.html)). Text: 17 pt default and 11 pt minimum on iOS, with light weights avoided ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography.json)); **4.5:1** contrast for body text and 3:1 for large ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)). Signifiers: "what people need, and what design must provide, are signifiers" ([Norman](https://jnd.org/signifiers-not-affordances/)); flat styling causes "click uncertainty" and the fix is "Flat 2.0", mostly flat with subtle shadows and highlights ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/flat-design/)); "People treat clicks like currency and they don't spend it frivolously" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/clickable-elements/)). Florence's rule is that the interactive thing is the thing that moves: "There's intentionally not much animation in the game" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/designing-i-florence-i-to-convey-the-ineffable-feeling-of-being-in-love)), and Simogo's rule for anything read at speed is "very simple shapes and distinctive colors because it's just swooshing by" ([Apple Developer](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=33kvkagk)). Device 6 kept its puzzles to "button presses between two options" and still scored 92 ([Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/games/device_6/b/ios/archive/2013/10/29/the-next-step-in-interactive-fiction)).

Inference, read against the draft: the chart is already an Obra Dinn bundle. A fix is confirmed when two lines exist and the tap lands; the night-one buoy tap is a visible right-or-wrong, not Hon's "not at all clear what criteria they're judged on". The Morse decoder is the exception. It asks the player to read the first one to three letters from four choices each, marks a wrong letter at once, and after two wrong letters highlights the right one. That is per-item confirmation, the thing both designers rejected, and a 1-in-4 guess per letter. For a two-letter word it barely matters; for DAD it means the reveal can be fumbled into. The fix is small: keep the four choices, make them sound-distinct (D against B and K is a near-synonym pair of the kind Kotaku names), and let the word light up as a whole instead of marking letters. The PTT needs a signifier because holds have none by nature: a tap on it should produce a click of static and the label, not nothing. The kitchen's "Hold to reach for his hand" needs a tap alternative, because guidance says a primary input "must not be required" and gives a held trigger as the example of what excludes players ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/107)). The knob, the four letter buttons and the chart's crossing are the controls WCAG names as most at risk: small, repeated, and steps in a sequence.

## A twist survives the second pass, and the quiet scene outranks the crossing lines

The fairness test is the same in every source: after the reveal, the audience replays the story and finds that nothing lied. The Sixth Sense's DVD extra exists "to explain the constraints they worked under", the "subtler touches" are "the flashes of red that mark certain scenes or the streaks of white in some characters' hair", the film has "lots and lots of silence", and the reviewer still finds the one hole: "why doesn't Bruce Willis cause the air to go cold the way the other dead characters do?" ([Salon](https://www.salon.com/ent/movies/dvd/review/2000/06/13/sixth_sense/)). That hole is what critics go looking for. McKee's rule, via Emily Short, is that a surprise is earned when "The unexpected consequence didn't rely on coincidences or accidents" and when "The fact that it was unexpected illustrated something about the character and her problem" ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2019/02/05/story-robert-mckee/)). The named cheats: Spec Ops forces the act and then blames the player ("How can I feel guilty about making a choice that's not even there to be made?", [GameCritics](https://gamecritics.com/guest-critic/spec-ops-the-line-second-opinion/)); Firewatch's reveal "makes so little sense" and reads like "an episode of Scooby-Doo" ([Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/games/firewatch/b/playstation4/archive/2016/02/08/firewatch-review-gabbin-in-the-woods.aspx)), is "dropped into your lap like a bag of bricks" ([Shacknews](https://www.shacknews.com/article/93116/firewatch-review-a-sizzling-tale-of-love-loss-and-loneliness)), and in Short's reading the fault is the midgame "pretending... that this game is about something it's not", with "the devs sort of gaslighting the players" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/reading-the-end-of-firewatch)). Gone Home's misdirection was genre, not lies, and it worked both ways: "I crept up the attic stairs as slowly as possible, frightened of what I was going to find" ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2014/04/23/moments-approaching-the-attic-in-gone-home.aspx)) against "Nothing great happened" ([Jeffrey Somers](https://www.jeffreysomers.com/blather/?p=3315)). Her Story gates its answer structurally, so "the most important words act as gates, preventing players from finding critical clips too early" ([PC Gamer, search excerpt](https://pcgamer.com/the-story-behind-her-story)).

Being ahead of the character is a payoff, not a cost, when the gap turns into dread. The interactive fiction forum warns that "it's really hard for the game-designer to talk directly to the player, bypassing the character", and the working examples use a hero who is "a bit thick" ([intfiction.org](https://intfiction.org/t/dramatic-irony-in-interactive-fiction/43872)). Telegraphing is forgiven when the scene is strong: a To the Moon player saw it coming ("Eva's odd behavior the next scene just telegraphed it even louder") and "the scene was still horrible and beautiful" ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/206440/discussions/0/522730701044532901)); Florence was guessable "but it didn't matter" ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/monument-valley-studios-new-game-shows-what-digital-com-1823041384)). Over-explaining is not forgiven. Stories Untold's fourth episode ties things up and "you lose a lot of the mystery" ([TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2020/01/30/stories-untold-review/)); Night in the Woods' plot ending is "sort of... boring" while the game "excels when it doesn't try to explain anything" ([AV Club](https://www.avclub.com/night-in-the-woods-explores-the-unsolvable-mysteri)).

The recognition beat that critics rank highest is small and physical. In The Sixth Sense it is the car scene, where Cole tells his mother what her dead mother answered: "Every day", a scene one critic calls "the more affecting of the two" against the twist that follows ([Industrial Scripts, search excerpt](https://industrialscripts.com/sixth-sense/); [Looper, search excerpt](https://looper.com/1146269/the-one-sixth-sense-quote-that-still-has-fans-fighting-back-tears)). In Brothers the dead brother's trigger is pressed by the player: "as the cutscene ends he stands still waiting for the player's input" and "In no other game have I experienced a truer breaking of the fourth wall" ([Cane and Rinse](https://caneandrinse.com/sound-of-silence/)). In God of War it is "the hovering of a hand, inches away from the shoulder it wishes to grasp" ([Captain Fez, search excerpt](https://captainfez.com/2018/05/24/god-of-war-2018/)). In the Apollo 13 record, the real line was "we've had a problem", Swigert called first, and Mission Control asked him to repeat it ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houston,_we_have_a_problem)); his tone was "more confused than panicked" ([Irish Times, search excerpt](https://www.irishtimes.com/news/science/houston-we-have-a-problem-the-myths-of-apollo-13-1.4222011)). The warning case is Bao: a wordless twist that "literally caused audiences to gasp" for one reviewer ([The Film Magazine, search excerpt](https://www.thefilmagazine.com/bao-oscar-nominated-2019-animated-short-film-review/)) while on Douban "the majority found it weird and 'didn't get it'" ([The World of Chinese, search excerpt](https://www.theworldofchinese.com/2018/06/bao-llyhoo-for-bao/)). Endings that rest are praised: To the Moon "does a fantastic job of *not* beating you with the sappy stick" ([Complex](https://www.complex.com/pop-culture/2011/12/review-to-the-moon-is-a-brilliant-touching-story-of-one-mans-death-wish)); Before Your Eyes gives "a satisfying ending, if not a happy one" ([FingerGuns](https://fingerguns.net/reviews/2021/04/06/before-your-eyes-review-blink-back-tears/)); A Short Hike ends on wind and a bicker about whether the mother should have said. Memory of an experience follows its peak and its end, **r = 0.58 across 174 effect sizes** ([OUCI](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4NG6JZvl)), and putting a feeling into words damps it ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576282/)).

Inference, read against the draft: Say Again passes the McKee test, because the surprise says something about the operator: he hears every voice on the lagoon except the one in his own house. The rules sheet exists in pieces (the boy hears 16 and 67, sees the lagoon and the mast from a high window, has Grandpa's key and the drawer batteries, cannot press harder) and should be written down and each night audited against it, the way Shyamalan audited the ghosts. On a second pass the lines hold: "A boy. About your age." is true and bitter; "No. Over." is a lie to a stranger that becomes a lie to a son, and the kitchen already answers it ("I know. I came back anyway."). The problem is the opposite of Firewatch's. Night one confirms the son three times before the first morning: IM 8, GRANDPA, and a note that says "he says 'over' at the table now. Your fault." Her Story's gating argues for suspicion early and certainty late; Gone Home shows that dread is the dividend of being ahead. The three notes should be a ladder, one new piece each, with the mother's "over" line and the batteries on the last of them. The operator's blindness already has its reason in the log lines about his father ("My father climbed it in half the time", "The kind of night he used to switch the lamp off for"); he is avoidant, not dim, and the box from the hall is a grief he will not open. One stated wrong theory per night ("Seagull, with a broken microphone?" is already there on night one) keeps the gap honest. The reveal itself should be played flat, like Swigert: the `music: 'reveal'` cue that fires before the cocked hat is a spoiler by music, and the earlier research found spoiled beats less moving ([ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/12/151210101816.htm)). Let the three lines cross in storm ambience, let the player decode DAD, then hold a dead carrier on 16 before the drive. At the table, "Say again. Over." held on the old radio is the Apollo line and the Brothers input in one, and "Every night." (the boy's answer to "You were scared last night") is the car scene. Those two beats and the hand are the ending; the picks that explain the mechanics of the week ("Why only clicks?") are the Stories Untold fourth episode and should be few, optional and short.

## Fathers land through acts withheld, and the son sets the terms

The sharpest rule in the notes is Nino Cipri's: "Atonement takes effort." Bad-dad arcs "inevitably end with the fathers' self-sacrifice"; "A catastrophe presents itself as the ultimate Get Out of Accountability Free opportunity"; "They die, because death is the only way we imagine fatherly failures being forgiven." The good version is A Wrinkle in Time, where "The story, the journey, the resolution, and the decision to forgive him belonged to Meg" and the father "admits that he's failed, goes home, and lives with his mistakes"; the bad one is A Quiet Place, where the father "waits to tell her he loves her until approximately seven seconds before he sacrifices himself" ([Uncanny Magazine](https://uncannymagazine.com/article/the-bad-dad-redemption-arc-needs-to-die/)). God of War is praised for restraint in the same breath as tenderness: "it's these terse moments that make the tender moments sing" ([The Ringer, search excerpt](https://www.theringer.com/2018/5/2/17309510/god-of-war-kratos-review-gameplay)), and "the tenderness he cannot voice is given a lot of time" ([Captain Fez, search excerpt](https://captainfez.com/2018/05/24/god-of-war-2018/)). Brothers ends "with father and son grieving at the graves of mother and brother" in a language of gibberish and gesture ([Cane and Rinse](https://caneandrinse.com/sound-of-silence/)). Night in the Woods' parents land through routine: "Despite their domestic mundanity, these are among the game's most memorable scenes", conversations "hewn with guilt and disappointment" that "gradually thaw" ([Thumbsticks](https://www.thumbsticks.com/night-in-the-woods-nintendo-switch-review/)). That Dragon, Cancer's strongest scene is a parent who cannot fix it: "a father with a distressed son he could not help" ([Destructoid](https://www.destructoid.com/reviews/review-that-dragon-cancer/)), "Our task is to sit there for a while and feel its invisible weight" ([Kill Screen](https://killscreen.com/the-impossibility-of-sadness-in-that-dragon-cancer)); it loses critics where it turns "broad and impersonal" ([A.V. Club, search excerpt](https://www.avclub.com/that-dragon-cancer-asks-players-to-share-the-burden-of-1798186343)). The earlier research adds the mechanism: fathers' affection toward sons travels through supportive acts more than words ([Floyd](https://kory-floyd-dev.squarespace.com/s/affectionate-communication-between-fathers-and-young-adult-sons.pdf)), and the strongest sadness clip ever tested in a lab is a boy weeping over his dead father ([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/the-saddest-movie-in-the-world-33826787/)).

Radio craft converges on four tools and a cast of six. The BBC's audio drama tips: "Most listeners of radio drama switch off in the first 3 minutes"; keep the cast to six voices or fewer and make each distinct; "Use natural dialogue. Pause. Silence. Subtext"; "If no one changes then there's probably no story" ([BBC Writersroom via mirror](https://bbcbreakingnews.pages.dev/blogs/writersroom/entries/5063d080-bc08-483d-a4c0-69528c84d4e2)). "You have only the four tools to work with:" dialogue, sound, music, silence, and the cardinal sin is the "look-see" line in which a character "carefully describes all visual incidents to a companion" ([Great Northern Audio](https://greatnorthernaudio.com/writing.html)). A practice-led thesis on silence in radio drama describes it "turning up the volume of the radio play" ([White Rose eTheses, search excerpt](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/23524)). The Morse device has one engine: misreading. In Johnny Got His Gun the staff take an SOS tapped with the head for a seizure until a nurse spells MERRY CHRISTMAS on his chest and "Joe signaled that he understood" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_Got_His_Gun)); in Interstellar a daughter reads STAY as a ghost ([Looper](https://www.looper.com/848848/the-one-scene-in-interstellar-that-made-no-sense-at-all)). No game was found that pairs a Morse-only child with a radio-operator parent; the small itch.io Morse games use the device for claustrophobia. The ground is open.

Inference, read against the draft: Say Again passes Cipri's test as written. The SOS is a tree down, no light, mother at work; nobody is hurt; Cape says so ("Nobody is in danger on that line. A scared kid in a dark house."); the father's act is to leave the desk against the rules, which is effort, not heroics, and the boy sets the terms afterwards ("Roger."). That must not be improved into a real emergency. The boy's two lines on night three ("MY DAD WORKS NIGHTS", "I NEVER SEE HIM") are the only speech in the game; the father never gets one, and the kitchen keeps him to "Say again. Over." and a hand. "DO U HAVE KIDS" is the MERRY CHRISTMAS beat, the first time the two sides prove they understand each other, and it needs its own silence before the answer. The operator's wrong theory each night is the Johnny Got His Gun seizure. What the draft lacks is the boy's fist as a signature: his unit varies from 90 to 150 with no habit, and Perry's "write in sound" argues for one quirk heard every night (a long gap before every word, one letter he always gets wrong) so that the operator can say on night three "that fist sounds old, like the old men", a line that reads differently after the box. The father is someone before he is a father, which Edith Finch's hollow ending shows is required ([Indie Game Culture](https://indiegameculture.com/indie-culture/edith-finch-was-just-okay)): the cat on the logbook, the lamp he turns off for the stars, and the father who "did not say much at home. On the radio he did" are that someone, and should stay.

## Conclusion

The draft's engine is a three-night routine that breaks on the fourth, and the research says the break is where the heart is: "Nothing on 67" at the boy's hour is the bond expected to fail, and the SOS on 16 is it honoured, in the only language the father answers. Everything the sources praise is already in the script in outline. What they would change is where the "oh" lives and how loud it is. The chart is the "oh": the player, not the operator, lays the third line, sees the hat close on the town, and taps D, A, D. The table is the heart, and it should be quieter than the draft has it: one held line, one two-word answer, the hand. The pattern that recurs in every failed example, from Firewatch to Night in the Woods to That Dragon, Cancer, is a story that tells the audience what it just understood.

The open questions are honest ones. No source gives a maximum comfortable hold or a right text speed; the hold cap of four seconds and the 22 characters per second are defensible and untested. No study compares a reveal performed by the player with one watched. The only cited method for finding where a mechanic fails to teach is Color Gray's: sit with "mouths shut" and watch a stranger get stuck ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/road-to-igf-2023/-the-case-of-the-golden-idol-i-used-frequent-testing-to-improve-its-mystery-solving)). Five silent playtests of night one, with the notes thinned and the first mother's note softened, would settle more than any further reading.

## What Say Again should do

### Story

1. Write the boy's rules sheet (hears 16 and 67, sees the lagoon and the mast from the top window, has Grandpa's key, took the drawer batteries, cannot press harder, will not speak) and audit every line of all four nights against it on a second pass [Salon on The Sixth Sense's "Rules and Clues" and its one "cold air" hole].
2. Make the mother's three notes a ladder of one new piece each: night one only "He was up in the night again", night two the box from the hall, night three "over at the table" plus the batteries and the hospital [Her Story's gating: suspicion early, certainty late; Gone Home's attic dread as the dividend of being ahead].
3. Move IM 8 off night one or keep only GRANDPA there, so the first morning raises a question and does not answer it [Her Story gates; To the Moon's forgiven telegraphing applies to the scene, not to the first night].
4. Give the operator one stated wrong theory per night, each further from the truth (a skipper with a dead mic, a kid on a yacht, a radio hobbyist, "that fist sounds old") [Johnny Got His Gun and Interstellar: the Morse device runs on misreading].
5. Keep the operator's blindness grounded in grief, with one log line per night about his own father, and never let it read as stupidity [intfiction thread on the dim hero; Short on Firewatch's midgame "pretending"].
6. Keep the midgame rescues real and self-contained (Seagull, Tern) and never fake one [Short: never pretend the game is about something it is not].
7. Keep the SOS a scared boy in a dark house with nobody hurt, keep Cape's "Nobody is in danger on that line", and keep the father's act as leaving the desk against the rules [Cipri: "Atonement takes effort"; the catastrophe absolution trap].
8. Give "DO U HAVE KIDS" a silence before the answer buttons appear, and keep both answers, since "No" becomes a lie to a son that the kitchen already answers [Johnny Got His Gun's MERRY CHRISTMAS beat; McKee via Short: the surprise must say something about the character].
9. Keep the boy's two lines on night three as the only speech in the game and give the father none, at the table or after [God of War's "boy"; Floyd: fathers' affection as acts; Cipri: the decision belongs to the child].
10. Cut the kitchen to three beats: the held "Say again. Over.", one or two of the boy's two-word answers with "Every night." kept, then the hand; make the explaining picks ("Why only clicks?") optional and no longer than one line each [The Sixth Sense car scene ranked above the twist; Stories Untold's and Night in the Woods' explained endings].
11. Keep the night-one "Over" or "Out" choice, the call sign and the "told" flag returning in the kitchen, and add no hidden scoring [Fendt: agency comes from a later reference to an early choice; Oxenfree's "fundamentally wrong" hidden consequences].
12. Keep the epilogue radio check and the player keying 88 as the descent after the peak, with no line that names what either of them learned [A Short Hike's glide down; peak-end; affect labelling damps feeling].
13. Make night four's "Nothing on 67" the longest silence in the game, with the wall clock visibly moving from 02:40 to 03:02 and no music [Frijda's order: the bond expected to fail; Nielsen's 10-second limit needs visible feedback].
14. Keep the HI HI laugh, the "Five by five" ribbing and the cat as breathers between rescues, and let humour sit one beat before each turn [Killer Frequency's humour as the breather; Spiritfarer and To the Moon marked down for tone breaks, not jokes].

### Interface

15. Cut night one from eleven sticky notes to the six the first two calls need (mayday, DF, one line, clicks, Morse, fix) and move FIGURES, CHANNEL 16 and HI HI to the moment each is first used [Killer Frequency's "long winded" tutorial; Not for Broadcast's "overwhelming" one; Pope's "overwhelmed at first" means by the task, not by notes].
16. Cut the knob turns from fourteen toward eight: keep every turn the fiction explains (the finder on 16, the boy on 67) and collapse the announce-on-16-then-read-on-67 round trip to one turn on night two and the 16 key on night four [Oxenfree's "grow tired of using the radio"; Stories Untold's repetition rule holds only while the turn carries news].
17. Keep the "say again" replay when the button is released early; it is the safety net that lets the game skip a tutorial [Papers, Please: "easy to go back and replay the day"].
18. Confirm Morse by the word, not the letter: keep the four choices per letter but stop marking a wrong letter at once; light the word when all its letters are placed, and give the "close" hint only after the whole word fails [Golden Idol's "two or fewer" indicator; Obra Dinn's threes; Klavins: per-item confirmation invites brute force].
19. Make the four letter choices sound-distinct (never D beside B or K, never S beside H) and for DAD make them unmistakable, so the reveal cannot be fumbled into [Kotaku on Obra Dinn's near-synonym causes of death].
20. Keep DAD as the one decode where the player reads all three letters at the slowest unit, and never auto-translate it [Brothers' trigger pressed by the player; BioShock's pinboard before the Ryan scene; enactment g = 1.23].
21. Keep the chart's crossing tap inert until two lines exist, and keep the night-one buoy tap as a visible right-or-wrong with no hidden criteria [Obra Dinn's withhold-until-deducible; Hon on Killer Frequency's unclear judging].
22. Give the PTT a signifier: a tap on it produces a click of static and the label "hold to talk", recognition starts within 400 to 500 ms, and an early release costs nothing but a "say again" [Norman's signifiers; NN/g click uncertainty; Android 400 ms and iOS 0.5 s thresholds].
23. Keep the hold cap at four seconds, and keep hold-to-talk as the one place text is timed, since the thumb controls the rate [Plotkin: timed text only under the player's direct control].
24. One tap completes a received line, the same tap on a completed line advances, never two taps; keep a scroll-back of the night's log [intfiction thread: "press twice" and "scroll back"].
25. Add a tap alternative to the kitchen hand hold and to the old radio's button, with progress kept on release [Xbox Accessibility Guideline 107: a primary input must not be required].
26. Size the knob, the four letter buttons, the lamp and the chart's fix at 44 to 48 px with 8 px gaps on the 390 px column [WCAG 2.5.5 names frequent controls and steps in a sequence; Android 48 dp plus 8 dp].
27. Keep received text at 22 characters per second and add a setting with an instant endpoint, plus an instant-text fast mode after the first ending for replays [In Stars and Time's "fast-but-not-instant" poll; Lifeline's Fast Mode; Killer Frequency's 33 "no skip" mentions].
28. Make the one thing to do next the only thing that moves or the only unlocked control, and never an arrow [Florence: the interactive thing is the thing that moves; NN/g: arrows are the least favourable cue].
29. Allow silence as an answer at one beat per night (CAN U FIND ME on night two is the natural one): not holding the button is a valid reply the boy reacts to [Firewatch and Killer Frequency praised for letting the player not answer; Oxenfree punished for forcing it].
30. Give idle time a job: traffic on other channels through the 16 watch receiver, weather on 67, the log to initial, instead of empty waits [Killer Frequency's "downtime and waiting for calls" (20 mentions); Firewatch's idle time reads as atmosphere because it shows something].

### Visuals and sound

31. Cut the `music: 'reveal'` cue before the chart on night four; let the three lines cross in storm ambience, let the player decode DAD, then hold a dead carrier on 16 for several seconds before the drive [Apollo 13's flat register; spoiled beats less moving; Marshall-Griffiths on silence turning up the volume].
32. Keep `music: 'off'` before the hand, and let the hand go out and stay out with nothing after it but the boy's one word [A Short Hike's music fading to wind; God of War's hovering hand; Brothers' silence].
33. Put the house on the chart as a named landmark from night one, and keep the 084° line faintly on the chart on nights two and three so the cocked hat closes on something the player has looked at for three nights [Bao's wordless twist read as random by many; Iron Lung: the player's own marks are the only picture].
34. Add a child's drawing of the mast with its red light, pinned in the home scene from night one and visible on the kitchen table at the end, so the camera speaks to the player past the operator [intfiction: speaking to the player past the character must be done visually; To the Moon's objects seen twice].
35. Give the boy's fist a signature heard every night: one habit (a long gap before each word, one letter always sent wrong) and a slightly uneven unit, so the ear knows him before the chart does [BBC Writersroom: write in sound, each voice distinct; Oxenfree's WWII radio recording as sound characterisation].
36. Show Morse as sound first and a slow decode second, never as instant subtitles; keep the tap-to-read-all only for words the player is not asked to read [TheSixthAxis on unexplained beeps; the decode is the player doing the listening the father did not].
37. Make the DF needle swinging while a carrier is held the diegetic cue that prompts the chart, the way the whistle prompts a choice [Finger Guns on the whistle as a decision prompt; A.V. Club on the dial that "starts to shake"].
38. Keep the night-three lamp blink as a gesture the boy sees from across the water, and never let the operator describe the lagoon aloud [Wells' "look-see" rule; Firewatch praised for radio talk that "sounded natural"].
39. Keep the cast to six voices or fewer and give each caller a distinct sound signature (Cape dry, Seagull hoarse, Tern clipped, Rescue One flat) [BBC Writersroom: six voices, each distinct in age and accent].
40. Draw bearing lines and the fix at 3:1 or better against the sea, distinguish the three lines of the cocked hat by more than colour, and check the chart in greyscale [WCAG contrast floors; NN/g: colour alone is unsafe; Golden Idol's nodes "hard to distinguish"].
41. Give the knob, the key and the PTT a Flat 2.0 treatment (a bevel, a shadow, a highlight on the live control) since they are drawn controls with no native signifier [NN/g on flat design's click uncertainty].
42. Keep text at 17 px or larger in regular or heavier weights on the phone column, with 4.5:1 contrast, and set the radio's screen text large enough to read while a thumb covers the PTT [Apple HIG typography; WCAG 1.4.3; Simogo: simple shapes and distinctive colours for anything read at speed].
43. Run five silent playtests of night one with strangers after changes 2, 15 and 18, and watch where they stall before reading anything else [Color Gray's "mouths shut" testing; the notes found no other cited method].
