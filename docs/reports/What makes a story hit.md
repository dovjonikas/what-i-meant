# One late reply answers a whole life

Psychology points to one mechanism under the moments in this story most likely to land: the player is held where nothing can be done, and then love arrives as an act. Powerlessness is probably the most common trigger of adult tears, and positive tears go with closeness and moral acts. Being moved follows a set order: a bond is awakened, the player is led to expect it will fail, and then it is honoured all at once. The porch frame (an old parent alone, staring at a "Read" with no reply) is that held state, and the kid's "home." is that act. Players stay inside it while the story stays picturable and plausible, and they fall out when something makes them watch themselves reading. It feels like their own life when the detail is concrete in the senses and vague about identity, and when it rests on the events nearly everyone expects to live through: a child, a partner, leaving home, parents dying. Choices and touches add force when they are few and come back later, and doing an action is remembered far better than watching it, provided most of the story is watched. Read against the current draft, five changes follow most directly: move the longest silence of the final wait to after "Read" (it now sits after "Delivered"), bring the porch back after the parents' deaths (the longest stretch without it holds both), show Dad's and Mom's last messages on the phone as patterns the player can see, let the player type the few loaded words with their own thumb, and cut the narrator lines that name a feeling or state a moral. Many of the findings were read only in abstracts, and no study tests a short relational story like this one. Nobody has measured whether a moving story makes people text their parents afterwards either, so every change below comes with a playtest.

## Players stay inside the story until something makes them watch themselves

The largest review of narrative transportation, the state of being carried into a story, pooled **132 effect sizes from 76 articles** ([City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/)), with a pooled sample one summary puts above 21,000 people. On the story side, transportation rose with characters the reader can identify, a plot that calls up rich mental images, and events that seem possible and plausible. On the reader's side it rose with attention and with familiarity with the territory ([OnFiction](https://www.onfiction.ca/2014/02/research-bulletin-summarizing-over.html), a secondary summary without effect sizes). Green's study of 152 readers found the same pull from experience: readers with personal knowledge of a story's themes were more transported, and transportation went with perceived realism ([UNB bibliography](https://narrativestudies.lib.unb.ca/bibcite/reference/17215), abstract only).

What breaks it is self-awareness. In Kaufman and Libby's experience-taking studies, readers who were reminded of their own identity could not take on a character's experience, and most students reading in a cubicle with a mirror could not get into the story at all ([ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/05/120507131948.htm)). The same work found an order effect: readers who identified with a character first and learned of a difference later (the character's race or sexual orientation) kept the identification ([Ohio State News](https://news.osu.edu/losing-yourself-in-a-fictional-character-can-affect-your-real-life/)). Both results come from press coverage; the papers were not read.

Grammatical person matters less than people assume, and tense may matter more. First-person stories produced more immersion than third-person versions, mainly in transportation and mental imagery, and sentence-level studies place "you" in an actor's view and "he" or "she" in an observer's ([PLOS ONE, Hartung et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4883771)). The one direct comparison of first and second person found no difference in identification, while **present tense beat past tense** ([Diegesis](https://www.diegesis.uni-wuppertal.de/index.php/diegesis/article/download/214/294?inline=1), seen through a search summary). Resembling the hero helps only a little (**g = 0.19 across 39 studies**), and shared feelings count for more than shared age or background ([Current Psychology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-023-04842-4)). In a test with science comics, cartoonish characters narrowed the identification gap between Black and white readers, and plainer backgrounds raised identification for everyone ([metatoc](https://metatoc.com/papers/180897-visual-realism-and-character-identification-in-science-comics-an-empirical-study-testing-mccloud-s-masking-effect-theory)). The draft's faceless people already follow that.

Inference: the porch opening does what the research asks. An old person watching tail lights go down a hill is a picture anyone can hold, and it asks nothing of the player's biography. The risks sit later, in lines that turn the player into a reader looking at the story from outside. The draft has a few: the hint "tap who you want close" and the narrator's "So you get to choose a bit" speak to the player as a player, and general statements such as "You start to sound like the people you spend your time with" ask the player to agree with a claim instead of living a moment. The life lines view is a bird's-eye, observer's device, and in the draft it carries most of the deaths: Grandpa's, the dog's, Dad's and Mom's are all told while the years are running, and Grandma's line ends there before her scene. The lines view is the story's clock and worth keeping. The inference is that it should carry time passing, while the heavy beats land in scenes and on the porch, where the player is an actor.

The draft tells memories in past tense and the porch in present tense, which marks the frame clearly. Macrae's single result favours present tense. Inference: that is too thin a basis to rewrite the whole story, but it is cheap to test on two memory scenes.

## Shared life events and early smells make a stranger's life feel like the player's own

Readers' own memories come back early and from description. In Seilman and Larsen's study, a short story and an expository text called up about the same number of personal memories, but the story produced **about twice as many memories in which the reader had been the one acting**. The memories clustered at the start of the texts, and descriptive passages produced many while action and dialogue produced almost none ([Miall, University of Alberta](https://sites.ualberta.ca/%7Edmiall/LiteraryReading/Readings/Seilman_Larsen_1989.htm)). Smell reaches furthest back. Odour-cued memories are older, more emotional, more vivid and rarer than memories cued by words or pictures, and they cluster in the **first decade of life**, while word and picture cues peak between 11 and 20 ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00312/full)). Whether a written smell word works like a real odour is unknown.

Inference: be specific in the senses and general about identity. The draft's line about Grandma's house smelling of dill and floor polish is the model: a reader who never had that grandmother still had a house that smelled of something. The other childhood scenes carry jokes and family facts (both parents in the first-word joke, Dad and a puppy in the next) and no smell. The order effect above suggests a mismatch costs less once the player has already stepped in, and the porch and "You don't remember any of this" do that stepping in before any family fact appears.

The events that travel are the ones people expect to live. In a Dutch study of 595 people listing the events of a typical life, the most named was **having children (72.1%, expected at about 29)**, then starting school, marriage, and **parents' death (43.5%, expected at about 50, and among the three most negative events)**. Positive events made up 73.3% of what people named, and 55.4% of them were expected between 16 and 30. Positive events have tight expected ages and negative ones are spread across life ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3972131)). Memory shows a bump in those years for positive events only ([Springer, Berntsen and Rubin](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758%2FBF03193303.pdf)). Turkish data show the same shape with some local items added (same PMC source).

Nostalgia follows a recognisable recipe. In seven studies, nostalgic memories put the self at the centre with close others or at big events. They held more positive than negative feeling and often ran as redemption sequences, in which a bad scene is later made good. Negative mood and loneliness triggered nostalgia, and nostalgia then raised social connectedness and self-regard ([Southampton ePrints](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/40445)). Students in **18 countries (1,704 people)** shared the same core idea of what nostalgia is ([Utrecht University](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/pancultural-nostalgia-prototypical-conceptions-across-cultures/)).

Inference: the porch frame, an old parent alone at night, is a textbook nostalgia trigger, and each return to it should release a memory. The draft returns after Morning, in the middle of Evening and after Dawn. Noon has no return, and none comes in Afternoon, which runs from the kid's question about the moon through both parents' deaths to the last chapter. The final "home." redeems the teenage "ok" left unanswered, which is the shape nostalgic memories take, so the echo between the two phone scenes should be exact in layout. The table ranks the draft's moments by how likely they are to reach a stranger; the ranking is inference from the findings above.

Inference, on plausibility: a sixteen-year-old who can leave a parent on read puts "you" among people born around 2000 or later. The old-age present then sits in the 2070s, and the parents who struggle with capital letters and big fonts would have used smartphones for most of their adult lives. Few players will do that arithmetic, but plausibility is a transportation factor, so a hidden birth year with every device checked against it is cheap insurance.

## Tears come where nothing can be done and love shows up as an act

A review of the science of emotional tears names powerlessness as "probably the most common emotional trigger" of adult crying ([Gračanin et al., PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6402489/)). Bereavement, breakups, moves and separations are the prototypical triggers, with loneliness and rejection among the strongest. Moral or sentimental tears come from stories of self-sacrifice and lasting love, and Vingerhoets calls such tears exclamation marks that flag what matters. Older adults cry more and more at positive, meaning-giving moments. People predict that big losses make them cry, but their most recent real crying more often involved conflict, criticism, small failures or rejection (same review). Tears during positive events went with feeling moved, goosebumps and chest warmth, and appraisals of **closeness and moral acts** explained them ([Seibt et al. 2017](https://biomed.reviewer.ly/app/publication/051d490e-e7e9-4d7d-9941-6c703e2c9aa6), abstract only). In the best-known lab test of sad film scenes (78 scenes, about 500 people), the clip that produced the most sadness was a boy weeping over his dead father in *The Champ* ([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/the-saddest-movie-in-the-world-33826787/), press coverage only).

Research on being moved, called kama muta, fits the same pattern. Across **19 countries, 15 languages and 3,542 people** it held up as a distinct, mostly positive emotion set off by a sudden intensification of a close bond, felt as moist eyes, chills, a warm chest and a lump in the throat ([ISCTE repository](https://repositorio-iul.iscte.pt/handle/10071/16718)). Its strong form goes with activity in the smiling muscle ([ISCTE](https://ciencia.iscte-iul.pt/publications/tears-of-joy-aesthetic-chills-and-heartwarming-feelings-physiological-correlates-of-kama-muta/73544)), and the review behind it cites Frijda's order: an attachment is awakened, the viewer is led to expect it will not be honoured, then it is honoured abruptly ([eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4zv550pt)). Labelling something as moving does nothing by itself: in four preregistered studies with 1,049 people, every effect ran through how moved people actually felt ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1112910)). A realistic benchmark is Journey, where 3 of 25 testers cried at the end ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journey_%282012_video_game%29)).

Inference: each death in the draft already comes paired with an act of care, which is what turns grief into being moved. Dad holds the dog at the end, Grandma's jar goes home with you, Dad sends "proud of you." and Mom signs her last text. Grandpa is the exception; his death has no act beside it, which is defensible as the death "you didn't really get yet" if it is a choice.

Fathers are the place where acts carry the most. In a study of 55 father and young adult son pairs, affection travelled more through supportive acts than through words, and a follow-up with 170 men supported the same prediction ([Floyd](https://kory-floyd-dev.squarespace.com/s/affectionate-communication-between-fathers-and-young-adult-sons.pdf), seen in a summary). Grief research names the missing word as its own wound. One scale of unfinished business has two factors, unfulfilled wishes (affirmations never said, missed chances) and unresolved conflict, and together with meaning-making it explained **50 to 60% of the variance in prolonged grief** ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7473452)). In 224 bereaved adults, what mattered was the distress about unresolved issues, whatever their type ([MSKCC](https://synapse.mskcc.org/synapse/works/76720), abstract only).

Inference: Dad's silence is the draft's best-built thread. The bike, "text me when you get there", the bag on and off the bus and holding the dog are four acts, and "proud of you." works because it breaks that long pattern; any extra verbal affection from him earlier would weaken it. His last chat in the draft shows one "home" and one "ok.". Several dated pairs scrolling up would let the player see years of the adult "you" texting home, the habit the teenager refused, and the one message from him that never got an answer, without a word of narration.

Poignancy needs a known ending. When people imagined a meaningful place for the third time as their **last** visit, sadness and mixed feelings rose while happiness stayed above the midpoint, and it worked the same for people aged about 20 and about 77. On graduation day, 110 seniors told it was their last day as students reported the same happiness as controls with more sadness and more mixed feeling ([PMC, Ersner-Hershfield et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2807633)). A chocolate labelled "the last one" was liked more ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/what-kind-of-chocolate-is-best-the-last-you-taste-says-a-new-study.html)), and people asked to live a month as if it were their last in a city gained well-being through feeling more connected to others ([eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2gh2s77q/qt2gh2s77q.pdf), seen in a snippet). People at every age expect to change little in the coming decade ([Harvard](https://psychology.fas.harvard.edu/publications/end-history-illusion)), though the finding has been criticised for relying on remembered selves ([BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/end-history-illusion-illusion)).

Inference: in the draft, every last time is discovered afterward. All three of them, Grandma's last visit, Dad's last chat and Mom's weather text, turn out to have been the last only once the person is gone. That produces regret and the unsaid, which hurt, but poignancy as the studies define it needs one moment the player knows is the last while it is still a happy moment. The old dog is the natural place, because the draft already sets up the gate as a daily ritual, the kind of repetition the experiment ran before its "last" trial. Pet-loss research adds that adults absent at a pet's euthanasia report more regret ([Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/all-dogs-go-to-heaven/201910/should-you-be-present-for-the-euthanasia-of-your-pet), a summary), so a known last walk is also the gentler version. The end of history illusion is why the role swaps in the draft work: players do not expect to become the one who waits, and the story shows it happening.

Two things weaken tears. Putting a feeling into words damps it: choosing an emotion label for an image reduced the amygdala response compared with other ways of encoding it ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576282/)), and a later review describes affect labelling as regulation that does not feel like regulation ([Emotion Review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1754073917742706)). These studies are about perceivers labelling their own feelings, so applying them to a narrator is inference. Spoilers are the second risk, with mixed evidence: in one study of 819 readers spoilers did not reduce enjoyment ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/08/110810093735.htm)), while in a study of 412 students spoiled stories were less moving and less immersive ([ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/12/151210101816.htm)). Inference: knowing the shape of the story helps, and knowing a beat before it lands costs. In the draft, Grandma's line ends in the lines view at 29.5 with no words, then the snow scene and the grief music arrive, and only then does the text say she died. On the other side, leftover arousal from one scene amplifies the next emotion ([Encyclopedia.com](https://encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/arousal-processes-and-media-effects)), and the draft already places jokes one beat before turns: Mom's thumb photos and Dad's capital letters come right before Mom's visit and the deaths.

## Few owned choices and a handful of physical acts carry the weight

Players feel agency when the story acknowledges their choice, and they feel it changed the story only when it comes back later. In Fendt and colleagues' experiment, a branching story beat a story with minimal feedback on every agency item. Against a linear story that acknowledged each choice at once, it differed on only one item, "the story would have been different" (**p = 0.030**), and the only feature the acknowledged version lacked was a later reference to early choices ([Fendt et al.](https://www.cs.uky.edu/%7Esgware/reading/papers/fendt2012achieving.pdf), full paper read). Choices rated moral, social and consequential raised appreciation ([researchr, Iten et al.](https://researchr.org/publication/ItenSO18), abstract only). Interactivity alone did not make players more prosocial afterward; appreciation of the story did ([Aalto, Steinemann et al.](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/interactive-narratives-affecting-social-change-a-closer-look-at-t/), snippet). Failbetter's advice from long practice is that five two-option choices beat ten options picked five times, that not every choice should be a big deal, and that a story should not tell players what their character feels ([Failbetter Games](https://www.failbettergames.com/narrative-snippets-agency-and-choices)).

Guilt has conditions. Of 75 players in Fallout 3, most treated non-player characters as if the exchanges were real, and antisocial choices raised guilt without lowering enjoyment ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23017118/)). Guilt depends on the act being unjustified and on the player's empathy ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.666518), citing Hartmann and colleagues). People also feel less agency over bad outcomes than good ones ([UCL Discovery](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1425000)), and critics of Spec Ops: The Line argue that forcing an act and then blaming the player breeds resentment ([The Artifice](https://the-artifice.com/white-phosphorus-in-spec-ops/), commentary only). Most players pick the kind option: in The Walking Dead's second episode, **87% waited instead of shooting and 80% spared a character** ([GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/telltale-games-the-walking-dead-statistics-trailer/), studio figures).

Inference: the draft has six real choices (the teen reply, goose, who you keep close, the weekend, writing to the best friend, the last reply) plus one-option replies that work as send buttons. That is the right number, and each real choice already returns later, which is the feature Fendt's result singles out. The weak one is the teen choice. Both options mean not texting home, so a player who would have texted is told they did not, and the porch repeats it before the final wait. By the guilt findings, an act the player did not choose produces disowning more than guilt. Two directions fit the research: an honest reply that does text home (the kitchen light is still on and Mom still could not sleep, which keeps the scene), or a typed "home" the player can send or let pass, so not texting becomes the player's own omission. The weekend choice is sound as it stands: the lake's cost is a missed chance, the kind of unfinished business that hurts in grief research, and no disaster follows from the pick.

Doing an action is remembered much better than watching it. A meta-analysis of **145 studies found an effect of g = 1.23** for memory of performed over non-performed actions, with most of the benefit coming from planning the action ([University of Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/memory-attention-cognition-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/enact22.pdf), abstract). The effect shows in free recall mainly when performed and watched actions are mixed in one list ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01907/full)). The developers whose input is the emotional peak tie one verb to the scene's meaning. Florence's maker says the feeling "has to come from the interactions" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/designing-i-florence-i-to-convey-the-ineffable-feeling-of-being-in-love)), Before Your Eyes makes the player hold on to moments until a blink ends them ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gone-before-you-blink-the-webcam-driven-gameplay-of-before-your-eyes)), and Spiritfarer's director said a game about care was close to impossible without physical affection, so the hug became central ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/inside-the-thoughtful-design-of-thunder-lotus-i-spiritfarer-i-)). Effort raises value only when it is finished: the IKEA effect vanished when builds were left incomplete or destroyed ([Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/12136084)). Accessibility guidance says long holds should have an alternative ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/107)). In 121 players' accounts of moving game moments, a lack of agency was itself one of the triggers ([Aalto, Bopp et al.](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/negative-emotion-positive-experience-emotionally-moving-moments-i/)), and Mass Effect 2 players skipped 15% of conversations overall and far fewer near the climax ([GameBanshee](https://www.gamebanshee.com/news/99618-mass-effect-2-statistics-interview.html)).

Inference: in the draft the player sends the opening message and holds twice (to pedal, to walk home); every other text types itself. The mixed-list result says a few performed moments among watched ones will stand out, and the planning result says the decision to start or stop counts most. The city night is the strongest candidate. Today the phone types "home" by itself and the send button stays dead; if the player types "home" with their own taps and then finds no one to send it to, Frijda's second step (the bond expected to fail) is something the player does. "hey" to the best friend is the second candidate, with backspace allowed, since deleting is the real choice there. The final wait is the right place for the opposite: no input does anything, and the player can only watch the status change. The holds already keep progress when the finger lifts; a tap alternative would meet the accessibility guidance.

## The final wait should peak after Read and end on one word

Messaging research gives the wait its shape. Among 213 students, negative feelings arrived **sooner when a message was marked Read than when it was unread** ([IRMA](https://irma-international.org/article/reply-speed-as-nonverbal-cue-in-text-messaging-with-a-read-receipt-display-function/239530/), abstract). The feeling of uncertainty alone, with the information held constant, made unpleasant events more unpleasant and pleasant ones more pleasant ([Harvard, Bar-Anan et al.](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/danielgilbert/files/gilbert_feelinguncertaintyintensifies.pdf)). Suspense needs a liked person, a threat and high but incomplete certainty ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01913/full), quoting Zillmann), and uncertain, unexplained waits feel longer ([David Maister](https://davidmaister.com/articles/5/52/index.html)). In a lab chat with 65 young adults, skin conductance rose from the typing phase into the reply, mainly when the reply was delayed, and the warmth of the reply strongly changed how pleasant the exchange felt ([Frontiers in Psychology, Camerini et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.840845/full)). Typing indicators act as uncertain signs of presence that create suspense ([PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/HERSWM)). The warning: during a reply delay, people rated the other person as less involved and liked them less, with or without a typing indicator ([CAS catalogue, Hwang et al.](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........184df5ea3f3b54c14f9242f69e90701a), abstract only).

Inference: the three states form a rising curve. "Delivered" says the message arrived, "Read" says they saw it and have not answered, and the dots say an answer is coming with unknown content, so the longest silence belongs after "Read". The draft holds 3.2 seconds after "Delivered", then shows "Read" and starts the dots at once, so its longest silence is in the least anxious state. The wait also has to stay short enough that worry about the kid does not turn into doubt about the page.

The research on parents and phones explains why "home." is the right last word. In Pew's 2010 survey, **98% of parents** who gave a teen a phone named staying in touch as a major reason, and 94% of parents and 93% of teens said the phone made them feel safer ([Pew Research](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2010/04/20/teens-and-mobile-phones/)). Among 703 Swedish fourteen-year-olds, what parents knew about their children's whereabouts came mainly from the children choosing to tell, and that disclosure was the factor most tied to lower delinquency ([Zendy](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/1467-8624.00210), abstract). In 196 parent and teen pairs, calls the teen started went with closeness, while calls felt as overmonitoring went with conflict ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/06/110629171102.htm)). Inference: the kid's "home." is a disclosure, the act the teenage "you" never made, which makes it a moral act and more than relief. That matters for what players do afterward. People who saw musicians thank their mentors spent about twice as long helping on a tedious task, and elevation predicted the help where amusement and happiness did not ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/02/100203121600.htm)). Meaningful films raised felt connection to family, though no study measured whether anyone then called ([Greater Good](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_positive_media_can_make_us_better_people)).

Endings decide the memory. People judged film clips by their most intense moment and their final one, with length barely counting ([University of Florida, Fredrickson and Kahneman](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/fredr-kahneman-jpsp1993.pdf)), and a meta-analysis put the peak-end correlation at **r = 0.58 across 174 effect sizes** ([OUCI](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4NG6JZvl)). People chose to repeat a longer cold-water trial that ended slightly better ([University of Zurich](https://ius.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:5ae9adc9-61ec-4174-b37c-4b752f36c23b/Kahnemann%20et%20al.%20-%20When%20More%20Pain%20is%20Preferred%20to%20Less%20%281993%29.pdf)), and a life ending at a high point was rated better than the same life with extra mildly pleasant years ([Scilit](https://scilit.com/publications/a9d0541822a5d3517ea84a7c627ec097)). A dramatic story reduced reactance by lowering perceived persuasive intent ([CORE, Moyer-Gusé and Nabi](https://core.ac.uk/download/357574034.pdf)), and mood after crying at a film dips and then rises above baseline within 90 minutes ([Gračanin et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6402489/)). Inference: a closing instruction to call one's parents would undo the first effect, and the second means the ending need not cheer anyone up. The draft's ending fits all of this. "home." is the peak; the reply choice and "you sound like grandpa" are a joke after it that lowers arousal gently and completes the role swap with Dad's "ok."; the light goes off and "You sleep fine." is the better end added after the peak.

Care for grieving players points the same way. A meta-analysis of 12 studies found that content warnings raised anxiety before the content without reducing distress or engagement afterward ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/2023-october-content-warnings-distress.html)). Bereaved adults found meaning in loss-themed games, but sudden or violent deaths pushed them away, and they needed control over pace ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10426378/)). The story's start screen already offers the matching design: an opt-in "What is in it" that names the losses, and a second tap that says the kid gets home. Inference: the main risk for a grieving player is a final wait that reads as the child not making it, and that note answers it for anyone who asks.

## Seventeen changes, ordered by expected effect

The order is an inference: it puts first the changes that touch the peak and the frame, where the research is most consistent. Every timing is a starting value to tune in playtests. Each change describes structure; any wording is for the author to write.

## Conclusion

Inference, from all of the above: the draft's emotional engine is a held state of helplessness, and the porch frame is what holds it. That makes the gap in the frame across Afternoon the largest structural weakness: both parents die in the observer's view of the life lines, and after the return at 21:44 the player does not sit on the porch again until the final wait. The lines view works as a clock, and the porch is where the feeling builds; the research on nostalgia and on helplessness both say the losses should flow into it. The same reading changes what interactivity is for. The moments where input does nothing (a dead send on the city night, a status that will not change) may hit harder than any choice, provided the player has a few owned acts elsewhere to compare them with.

The open question is the outcome earlier research named as this story's hope: players reaching out to someone afterward. Elevation predicts helping in the lab and meaningful films raise felt closeness to family, but no study found checks whether people contact a parent after a story like this one. A one-question follow-up a day after a playtest would measure that directly, and with a few dozen players it would already say more about this story than the literature can. Inference: the sensible order of work is changes 1 and 2 first, since they touch the peak and the frame, and then a small playtest that asks the questions in the last column before the rest are built.
